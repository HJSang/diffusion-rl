Learning diffusion RL through DiffusionART

Five steps that take you from "I know GRPO for language models" to reading every equation in DiffusionART: Efficient, Stable and Scalable Reinforcement of Diffusion Models and running its code.

This guide is written for someone who already trains language models with RL. You know what an advantage is, why PPO clips a ratio, and how a rollout engine feeds a trainer. Most of that carries over to image and video generators. What changes is the generator itself, which builds a sample by integrating a velocity field instead of emitting tokens, and that one change reshapes which RL tricks are available. The guide spends its time on exactly those differences.

Each step is a chapter with the motivation, the mechanism, the math you need (derived, not just stated), a main code block, a worked example with numbers, pitfalls and exercises with solutions. Toy code runs on a laptop CPU in seconds. Interactive figures let you move the knobs that the paper turns.

What the paper claims

  • NFT-style objectives contain a hidden forward–reverse consistency force that pushes velocities in a direction unrelated to reward. Training the positive and negative branches jointly cancels it only partly.
  • EMA rollout policies stabilize NFT through a restoration force toward the lagged model, not because the rollouts are off-policy. The EMA schedule must be tuned per task.
  • DiffusionART removes the consistency force by construction and replaces EMA with a second, trust-region-masked update. It needs no differentiable reward, works with few samples per prompt, and supports pairwise reward models.
  • On HunyuanVideo-13B with equally weighted visual and motion quality rewards, it reaches the late-stage mean reward of DiffusionNFT and Flow-GRPO-Fast with 12.02× and 11.72× fewer GPU hours, and ends with 28.8% and 53.0% higher mean training reward.

Step 1 · about 1 day

The generation side: flow matching, ODE/SDE sampling and CFG

After this step you can read every symbol in the paper's §2 Preliminaries: you will know what a velocity field is, why the regression target $u_t=x_0-\epsilon$ trains the ideal velocity $\mathbb E[u_t\mid x_t]$, how ODE and SDE samplers turn that field into images, why only the SDE gives you a log-prob, and what classifier-free guidance does. You will also know which sign convention each piece of code uses.

1. Why generation needs a path from noise to data

An LLM generates by factorizing the joint distribution over tokens with the chain rule, $p(y\mid c)=\prod_k p(y_k\mid y_{<k},c)$, and sampling one token at a time. Each factor is a categorical distribution over a finite vocabulary, so the network outputs logits, sampling is a softmax draw, and the log-probability of a whole response is a sum of per-token log-probs. Everything in PPO and GRPO (ratios, KL, clipping) is built on that sum.

An image does not have a natural left-to-right order, and a pixel or latent value is continuous. A $512\times512$ RGB image is a point in roughly $786{,}000$ dimensions; SD3.5's latent for the same image is a $16\times64\times64$ tensor, still $65{,}536$ continuous numbers. You cannot write down a normalized density over that space with a softmax. What you can do is start from a distribution you can sample trivially, a standard Gaussian $\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$, and learn a transport: a rule that moves Gaussian samples, a little at a time, until their distribution matches the data distribution. The sequence of intermediate distributions is the path, and the rule that moves a sample along it is a vector field.

Breaking the transport into many small moves is what makes it learnable. A single network that maps noise to an image in one shot would have to solve a hard, highly multimodal regression. A network that only has to answer "at this partially noisy point, which way is the data?" solves a much easier, local problem, and we can supervise it with simple regression on pairs (clean sample, noise) that we generate ourselves.

Diffusion and flow matching in one page

Two families of models implement this idea. They differ mainly in what the network predicts.

  • Diffusion models (DDPM, score-based models) define a forward process that gradually adds Gaussian noise, $x_t=\alpha_t x_0+\sigma_t\epsilon$, with a variance-preserving schedule. The network predicts the noise $\epsilon$ (equivalently the score $\nabla_x\log p_t(x)$, the gradient of the log-density of the noisy marginal). Sampling inverts the noising by a stochastic or deterministic reverse process that uses the score.
  • Flow matching (Lipman et al., 2022) and rectified flow (Liu et al., 2022) choose the straight interpolation $\alpha_t=1-t$, $\sigma_t=t$ and train the network to predict a velocity, the time derivative of the path. Sampling integrates an ordinary differential equation (ODE) along that velocity.

At a fixed noisy point $x_t$ and time $t$, the four quantities $\mathbb E[x_0\mid x_t]$, $\mathbb E[\epsilon\mid x_t]$, the score, and the velocity are affine functions of each other (you will derive the conversions in §2). So the families are not different models of the world; they are different parameterizations of the same object, with different numerical behavior. SD3/SD3.5, FLUX, HunyuanVideo and Wan, the models in the paper, are all trained as rectified flows that predict velocity. The paper, like most of the field, still says "diffusion" and "diffusion RL" as an umbrella term; whenever it writes equations, it means the rectified-flow parameterization defined next (the paper, §2).

From LLM RL

Think of the velocity network as the policy and of one sampling step as one action. The analogy to decoding is real but imperfect: sampling steps are like decode steps, but every step rewrites the whole sample. A token, once emitted, is fixed; a diffusion step updates all 65,536 latent coordinates at once, and the "response" is only the final state $x_0$. Typical step counts are small (10 for SD3.5-M rollouts in the paper, 4 to 16 for video), so trajectories are short but each action is enormous.

2. Rectified flow and conditional flow matching

The interpolation and the per-sample target

Use the paper's notation (§2). Draw a clean sample (an endpoint) $x_0\sim p_{\text{data}}$ and independent noise $\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$. For $t\in[0,1]$ define the forward (noising) state

\[ x_t=\alpha_t x_0+\sigma_t\epsilon,\qquad \alpha_t=1-t,\ \ \sigma_t=t,\qquad\text{so}\quad x_t=(1-t)\,x_0+t\,\epsilon. \]

At $t=0$ the state is the clean sample; at $t=1$ it is pure noise. For a fixed pair $(x_0,\epsilon)$ the path $t\mapsto x_t$ is a straight line segment. Generation will run $t$ from $1$ down to $0$, so the paper defines the velocity in the denoising direction, which is minus the time derivative:

\[ u_t=-\dot\alpha_t\,x_0-\dot\sigma_t\,\epsilon=-(-1)\,x_0-(1)\,\epsilon=x_0-\epsilon . \]

$u_t$ is the per-sample (conditional) velocity target. For rectified flow it does not depend on $t$: every point on the segment has the same velocity, pointing from the noise end toward the data end. If you stand at $x_t$ and take a step of size $\Delta$ toward smaller $t$, you land exactly on $x_{t-\Delta}=x_t+\Delta\,u_t$.

From per-sample targets to the ideal velocity field

At sampling time you do not know $x_0$ or $\epsilon$; you only see the current state $x_t$. Many different pairs $(x_0,\epsilon)$ pass through the same $x_t$, and they disagree about the direction. The best single answer, in squared error, is their average. Define the marginal or ideal velocity field

\[ v^*(x,t)=\mathbb E\big[u_t\mid x_t=x\big]=\mathbb E\big[x_0-\epsilon\mid x_t=x\big], \]

where the expectation is over the joint distribution of $(x_0,\epsilon)$ conditioned on landing at $x$ at time $t$. Flow matching trains a network $v_\theta(x_t,t,c)$ by regressing the per-sample target:

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{FM}}(\theta)=\mathbb E_{t\sim\mathcal U[0,1],\,x_0\sim p_{\text{data}},\,\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)}\Big[\big\lVert v_\theta(x_t,t,c)-(x_0-\epsilon)\big\rVert_2^2\Big]. \]

Why the minimizer is the conditional expectation. Fix $t$ and write $v=v_\theta(x_t,t)$, $v^*=v^*(x_t,t)$. Add and subtract $v^*$:

\[ \mathbb E\lVert v-u_t\rVert^2=\mathbb E\lVert v-v^*\rVert^2+2\,\mathbb E\big[\langle v-v^*,\,v^*-u_t\rangle\big]+\mathbb E\lVert v^*-u_t\rVert^2 . \]

The middle term vanishes by the tower property: condition on $x_t$; then $v-v^*$ is a fixed vector and $\mathbb E[v^*-u_t\mid x_t]=v^*-v^*=0$. So

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{FM}}(\theta)=\underbrace{\mathbb E\lVert v_\theta(x_t,t)-v^*(x_t,t)\rVert^2}_{\text{what training reduces}}+\underbrace{\mathbb E\,\operatorname{tr}\operatorname{Cov}\!\big[u_t\mid x_t\big]}_{\text{irreducible, independent of }\theta}. \]

Minimizing the loss over all functions gives $v_\theta=v^*$ almost everywhere. This is the central trick of flow matching: we never compute $v^*$, we only regress noisy per-sample targets, and the expectation does the averaging for us. It is exactly the same argument that makes a cross-entropy-trained LM output $p(y_k\mid y_{<k})$ even though each training example shows only one next token.

From LLM RL

The loss does not go to zero. The second term is the analogue of the entropy floor of next-token cross-entropy: it measures how uncertain the direction is, not how bad the model is. A flow-matching loss curve therefore tells you little about sample quality, just as LM pretraining loss tells you little about RL reward.

The ideal velocity is induced by an endpoint distribution

Look at what $v^*$ depends on: the distribution of endpoints $x_0$ (and the fixed noise distribution). Change the endpoint distribution and you change the ideal field. The paper makes this explicit in §3.1. For an endpoint distribution $P_b=p_b(x_0\mid c)$ it forms the joint forward distribution $Q_b$ of $(x_0,\epsilon,x_t,u_t)$ by drawing $x_0\sim P_b$, $\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$ and noising, and defines, with $s_t=(x_t,t,c)$,

\[ v^b_{\text{fwd}}(s_t):=\mathbb E_{Q_b}\big[u_t\mid s_t\big]. \]

This is our $v^*$ with the data distribution replaced by $P_b$. The paper calls it the ideal forward velocity induced by $P_b$. Three facts from this step will carry the analysis in Steps 3 and 4:

  1. Reweighting the endpoints (for example by a reward, $p_+\propto r\,p_{\text{old}}$) changes $v_{\text{fwd}}$ in a computable way. In the Gaussian-mixture example below, reweighting simply changes the mixture weights in the closed form.
  2. The field a sampler actually uses, $v_{\text{rvs}}^{\text{old}}:=v_{\text{old}}$ (a trained network), is not guaranteed to equal the ideal forward field of the endpoints it produces. The network is imperfect and the sampler discretizes. The paper names this gap $e=v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}}-v^{\text{old}}_{\text{rvs}}$ and shows that it leaks into forward-process RL objectives (the paper, §3.1, the proposition on the forward–reverse consistency force).
  3. Under rectified flow $u_t=x_0-\epsilon$ with $\epsilon$ independent of $x_0$, so any covariance between a reward and $u_t$ equals the covariance between the reward and $x_0$ at every noise level. DiffusionART's guidance direction is built on this (the paper, §4.1).

Useful conversions

Because $x_t=(1-t)x_0+t\epsilon$ is linear, conditioning both sides on $x_t$ gives $(1-t)\,\mathbb E[x_0\mid x_t]+t\,\mathbb E[\epsilon\mid x_t]=x_t$, and by definition $\mathbb E[x_0\mid x_t]-\mathbb E[\epsilon\mid x_t]=v^*$. Solving the two linear equations:

\[ \hat x_0(x_t):=\mathbb E[x_0\mid x_t]=x_t+t\,v^*,\qquad \hat\epsilon(x_t):=\mathbb E[\epsilon\mid x_t]=x_t-(1-t)\,v^*. \]

The score of the noisy marginal follows from Tweedie's formula. Since $p(x_t\mid x_0)=\mathcal N\big((1-t)x_0,\,t^2I\big)$, $\nabla\log p_t(x)=\mathbb E\big[-(x-(1-t)x_0)/t^2\mid x_t=x\big]=-\mathbb E[\epsilon\mid x_t=x]/t$, so

\[ \nabla_x\log p_t(x)=-\frac{x-(1-t)\,v^*(x,t)}{t},\qquad v^*(x,t)=\frac{x+t\,\nabla_x\log p_t(x)}{1-t}. \]

These are the "affine conversions" promised in §1. The repo uses the first one directly: the "endpoint" estimate $x_t-t\,v$ appears in src/visual_rl/sampling.py and src/visual_rl/training/nft_sampling.py, with the sign flipped because the code uses the SD3 convention (§6).

Worked example: a single Gaussian endpoint

Let $x_0\sim\mathcal N(m,s^2)$ in one dimension. Then $(x_0,x_t)$ is jointly Gaussian with $\operatorname{Var}(x_t)=V_t:=(1-t)^2s^2+t^2$, $\operatorname{Cov}(x_0,x_t)=(1-t)s^2$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(\epsilon,x_t)=t$. Gaussian conditioning gives $\mathbb E[x_0\mid x_t]=m+\tfrac{(1-t)s^2}{V_t}(x_t-(1-t)m)$ and $\mathbb E[\epsilon\mid x_t]=\tfrac{t}{V_t}(x_t-(1-t)m)$. Subtract:

\[ v^*(x,t)=m+\frac{(1-t)s^2-t}{(1-t)^2s^2+t^2}\,\big(x-(1-t)m\big). \]

Numbers: $m=2$, $s=0.5$, $t=0.5$, $x=1.5$. Then $V_t=0.25\cdot0.25+0.25=0.3125$, the slope is $(0.125-0.5)/0.3125=-1.2$, and $x-(1-t)m=0.5$, so $v^*=2-0.6=1.4$. A Monte Carlo estimate (average $x_0-\epsilon$ over 38,187 samples whose $x_t$ fell within $0.01$ of $1.5$) gives $1.397$. Check the endpoints: at $t=1$ the slope is $-1$ and $v^*=m-x$, so one Euler step of size $1$ lands exactly on $m$; at $t=0$ the slope is $s^2/s^2=1$ and $v^*=x$, which is $x_0-\mathbb E[\epsilon\mid x_0]=x_0$.

3. The closed-form ideal velocity for a Gaussian mixture

Real data is multimodal, so the single Gaussian is too simple. A mixture of Gaussians is the richest case that stays analytic, and it is the endpoint distribution used by the toy code and the widget in this step. Let

\[ p(x_0)=\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\text{mix}}}\pi_k\,\mathcal N(x_0;\,m_k,\,s^2I). \]

Condition on the component $k$ first. Inside component $k$ everything is the single-Gaussian case, with $x_t\mid k\sim\mathcal N\big((1-t)m_k,\,V_tI\big)$ and $V_t=(1-t)^2s^2+t^2$. The posterior probability of component $k$ given the noisy point is a softmax:

\[ w_k(x,t)=\frac{\pi_k\,\mathcal N\big(x;(1-t)m_k,V_tI\big)}{\sum_j\pi_j\,\mathcal N\big(x;(1-t)m_j,V_tI\big)},\qquad v^*(x,t)=\sum_k w_k(x,t)\Big[m_k+\frac{(1-t)s^2-t}{V_t}\big(x-(1-t)m_k\big)\Big]. \]

Read it as attention over modes. Near $t=1$ every $V_t\approx1$ and the $(1-t)m_k$ are close to $0$, so the weights stay near the priors $\pi_k$ and the field points at the overall mean. As $t$ decreases, the components separate in $x_t$-space, the softmax sharpens, and each particle commits to one mode. The commitment happens early on the noisy end, which is why the paper's trust-region weight multiplies by $t$ to emphasize high-noise states (the paper, §4.3) and why the mode a sample ends in is mostly decided in the first few steps.

Two properties make this formula useful later. First, reweighting endpoints by a function that is constant within each component only changes the $\pi_k$, so you can compute the positive, negative and tilted forward fields of Step 3 exactly. Second, setting $\pi$ to a one-hot vector gives a conditional field for that component, which is how the widget and the CFG example produce $v(x\mid c)$ without training anything. The implementation is ten lines (ideal_velocity in the toy below); it computes the softmax in log space for numerical stability and drops the Gaussian normalizer, which is the same for all components because they share $s$.

4. Sampling as an ODE

Given a velocity field, generation solves the ODE $\tfrac{dx_t}{d(-t)}=v(x_t,t,c)$ from $x_1=\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$ to $t=0$. The simplest solver is Euler on a decreasing grid $1=t_0>t_1>\dots>t_N=0$. With step size $\Delta_n=t_n-t_{n+1}>0$:

\[ x_{t_{n+1}}=x_{t_n}+\Delta_n\,v_\theta(x_{t_n},t_n,c). \]

This is the paper's ODE step. Three properties matter for RL:

  • The sampler is a deterministic map. Given the prompt and the initial noise, the endpoint is fixed. The $K$ samples per prompt differ only through their initial noise $x_1$. The randomness lives entirely at $t=1$.
  • Each step costs one network evaluation (two with CFG, see §5). For a 2.5B-parameter transformer on a $64\times64$ latent, a step is a full forward pass, so step count is the main cost knob. The paper's SD3.5-M rollouts use 10 steps; evaluation uses 40 (the repo's configs/paper/image/sd3_geneval_art.yaml).
  • Few steps cost accuracy. The ideal trajectories are curved (the field changes as the posterior sharpens), and Euler follows the tangent. With one step from $t=1$, $x_0=x_1+1\cdot v^*(x_1,1)$. Using the conversion of §2, $x_t+t\,v^*=\mathbb E[x_0\mid x_t]$, so one Euler step returns $\mathbb E[x_0\mid x_1]$, which at $t=1$ is just the data mean: every noise sample collapses to the same blurry average. The toy output below shows exactly this.

Why integrating the marginal field produces the right distribution

Let $p_t$ be the distribution of $x_t=(1-t)x_0+t\epsilon$. A density moved by a velocity field obeys the continuity equation (conservation of probability mass). In increasing time the motion is $dx/dt=-v$, so

\[ \partial_t p_t(x)+\nabla\!\cdot\!\big(p_t(x)\,(-v^*(x,t))\big)=0 . \]

Here is the intuition for why $v^*$ satisfies it. Each straight conditional path, with its own constant velocity $-u_t$, moves its own little conditional density $p_t(x\mid x_0,\epsilon)$ consistently. Summing over all pairs, the total probability flux through a point $x$ is $\mathbb E\big[-u_t\,\delta(x_t-x)\big]=-p_t(x)\,\mathbb E[u_t\mid x_t=x]=-p_t(x)\,v^*(x,t)$. So the marginal field carries exactly the flux of all the conditional paths combined, and starting from $p_1=\mathcal N(0,I)$ the ODE reproduces every intermediate $p_t$, ending at $p_0=p_{\text{data}}$. Lipman et al. (2022) give the full proof. The point to keep: the averaged field produces the right marginals, but its trajectories are not the straight conditional segments; they bend, and they never cross.

Pitfall

Do not assume more steps means a different distribution. For an exact field, every step count targets the same $p_0$; steps only control discretization error. But for a trained field the endpoint distribution does depend on the step count and solver, which is one concrete source of the paper's forward–reverse gap $e$: the endpoints you score come from the discretized sampler, not from the ideal ODE.

5. ODE versus SDE sampling

Same marginals, injected noise

The ODE is not the only process with marginals $p_t$. Consider, in increasing time, the stochastic differential equation (SDE)

\[ dx=\Big[f_t(x)+\tfrac{g_t^2}{2}\nabla\log p_t(x)\Big]dt+g_t\,dW,\qquad f_t:=-v^*, \]

where $W$ is a Brownian motion and $g_t\ge0$ is any noise schedule. Its density obeys the Fokker–Planck equation $\partial_tp=-\nabla\!\cdot\!\big(p f\big)-\nabla\!\cdot\!\big(\tfrac{g^2}{2}p\nabla\log p\big)+\tfrac{g^2}{2}\Delta p$. Since $p\nabla\log p=\nabla p$, the last two terms are $-\tfrac{g^2}{2}\Delta p+\tfrac{g^2}{2}\Delta p=0$, leaving the continuity equation of the ODE. So for every choice of $g_t$ the SDE has the same marginals as the ODE. The extra drift exactly compensates the diffusion the noise causes.

For generation we run time backward. The time reversal of a forward SDE $dx=F\,dt+g\,dW$ is $dx=\big[F-g^2\nabla\log p_t\big]dt+g\,d\bar W$ with $dt<0$ (Anderson, 1982). With $F=f+\tfrac{g^2}{2}\nabla\log p$ this is $dx=\big[f-\tfrac{g^2}{2}\nabla\log p_t\big]dt+g\,d\bar W$. Take one Euler–Maruyama step from $t$ to $t-h$ ($h>0$, $dt=-h$, $f=-v$):

\[ x_{t-h}=x_t+h\Big[v+\tfrac{g_t^2}{2}\nabla\log p_t(x_t)\Big]+g_t\sqrt h\,z,\qquad z\sim\mathcal N(0,I). \]

Substitute the score from §2, $\nabla\log p_t=-(x_t-(1-t)v)/t$, and choose the Flow-GRPO noise schedule $g_t^2=\nu^2\,t/(1-t)$ (Liu et al., 2025), with a scalar noise level $\nu$. Then $\tfrac{g_t^2(1-t)}{2t}=\tfrac{\nu^2}{2}$ and $\tfrac{g_t^2}{2t}=\tfrac{\nu^2}{2(1-t)}$, and the step becomes a Gaussian transition

\[ x_{t-h}\sim\mathcal N\big(\mu(s_t,v),\ \sigma_{\text{ref}}^2(t)\,I\big),\qquad \mu(s_t,v)=x_t+h\Big[\Big(1+\frac{\nu^2}{2}\Big)v-\frac{\nu^2}{2(1-t)}\,x_t\Big],\qquad \sigma_{\text{ref}}^2(t)=\nu^2\,\frac{t}{1-t}\,h . \]

This is exactly the reference transition in the paper's Appendix C.2 (it writes $\mu(s_t,v)$ and $\sigma^2_{\text{ref}}(t)$ and uses $\nu=0.8$). Setting $\nu=0$ recovers the Euler ODE step. The coefficient $t/(1-t)$ blows up at $t=1$: the paper uses the endpoint convention $\mu=x_t+hv$, $\sigma_{\text{ref}}^2(1)=\nu^2$ there, while the repo's sampler replaces $1-t$ by $1-t_1$ (the next grid point) at $t=1$.

Map it to the formula: diffusion is $g_t=\nu\sqrt{t/(1-t)}$ with noise_level $=\nu$ (repo default $0.7$). Expanding, the mean is $x+dt\,\big[v_{\text{SD3}}+\tfrac{g_t^2}{2t}\big(x+(1-t)\,v_{\text{SD3}}\big)\big]$, which is Flow-GRPO's drift written in the SD3 sign; substitute $v_{\text{SD3}}=-v$ and $dt=-h$ and you get $\mu(s_t,v)$ above. The standard deviation is $g_t\sqrt h$.

The consequence that matters for RL: who has a log-prob

An SDE step is a Gaussian with a known mean and variance, so you can evaluate $\log p_\theta(x_{t-h}\mid x_t,c)=-\tfrac{\lVert x_{t-h}-\mu_\theta\rVert^2}{2\sigma^2}-D\log\sigma-\tfrac D2\log2\pi$ for any $\theta$, where $D$ is the number of coordinates. (The repo averages over coordinates instead of summing, the .mean(1) above, which rescales log-probs and KLs by $1/D$, exactly as the paper's KL proxy divides by $D$.) That is everything PPO needs. Flow-GRPO treats each step as an action and uses the per-step ratio

\[ \rho=\frac{p_\theta(x_{t-h}\mid x_t,c)}{p_{\text{old}}(x_{t-h}\mid x_t,c)},\qquad \log\rho=\frac{\lVert x_{t-h}-\mu_{\text{old}}\rVert^2-\lVert x_{t-h}-\mu_\theta\rVert^2}{2\sigma_{\text{ref}}^2(t)}, \]

since both policies share $\sigma_{\text{ref}}(t)$ and differ only through $v$ in the mean (the paper, Appendix C.1). An ODE step is a point mass, $x_{t-h}=x_t+hv_\theta$. It has no density, the ratio between two different ODE policies is $0$ or undefined, and the KL between them is infinite. An SDE step has a computable log-prob; an ODE step has none.

This one fact organizes diffusion RL. Reverse-process methods (Flow-GRPO, DanceGRPO) must roll out with an SDE to get ratios, paying for injected noise that degrades samples at few steps. Forward-process methods (DiffusionNFT, DiffusionART) never need a likelihood: they train with MSE on velocities and can roll out with the deterministic ODE. DiffusionART still wants a PPO-like trust region, so it borrows the SDE only as a reference to define a KL proxy. Because the two reference Gaussians share $\sigma_{\text{ref}}$, their KL is $\lVert\mu_\theta-\mu_{\text{old}}\rVert^2/(2\sigma_{\text{ref}}^2)$ and $\mu_\theta-\mu_{\text{old}}=h(1+\nu^2/2)\,(v_\theta-v_{\text{old}})$. The rollout itself remains an ODE (the paper, §4.3). Step 5 builds this mask.

From LLM RL

In LLM RL the policy is stochastic by construction (a softmax), so log-probs are free and "temperature 0" is the special case. In flow models the natural sampler is deterministic and stochasticity is something you add, with a knob $\nu$, purely so that a likelihood exists. Raising $\nu$ is not like raising temperature: the SDE has the same marginals for every $\nu$ in continuous time. It changes how much each step is randomized, not what distribution you sample, up to discretization error.

Worked example: one SDE step

Take the toy mixture used below, the unconditional field at $x_t=(0.5,0.5)$, $t=0.5$, which is $v=(-0.355,\,1.961)$, a step $h=0.1$ and $\nu=0.7$.

  • ODE: $x_{0.4}=x_t+0.1\,v=(0.4645,\,0.6961)$.
  • SDE: $g_t^2=0.49\cdot0.5/0.5=0.49$, $\sigma_{\text{ref}}=\sqrt{0.49\cdot0.1}=0.2214$. The mean is $x_t+0.1\big[1.245\,v-0.49\,x_t\big]=(0.4313,\,0.7196)$. The mean is pulled slightly toward the origin by the $-x_t$ term and pushed further along $v$, which makes room for the injected noise.
  • If the realized noise is $z=(1,-0.5)$, the sample is $\mu+0.2214z$ and its log-density is $-\tfrac{1.25}{2}-2\log0.2214-\log2\pi=0.553$. At $z=0$ it is $1.178$. Log-densities of continuous variables can be positive; only differences (ratios) are meaningful.

If the trained velocity drifts by $d=(0.05,0.05)$, then $\Delta\mu=0.1\cdot1.32\cdot d$, $\lVert\Delta\mu\rVert^2=8.71\times10^{-5}$, and $m=\lVert\Delta\mu\rVert^2/(2D\sigma_{\text{ref}}^2)=3.4\times10^{-4}$, well above the paper's threshold $\delta=10^{-5}$.

8. Classifier-free guidance

Motivation

A text-conditioned model $v_\theta(x_t,t,c)$ trained only by flow matching samples from $p(x_0\mid c)$, and in practice those samples often follow the prompt loosely. Classifier-free guidance (CFG; Ho and Salimans, 2022) is the standard inference-time fix. It sharpens the conditional distribution toward samples that are more typical of $c$ than of images in general, trading diversity for prompt adherence.

The formula

During pretraining the prompt is dropped (replaced by an empty or null embedding) for a fraction of examples, so the same network also learns the unconditional field $v_\theta(x_t,t,\varnothing)$. At sampling time, evaluate both and extrapolate:

\[ v_{\text{cfg}}(x_t,t,c)=v_\theta(x_t,t,\varnothing)+w\,\big[v_\theta(x_t,t,c)-v_\theta(x_t,t,\varnothing)\big]. \]

$w$ is the guidance scale. $w=1$ is the plain conditional model, $w=0$ is unconditional, and $w>1$ (typical values 3.5 to 7 for SD3-class models) extrapolates past the conditional field.

Why it works: Bayes' rule on scores

By Bayes' rule $p_t(x\mid c)\propto p_t(x)\,p_t(c\mid x)$, so $\nabla\log p_t(x\mid c)=\nabla\log p_t(x)+\nabla\log p_t(c\mid x)$. The second term is the gradient of an implicit classifier: it points toward noisy states that make the prompt more likely. CFG amplifies it by $w$:

\[ \nabla\log p_t(x)+w\,\nabla\log p_t(c\mid x)=(1-w)\,\nabla\log p_t(x)+w\,\nabla\log p_t(x\mid c)=\nabla\log\Big[p_t(x)^{1-w}\,p_t(x\mid c)^{w}\Big]. \]

Why does a score formula apply to velocities? From §2, at fixed $(x,t)$ the velocity is an affine function of the score with condition-independent coefficients: $v=(x+t\,\nabla\log p_t)/(1-t)$. An affine map preserves combinations whose weights sum to one, and $(1-w)+w=1$. So the velocity combination above is exactly the guided score translated into velocity space. The target is, roughly, the sharpened distribution $p(x)^{1-w}p(x\mid c)^w\propto p(x\mid c)\,p(c\mid x)^{w-1}$. ("Roughly" because the guided noisy marginals are not exactly the noised version of one fixed endpoint distribution; CFG is a heuristic in that sense.)

Because the combination is linear and both terms share the same sign convention, the SD3 sign flip does not affect this formula.

Distilling CFG, and why DiffusionART borrows the word "guidance"

CFG doubles the cost of every step. CFG distillation trains a student network $v_\phi(x_t,t,c)$ to reproduce the guided teacher with a single evaluation, by regression: minimize $\lVert v_\phi(x_t,t,c)-v_{\text{cfg}}(x_t,t,c)\rVert^2$ on states the teacher visits. FLUX.1-dev is distilled this way and takes the scale as an input ("guidance embedding"); HunyuanVideo and the LightX2V Wan2.1-I2V checkpoint used in the paper are CFG-distilled. Consequently the paper runs FLUX with "its built-in guidance embedding at scale 3.5, without additional CFG", and trains SD3.5-M "without CFG" (the paper, §5.1); the repo's SD3.5 configs set guidance_scale: 1.

Keep the shape of CFG distillation in mind: take the current model's velocity, add a direction that makes samples better, and regress the model onto that frozen sum. DiffusionART has exactly this shape. Its frozen target is $v^{\text{ART}}_{\text{tgt}}(s_t)=v_\theta^{(0)}(s_t)+G(c)$, where $G(c)$ is a normalized reward–endpoint covariance (the paper, §4.1). The repo README calls this reward direction "guidance" and describes the method as letting the model internalize reward guidance, "much like distilling CFG". The difference is where the direction comes from: CFG's direction comes from the conditional versus unconditional model; DiffusionART's comes from which of the prompt's $K$ rollouts scored higher. Steps 4 and 5 develop this.

From LLM RL

CFG is logit arithmetic. In LLM terms it is $\ell_{\text{uncond}}+w(\ell_{\text{cond}}-\ell_{\text{uncond}})$ on logits, the same form as contrastive decoding and "CFG for language models", and it samples from $p(y\mid c)\,p(c\mid y)^{w-1}$. Distilling it is like fine-tuning a model on its own contrastively decoded outputs so that you no longer need two forward passes.

9. Latent diffusion and practicalities

VAE latents

SD3, FLUX, HunyuanVideo and Wan do not run the flow in pixel space. A pretrained variational autoencoder (VAE) compresses images (or video frames) into a latent tensor, and the flow is learned on latents. For SD3.5-M the latent has 16 channels and $8\times$ spatial downsampling, so a $512\times512$ image is a $16\times64\times64$ latent (latent_channels: 16, spatial_factor: 8 in configs/examples/sd3_pickscore.yaml). The latent is also normalized with an affine map so that it has roughly unit scale: the model works on $z=(z_{\text{vae}}-\text{shift})\cdot\text{scale}$ with scaling_factor: 1.5305, shift_factor: 0.0609, and decoding inverts it (affine_decode in src/visual_rl/models/base.py: latents / scale + shift). Everything in this tutorial ($x_0$, $x_t$, $v$, the covariance $G(c)$) lives in this normalized latent space. Rewards are computed after decoding to pixels, so a reward is a function of $x_0$ through a fixed decoder.

Text encoders

The prompt $c$ enters as embeddings. SD3 uses three frozen encoders: two CLIP text encoders, which provide token embeddings and a pooled vector, and T5-XXL, which provides long token embeddings. The repo calls pipeline.encode_prompt once and caches the outputs (src/visual_rl/models/sd3_text.py); the transformer receives encoder_hidden_states (token embeddings) and pooled_projections. During RL only the transformer (LoRA in the paper's image runs) is trained; the encoders and VAE are frozen.

Time schedules and shifting

A uniform grid on $[0,1]$ is not ideal. At high resolution, neighboring latent pixels are strongly correlated, so a given noise level $t$ destroys less information than it would at low resolution, and the model needs more steps at high noise to make the global decisions (layout, which mode). SD3 (Esser et al., 2024) therefore shifts the schedule:

\[ t'=\frac{\lambda\,t}{1+(\lambda-1)\,t},\qquad \lambda>0 . \]

With $\lambda=3$ (the repo's SD3.5 setting, shift: 3) and 10 steps, the uniform grid becomes (from cfg_toy.py):

uniform: [1.  0.9 0.8 0.7 0.6 0.5 0.4 0.3 0.2 0.1 0. ]
shift=3: [1.    0.964 0.923 0.875 0.818 0.75  0.667 0.562 0.429 0.25  0.   ]

Half of the steps now sit above $t=0.8$, and the last step jumps from $0.25$ to $0$. The repo implements this in shifted_schedule and, for SD3, takes the grid directly from diffusers' FlowMatchEulerDiscreteScheduler(shift=...) (src/visual_rl/training/image_contracts.py):

Two more conventions: the transformer receives $t\cdot1000$ as its timestep input (timestep_scale = 1000.0 in src/visual_rl/models/sd3.py); and the NFT endpoint sampler floors model time to multiples of $1/1000$ to reproduce the SD3 pipeline exactly (quantize_model_time in src/visual_rl/training/nft_sampling.py). Video models use larger shifts (the HunyuanVideo configs use shift: 7.0).

An annotated SD3.5 sampling loop

Every piece maps to a section of this step: the text encoders produce $c$, the scheduler gives the shifted grid, the loop is Euler (§4) in the SD3 sign, the chunk(2) line is CFG (§8), and the last line undoes the latent normalization. An RL rollout in the repo is this loop with $w=1$ (no CFG), batched over $K$ noises per prompt, and with every state $x_t$ and prediction $v$ cached for training.

Pitfalls

  • Sign. Check every formula you port against the table above. A guidance direction added with the wrong sign trains the model to make samples worse, and the MSE loss will not tell you.
  • The $t=1$ singularity. The Flow-GRPO SDE coefficient $t/(1-t)$ is infinite at $t=1$. Code must special-case the first step (the repo uses the next grid point; the paper's trust-region reference uses $\sigma_{\text{ref}}^2(1)=\nu^2$).
  • Log-prob normalization. The repo's transition log-prob is a per-coordinate mean, not a sum. Ratios and KLs computed from it are per-coordinate quantities; thresholds such as $\delta=10^{-5}$ assume that.
  • Steps and noise interact. SDE sampling at 10 steps is visibly worse than ODE at 10 steps (toy: within-mode std up to 0.43 versus 0.35 for the data). Do not compare an SDE-rolled-out policy and an ODE-rolled-out policy at equal steps and attribute the difference to training.
  • CFG cost and batch layout. CFG doubles the batch; the repo puts the negative prompt first for SD3 and second for Wan. Mixing up the halves flips the sign of the guidance term.
  • Loss is not quality. The flow-matching loss has an irreducible floor, and its value depends on the endpoint distribution. Do not read RL progress from the MSE value.

10. Exercises

Exercise 1.1 — Derive $\mathbb E[u_t\mid x_t]$ for a single Gaussian endpoint $x_0\sim\mathcal N(m,s^2I)$ in $D$ dimensions, and check the limits $t\to0$ and $t\to1$.

Coordinates are independent, so work per coordinate. $x_t=(1-t)x_0+t\epsilon$ is Gaussian with mean $(1-t)m$ and variance $V_t=(1-t)^2s^2+t^2$. Covariances: $\operatorname{Cov}(x_0,x_t)=(1-t)s^2$, $\operatorname{Cov}(\epsilon,x_t)=t$. For jointly Gaussian $(a,b)$, $\mathbb E[a\mid b]=\mathbb E a+\tfrac{\operatorname{Cov}(a,b)}{\operatorname{Var}b}(b-\mathbb Eb)$. Hence

\[ \mathbb E[x_0\mid x_t]=m+\tfrac{(1-t)s^2}{V_t}(x_t-(1-t)m),\quad \mathbb E[\epsilon\mid x_t]=\tfrac{t}{V_t}(x_t-(1-t)m), \]
\[ v^*(x_t,t)=\mathbb E[x_0-\epsilon\mid x_t]=m+\frac{(1-t)s^2-t}{V_t}\,(x_t-(1-t)m). \]

$t\to0$: $V_0=s^2$, slope $1$, $v^*=m+(x_0-m)=x_0$. This is $x_0-\mathbb E[\epsilon\mid x_0]=x_0-0$, since at $t=0$ the state carries no information about $\epsilon$. $t\to1$: $V_1=1$, slope $-1$, $v^*=m-x_1$, so the field points from the current noise straight at the data mean. Numerically, $m=2,s=0.5,t=0.5,x=1.5$ gives $1.4$, and the Monte Carlo check in cfg_toy.py gives $1.397$.

Exercise 1.2 — Show that one Euler step from $t$ to $0$ under the ideal velocity returns $\mathbb E[x_0\mid x_t]$. What does one step from $t=1$ produce, and what does that imply for few-step samplers?

One step of size $\Delta=t$ gives $x_t+t\,v^*(x_t,t)$. Using $v^*=\mathbb E[x_0\mid x_t]-\mathbb E[\epsilon\mid x_t]$ and $x_t=(1-t)\mathbb E[x_0\mid x_t]+t\,\mathbb E[\epsilon\mid x_t]$ (condition the identity $x_t=(1-t)x_0+t\epsilon$ on $x_t$):

\[ x_t+t\,v^*=(1-t)\hat x_0+t\hat\epsilon+t\hat x_0-t\hat\epsilon=\hat x_0=\mathbb E[x_0\mid x_t]. \]

At $t=1$, $x_1=\epsilon$ is independent of $x_0$, so $\mathbb E[x_0\mid x_1]=\mathbb E[x_0]$: every noise sample maps to the data mean. The toy confirms it ("one-step Euler from t=1 lands at [-0.4 -0.8]", the mixture mean). The ideal velocity gives the posterior mean, which for multimodal data is not a sample. Few-step generation therefore needs either many steps, a higher-order solver, or a distilled model that is trained to jump to samples rather than means (this is what "step-distilled" means for the Wan checkpoint in the paper).

Exercise 1.3 — Compute the CFG velocity by hand. For the toy mixture and condition "mode C" ($m=(0,2)$, $s=0.35$), compute $v_{\text{cond}}$ at $x_t=(0.5,0.5)$, $t=0.5$, then $v_{\text{cfg}}$ with $w=3$ given $v_{\text{uncond}}=(-0.355,1.961)$. Also: which distribution does CFG target, in terms of $p(x\mid c)$ and $p(c\mid x)$?

One-hot $\pi$ reduces the mixture to the single Gaussian of Exercise 1.1. $V=0.25\cdot0.1225+0.25=0.280625$. Slope $=(0.5\cdot0.1225-0.5)/0.280625=-0.43875/0.280625=-1.5635$. $x-(1-t)m=(0.5,0.5)-(0,1)=(0.5,-0.5)$. So $v_{\text{cond}}=(0,2)-1.5635\,(0.5,-0.5)=(-0.782,\,2.782)$.

$v_{\text{cond}}-v_{\text{uncond}}=(-0.427,\,0.821)$, so $v_{\text{cfg}}=(-0.355-1.281,\ 1.961+2.463)=(-1.636,\,4.424)$, matching the script's $(-1.635,4.423)$ up to rounding. The guided score is $\nabla\log[p(x)^{1-w}p(x\mid c)^w]$ and, since $p(x)=p(x\mid c)p(c)/p(c\mid x)$, $p(x)^{1-w}p(x\mid c)^w\propto p(x\mid c)\,p(c\mid x)^{w-1}$: the conditional distribution reweighted toward states where the implicit classifier is confident. This explains why $w=4$ pushes mode-C samples upward, away from modes A and B, where $p(c\mid x)$ is highest.

Exercise 1.4 — For the Flow-GRPO SDE step, (a) show that $\nu\to0$ recovers Euler; (b) write $\log\rho$ between two policies that differ only in $v$; (c) explain why the same construction gives no usable ratio for an ODE policy.

(a) With $\nu=0$, $\mu=x_t+h[(1+0)v-0]=x_t+hv$ and $\sigma_{\text{ref}}^2=0$, which is the Euler step.

(b) Both transitions are $\mathcal N(\mu(s_t,v_\cdot),\sigma_{\text{ref}}^2I)$ with the same variance. For an observed next state $x'$:

\[ \log\rho=\log\frac{\mathcal N(x';\mu_\theta,\sigma^2I)}{\mathcal N(x';\mu_{\text{old}},\sigma^2I)}=\frac{\lVert x'-\mu_{\text{old}}\rVert^2-\lVert x'-\mu_\theta\rVert^2}{2\sigma^2},\qquad \mu_\theta-\mu_{\text{old}}=h\Big(1+\frac{\nu^2}{2}\Big)(v_\theta-v_{\text{old}}). \]

The normalizers cancel. The ratio depends on the velocity difference scaled by $h(1+\nu^2/2)/\sigma$, so small $\nu$ makes the ratio extremely sensitive to small velocity changes.

(c) As $\nu\to0$, $\sigma\to0$ and $\log\rho$ diverges for any $v_\theta\neq v_{\text{old}}$; at $\nu=0$ the transitions are Dirac masses and the ratio is $0$ or undefined. DiffusionART therefore rolls out with the ODE and uses an MSE objective; it uses the SDE only as a fixed $\nu=0.8$ reference to measure drift, $m=\lVert\Delta\mu\rVert^2/(2D\sigma_{\text{ref}}^2)$ (the paper, §4.3).

Exercise 1.5 — The repo computes the clean-sample estimate as x - t * v and steps with x + (nxt - t) * v. Show both agree with the paper's formulas, and decide whether adding a data-space direction $G$ to the model output in repo code should use + G or - G.

The SD3 network approximates $v_{\text{SD3}}=\mathbb E[\epsilon-x_0\mid x_t]=-v^*$. Then x - t * v $=x_t+t\,v^*=\hat x_0$ (Exercise 1.2), and x + (nxt - t) * v $=x_t+(t-t_{\text{next}})\,v^*=x_t+\Delta v^*$, the paper's Euler step.

The paper's target is $v_{\text{tgt}}=v_{\text{old}}+G$ in the paper convention. In the SD3 convention the target becomes $-v_{\text{tgt}}=v_{\text{SD3,old}}-G$. So a repo that regresses raw network outputs must use - G (or negate the outputs first). An MSE such as $\lVert v_\theta-v_{\text{tgt}}\rVert^2$ is invariant to negating both arguments, so the only place the sign matters is how $G$ is added. Keep this in mind when you read src/visual_rl/algorithms/art.py in a later step.

Takeaways

  • Rectified flow noises with $x_t=(1-t)x_0+t\epsilon$; the per-sample velocity is $u_t=x_0-\epsilon$, pointing toward data as $t$ goes $1\to0$.
  • MSE regression on $u_t$ learns $v^*(x_t,t)=\mathbb E[u_t\mid x_t]$, the ideal forward velocity induced by the endpoint distribution. Reweight the endpoints and the ideal field changes; the paper's §3 analysis rests on this.
  • $\hat x_0=x_t+tv$, $\hat\epsilon=x_t-(1-t)v$, and the score $=-\hat\epsilon/t$ are interchangeable views of the same prediction.
  • Euler ODE sampling is a deterministic map from noise to sample; few steps collapse toward posterior means.
  • The Flow-GRPO SDE has the same marginals, with Gaussian steps $\mathcal N(\mu(s_t,v),\sigma_{\text{ref}}^2I)$. Only SDE steps have log-probs, so likelihood-ratio RL needs SDE rollouts; DiffusionART uses ODE rollouts and the SDE only as a reference for a KL proxy.
  • CFG is $v_{\text{u}}+w(v_{\text{c}}-v_{\text{u}})$, justified by Bayes' rule on scores; distilling it regresses a model onto "current velocity plus an improving direction", the template DiffusionART reuses with a reward direction.
  • Production models work in normalized VAE latents with shifted schedules, and SD3/diffusers predict $\epsilon-x_0$: always check the sign.

Step 2 · about half a day

From LLM RL to diffusion RL

After this step you can write image or video generation as an MDP, compute the log-probability and importance ratio of a denoising step, build GRPO advantages from vision rewards, and explain why one terminal reward spread over many denoising steps gives a noisy gradient. You will also be able to place every algorithm in Step 3 into one of two families: reverse-process RL or forward-process RL.

Why RL for a diffusion model, and what carries over

Step 1 trained a velocity field $v_\theta(x_t,t,c)$ by regression. Given an endpoint $x_0$ from the dataset, a noise draw $\epsilon$ and a time $t$, the model learns to predict $u_t=x_0-\epsilon$ at the state $x_t=(1-t)x_0+t\epsilon$. That objective only asks the model to reproduce the data distribution. It contains no term for "the hands have five fingers", "the image shows exactly three red cubes", "the text on the sign reads OPEN", or "the motion is smooth". Those properties are judged by other models, such as preference models, object detectors, OCR engines and video VLMs, and the judgement is available only after a complete image or video exists.

You already know this situation from LLM post-training. A pretrained language model imitates text, a verifier or reward model scores complete responses, and RL moves the policy toward higher-scoring responses. The DiffusionART paper studies the same setting for images and videos, specifically online RL with black-box rewards: the generator samples, a frozen reward model scores the decoded pixels, and we update the generator using only the scalar scores (the paper, §1). Two neighbouring settings are out of scope for this step. ReFL backpropagates through a differentiable reward model, and Diffusion-DPO learns from a fixed preference dataset. Step 3 places both.

Most of your toolkit transfers directly: group sampling, advantage normalization, importance ratios, clipping, the rollout/trainer split, old and reference policies. Three things change, and each one shapes the algorithms in the rest of this tutorial.

  1. The action is a whole image. One "token" of the diffusion policy is the next latent, a continuous vector with tens of thousands to about a million coordinates. Densities over such vectors behave very differently from a softmax over a vocabulary.
  2. The policy has no intrinsic randomness. The standard sampler is a deterministic ODE. To have a likelihood at all, you must inject noise yourself by converting the ODE to an SDE, and you choose how much.
  3. Credit assignment is harder. An LLM response of 500 tokens also gets one terminal reward. In diffusion, however, each of the $T$ steps rewrites every pixel a little, and no single step owns any part of the output. The rest of this tutorial revolves around this point.

The denoising MDP

Pick a time grid $1=t_0>t_1>\dots>t_T=0$, where $T$ is the number of sampling steps. The paper uses $T=10$ for SD3.5-M, $T=6$ for FLUX.1-dev, $T=16$ for HunyuanVideo-13B and $T=4$ for the step-distilled Wan2.1-I2V-14B (the paper, §5). Index steps by $k=0,\dots,T-1$ and write $h_k=t_k-t_{k+1}>0$ for the step size. The MDP is:

  • State $s_k=(c,\,t_k,\,x_{t_k})$: the prompt, the current time, and the current latent. The state has a fixed size. It does not grow the way an LLM context grows, and it is Markov: the latent is the whole memory.
  • Initial state $s_0=(c,1,x_1)$ with $x_1\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$. The prompt comes from the dataset; the initial noise is drawn fresh.
  • Action $a_k=x_{t_{k+1}}$: the next latent itself.
  • Policy $\pi_\theta(a_k\mid s_k)=p_\theta(x_{t_{k+1}}\mid x_{t_k},c)$: one sampler step. With an SDE sampler it is a Gaussian whose mean is computed from $v_\theta(x_{t_k},t_k,c)$.
  • Transition deterministic: $s_{k+1}=(c,t_{k+1},a_k)$. The environment just copies the action into the state, exactly like appending a token.
  • Reward sparse and terminal: $0$ at every intermediate step, and $R(x_0,c)$ after the last step, where $x_0=x_{t_T}$ is decoded by the VAE and scored by a reward model.
  • Horizon $T$, undiscounted.

The return of a trajectory $\tau=(x_1,x_{t_1},\dots,x_0)$ is just $R(x_0,c)$, and the standard policy-gradient identity applies unchanged:

\[ \nabla_\theta\,\mathbb E_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}[R(x_0,c)] \;=\; \mathbb E_{\tau}\Big[R(x_0,c)\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x_{t_{k+1}}\mid x_{t_k},c)\Big]. \]

The initial noise $x_1$ does not appear in the sum because its density does not depend on $\theta$. Keep this in mind: $x_1$ affects the reward, but no action controls it. To the policy gradient it is pure noise.

From LLM RL

In LLM RL, "action = next token, transition = append" makes the trajectory log-probability the sum of token logprobs, which the trainer recomputes in one teacher-forced forward pass. In diffusion the decomposition is identical, but each term needs its own forward pass of the full network at a different noisy latent. Recomputing the log-probabilities of a 10-step trajectory costs 10 transformer forwards, not one. This is why reverse-process methods usually train on a subset of the steps.

The log-probability of one denoising step

Why we need noise at all

The Euler ODE step from Step 1 is $x_{t-\Delta}=x_t+\Delta\,v_\theta(x_t,t,c)$. Given the state, the next latent is a deterministic function of $\theta$. Its "density" is a Dirac delta: the ratio $p_\theta/p_{\text{old}}$ is $1$ if the new parameters happen to map to the same point and undefined otherwise. You cannot run PPO on that. Likelihood-based RL therefore replaces the ODE sampler with an SDE sampler that has the same marginal distributions $p_t(x_t\mid c)$ but injects Gaussian noise at every step (Step 1 derived the family). DDPO did not need this conversion because it fine-tuned stochastic DDPM/DDIM-style samplers that already inject noise at every step. For rectified-flow models (SD3, FLUX, HunyuanVideo, Wan), Flow-GRPO introduced the conversion.

The Flow-GRPO SDE step, in our notation

With step size $h=t-t_{\text{next}}>0$ and noise level $\nu\in(0,1]$, the paper writes the reverse transition as (the paper, Appendix "Reference Transitions for ODE Trust-Region Masking"):

\[ \mu(s_t,v)=x_t+h\Big[\big(1+\tfrac{\nu^2}{2}\big)v-\frac{\nu^2}{2(1-t)}\,x_t\Big],\qquad s^2(t)=\nu^2\,\frac{t}{1-t}\,h, \qquad 0<t<1. \]

and $x_{t_{\text{next}}}=\mu(s_t,v_\theta(s_t))+s(t)\,\xi$ with $\xi\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$. As a sanity check, setting $\nu=0$ gives $\mu=x_t+h v$ and $s=0$: the Euler ODE step. The extra drift term $\tfrac{\nu^2}{2}(v-x_t/(1-t))$ is the score correction that keeps the marginals unchanged when noise is added.

Here is the repo's implementation. It uses the opposite velocity sign: the model output in diffusers/SD3 is $v^{\text{repo}}=\epsilon-x_0=-v$, and the code integrates with dt = sigma_next - sigma $=-h<0$, so its ODE step x + dt * v equals our $x_t+h\,v$.

Map it to our notation. Inside the open interval $0<t<1$, guard = t, so diffusion$^2=\nu^2 t/(1-t)$ and diffusion$^2/(2t)=\nu^2/(2(1-t))$. Substituting dt$=-h$ and v$=-v$:

\[ \texttt{mean}=x_t\Big(1-\frac{\nu^2 h}{2(1-t)}\Big)+(-v)\Big(1+\frac{\nu^2}{2}\Big)(-h) = x_t+h\Big[\big(1+\tfrac{\nu^2}{2}\big)v-\frac{\nu^2}{2(1-t)}x_t\Big], \]
\[ \texttt{std}=\nu\sqrt{\tfrac{t}{1-t}}\sqrt{h}=s(t). \]

This matches the paper. At $t=1$ the formula divides by zero, and the two sources resolve it differently. The rollout code replaces $1-t$ by $1-t_{\text{next}}$ inside the guard (sigma_max is the schedule's second sigma). The paper's trust-region reference uses the convention $\mu=x_t+hv$, $s^2=\nu^2$ at $t=1$. Our toy below follows the rollout code.

Two more details in the code matter. First, sample is either drawn (innovation) or supplied (observed). During training you replay a stored transition: you recompute mean with the current parameters and evaluate the density at the stored next latent. This is teacher forcing for diffusion. Second, the last line returns logp.flatten(1).mean(1): the log-density is averaged over the $D$ coordinates, not summed. We return to this choice after deriving the ratio.

Deriving the log-probability and the ratio

Write $\mu_\theta=\mu(s_t,v_\theta(s_t))$ and $s=s(t)$. The transition is an isotropic Gaussian in $D$ dimensions, so

\[ \log p_\theta(x'\mid s_t) = -\frac{\lVert x'-\mu_\theta\rVert^2}{2s^2} - D\log s - \frac D2\log 2\pi . \]

The variance $s^2$ depends only on the schedule and $\nu$, not on $\theta$. In the importance ratio between the current and the rollout policy, the normalizers cancel:

\[ \log\rho = \log p_\theta(x'\mid s_t)-\log p_{\text{old}}(x'\mid s_t) = \frac{\lVert x'-\mu_{\text{old}}\rVert^2-\lVert x'-\mu_\theta\rVert^2}{2s^2}. \]

The stored sample was drawn from the old policy, $x'=\mu_{\text{old}}+s\,\xi$. Let $\Delta=\mu_\theta-\mu_{\text{old}}$. Then $\lVert s\xi\rVert^2-\lVert s\xi-\Delta\rVert^2 = 2s\,\xi^\top\Delta-\lVert\Delta\rVert^2$, and

\[ \boxed{\;\log\rho=\frac{\xi^\top\Delta}{s}-\frac{\lVert\Delta\rVert^2}{2s^2}\;},\qquad \Delta=\mu_\theta-\mu_{\text{old}}=h\big(1+\tfrac{\nu^2}{2}\big)\big(v_\theta(s_t)-v_{\text{old}}(s_t)\big). \]

The $x_t$ term in $\mu$ is the same for both policies and cancels, so the ratio depends on the parameters only through the velocity difference at the stored state, and on the sample only through the stored noise $\xi$. Under the old policy $\xi\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$, so $\log\rho$ is exactly Gaussian:

\[ \log\rho\;\sim\;\mathcal N\!\Big(-\mathrm{KL},\;2\,\mathrm{KL}\Big),\qquad \mathrm{KL}=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\text{old}}\,\Vert\,p_\theta)=\frac{\lVert\Delta\rVert^2}{2s^2}. \]

(Check: $\mathbb E[\rho]=\exp(-\mathrm{KL}+\mathrm{KL})=1$, as an importance weight must.) The same expression $\lVert\Delta\rVert^2/(2s^2)$ is the KL used by the repo's transition_kl_per_sample in src/visual_rl/algorithms/policy.py and, divided by $D$, the paper's drift proxy $m(s_t)$ in §4.3.

The gradient follows by differentiating at $\theta=\theta_{\text{old}}$:

\[ \nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x'\mid s_t)\Big|_{\theta_{\text{old}}}=\frac{(x'-\mu_{\text{old}})^\top}{s^2}\,\frac{\partial\mu_\theta}{\partial\theta}=\frac{h(1+\nu^2/2)}{s}\;\xi^\top\,\frac{\partial v_\theta(s_t)}{\partial\theta}. \]

Read this carefully, because the credit-assignment argument rests on it. The log-probability gradient of a transition pushes the predicted velocity along the noise $\xi$ that this transition happened to draw, with a scalar coefficient $h(1+\nu^2/2)/s=\sqrt h\,(1+\nu^2/2)\,\nu^{-1}\sqrt{(1-t)/t}$. That coefficient grows as $t\to0$, where the injected noise is small, and as $\nu\to0$, the ODE limit, where it diverges.

High dimension makes the true ratio sharp

Suppose a parameter update changes every coordinate of the velocity by the same small amount, so each coordinate of $\Delta$ has magnitude $\delta s$ for a per-coordinate shift $\delta$ measured in units of the step noise. Then $\mathrm{KL}=D\delta^2/2$ and the standard deviation of $\log\rho$ is $\sqrt D\,\delta$. A shift that is invisible per coordinate becomes a large ratio once you multiply by $D$.

Worked numbers. SD3 at $512^2$ has latents of shape $16\times64\times64$, so $D=65{,}536$. Take a mid-trajectory step with $t=0.5$, $h=0.1$ (10 uniform steps) and $\nu=0.8$. Then $s=0.8\sqrt{0.5\cdot0.1/0.5}=0.253$. Let one update change each velocity coordinate by $0.01$. Then $\Delta_j=0.1\cdot1.32\cdot0.01=1.32\times10^{-3}$ and $\delta=\Delta_j/s=5.2\times10^{-3}$.

  • Summed over coordinates (the true density ratio): $\mathrm{KL}=65536\cdot(5.2\times10^{-3})^2/2=0.89$ nats, and $\log\rho\sim\mathcal N(-0.89,\,1.34^2)$. About 90% of transitions fall outside the usual PPO band $[0.8,1.2]$.
  • For HunyuanVideo at $480\times480$, 53 frames, the latent is about $16\times14\times60\times60\approx8.1\times10^5$ (assuming the usual 4× temporal and 8× spatial VAE compression). The same per-coordinate change gives $\mathrm{KL}\approx11$ nats per step: the new policy assigns essentially zero density to the old samples.

We verified these numbers by Monte Carlo (tutorial/scratch/step2/ratio_dim.py): for $D=65{,}536$, the empirical mean and standard deviation of $\log\rho$ were $-0.92$ and $1.31$ against theory $-0.89$ and $1.34$, with an empirical clip fraction of 0.896.

This is why the repo, following Flow-GRPO, averages the per-coordinate log-density instead of summing it. The averaged log-ratio is $\tfrac1D\log\rho\sim\mathcal N(-\delta^2/2,\ \delta^2/D)$: in our example its standard deviation is $2.0\times10^{-5}$. A clip of $0.2$ would never bind on that quantity, which is why the paper configs use ratio_clip: 1.0e-05 (Flow-GRPO, Flow-GRPO-Fast) or 0.0001 (DanceGRPO):

From LLM RL

In LLM PPO, the per-token ratio is a true probability ratio and $\epsilon=0.2$ is an intuitive trust region. Here the per-transition object being clipped is $\exp\big(\tfrac1D\log\rho\big)$, a geometric-mean ratio per coordinate. It is no longer an unbiased importance weight, and $\epsilon$ must be re-tuned on a completely different scale. Treat ratio_clip as a knob on "average per-coordinate log-density drift", and monitor clip_fraction just as you would in miles. Expect it to be much higher than in LLM RL.

Scalar vs pairwise feedback

Most reward models above return a scalar per sample. Many visual preferences, however, are naturally comparative ("which of these two looks better?"). The paper's DiffusionART-Pairwise variant (the paper, §4.4) splits the $K$ samples of a prompt into $K/2$ disjoint pairs and assigns winner/loser labels $(1,0)$, or $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$ for a tie; these labels replace the scalar rewards in an otherwise unchanged pipeline. The repo has configs for $K=2,4,8$ (configs/examples/hunyuan_pairwise_k*.yaml). Group normalization, which we cover next, already discards the absolute level of the reward and keeps only the within-group ordering and spread. That is why scalar and pairwise feedback fit into the same machinery.

Composite rewards

To optimize several criteria at once you have two options, and the repo keeps them strictly separate (docs/composite-rewards.md):

  1. Weighted scalar. Normalize each component with fixed statistics, $(R^{(j)}-m_j)/\sigma_j$, then form $R=\sum_j w_j\,\tilde R^{(j)}$ with literal weights. The paper's video runs use this with VideoReward heads (TA:VQ:MQ = 7:2:1, or VQ:MQ = 1:1).
  2. Per-component advantages. Group-normalize each component separately, then combine the advantages with positive weights divided by their sum: $A_i=\sum_j \tfrac{w_j}{\sum_l w_l}\,A_i^{(j)}$. The paper's four-reward T2I experiment (GenEval, PickScore, CLIPScore, HPSv2.1) uses this, without renormalizing the combination (renormalize_combined: false; src/visual_rl/training/multi_reward.py, combined_group_advantages).

The second option makes every component contribute on the same footing regardless of its native scale or spread. The first lets a high-variance component dominate the group statistics.

Pitfall: reward hacking

The paper does not catalogue reward hacking, so the examples here come from general practice with these scorers rather than from its experiments. Each reward model has blind spots that RL will find: preference and aesthetic models can reward over-saturated, high-contrast, over-smoothed "AI look" images; an OCR reward with a substring rule is satisfied by huge text that ignores the rest of the prompt; detector-based GenEval can be satisfied by unnatural layouts that make objects easy to detect; a motion-quality head can prefer nearly static videos if they look clean. The defences mirror LLM RL: combine complementary rewards (the paper's video runs keep TA at weight 7 to preserve prompt following), evaluate with held-out metrics and visual inspection, and optionally add a KL term to a reference policy, which is exact and cheap for Gaussian transitions.

Group normalization for K images per prompt

For one prompt $c$, sample $K$ endpoints and score them: $R_1,\dots,R_K$. The GRPO advantage is the z-score within the group:

\[ A_i=\frac{R_i-\bar R_c}{\operatorname{std}_c(R)+\varepsilon},\qquad \bar R_c=\frac1K\sum_{i=1}^K R_i . \]

As in LLM GRPO, centering by the group mean is a per-prompt baseline: it removes the prompt's difficulty, which does not depend on the policy's choice among samples, without needing a value network. Dividing by the spread makes the step size comparable across prompts and reward models. The repo implements several variants that you must not confuse:

  • group_sample_std: per-prompt unbiased std (src/visual_rl/training/objectives.py, group_advantages).
  • Per-prompt centering, but division by the global population std of the whole rollout batch plus $10^{-4}$ (src/visual_rl/training/image_contracts.py, prompt_advantages). This is what the SD3 Flow-GRPO path uses.
  • Per-component population std, combined with weights, for multi-reward runs (above).

Worked example, $K=4$. Four PickScore logits for one prompt: $R=(22.1,\,21.4,\,22.8,\,21.7)$.

  1. Mean: $\bar R=88.0/4=22.0$. Deviations: $(0.1,\,-0.6,\,0.8,\,-0.3)$; sum of squares $=0.01+0.36+0.64+0.09=1.10$.
  2. Sample std (divide by $K-1=3$): $\sqrt{1.10/3}=0.6055$. Advantages: $(0.165,\,-0.991,\,1.321,\,-0.495)$.
  3. Population std (divide by $K=4$): $\sqrt{1.10/4}=0.5244$. Advantages: $(0.191,\,-1.144,\,1.525,\,-0.572)$.
  4. Global std: if the std of all rewards in the rollout batch (all prompts) were $1.5$, the advantages would be $(0.067,\,-0.400,\,0.533,\,-0.200)$. This prompt has a smaller spread than the batch, so it gets a smaller step.

Now a nearly tied group: $R=(22.10,\,22.11,\,22.09,\,22.10)$. The per-group sample std is $0.0082$, and the advantages become $(0,\,1.21,\,-1.21,\,0)$. A difference of $0.01$ logits, well inside the reward model's noise, receives the same push as the clear $0.8$ difference above. With a global std of $1.5$ it would get $\pm0.0067$ instead. This is the main reason the global-std variant exists. An exact tie gives zero advantage, and the Flow-GRPO config filters such groups (zero_advantage_mode: filter_pad).

From LLM RL

With 0/1 verifier rewards in LLM RL, an all-correct or all-wrong group has zero advantage and is dropped, the same "dynamic sampling" logic as filter_pad. Visual rewards are continuous, so groups are almost never exactly tied, yet they are often effectively tied. Per-group std normalization then amplifies reward-model noise into full-size advantages. If your reward curve is flat but gradient norms look healthy, check this first.

Pitfall

The $\varepsilon$ in the denominator has units. docs/composite-rewards.md points out that $(R/26-\overline{R/26})/(\operatorname{std}(R/26)+\varepsilon)$ is not the same as the unscaled version. For our tied group with $\varepsilon=10^{-4}$ and population std, the advantages are $\pm1.39$ on raw logits but $\pm1.03$ after dividing by 26. Keep the reward scale fixed when you compare runs.

A complete toy: rollout, log-probs, advantages, one update

The next two blocks form one runnable file. A tiny MLP velocity model is pretrained by flow matching on a 2-D distribution with two blobs at $(\pm2,0)$. It is then treated as a policy. Each "prompt" $c\in\{-1,+1\}$ asks the endpoint to land on its side, with reward $R=c\cdot x_0^{(1)}$. The model is fed $c$ but was pretrained without any dependence on it, so before RL it lands on either side with roughly equal probability. Everything else is the real reverse-process pipeline in miniature: SDE rollout with the repo's mean and std (in contract sign), per-step log-probs, replay, GRPO advantages, per-transition gradients and one clipped update.

What to read from the output:

  • Log-probs are not bounded by zero. Trajectory 0's per-step log-probs range from $-4.65$ to $+2.26$. As $t\to0$ the step std shrinks from 0.80 to 0.084, the density peaks, and the log-density becomes positive. Never write "logprob ≤ 0" assertions in a diffusion trainer.
  • Replay is exact. Recomputing every transition with unchanged weights gives a ratio of exactly 1.0, which is the diffusion analogue of checking that the trainer reproduces rollout logprobs. In the real pipeline, latent rounding (FLUX) or a different precision breaks this, and the repo documents it: initial ratios "need not" be exactly one (src/visual_rl/training/rollout.py, L64–67).
  • Group normalization ignores absolute quality. The first prompt ($c=-1$) got four samples on the wrong side, with rewards from $-1.65$ to $-2.45$, yet it receives advantages up to $+1.01$, decided by how close to $x=0$ each sample landed. The second prompt has three good samples and one bad one. Its bad sample gets $-1.49$, and its good samples get only $+0.37$ to $+0.63$.
  • Per-transition gradients mostly cancel. The average norm of a single transition's gradient $A_i\nabla\log p$ is 3.47, while the norm of their mean is 0.78. The per-step signal-to-noise ratio across the 8 trajectories is 0.2–0.4. Most of each transition's gradient is its own noise $\xi$, as derived above.
  • Ratios drift most at low noise. After one SGD step, the ratio range grows from $[0.9999,1.0002]$ at $t=1$ to $[0.9935,1.0064]$ at $t=0.1$. The coefficient $\sqrt{(1-t)/t}$ in the score makes late steps the most sensitive. The toy has $D=2$; at $D=65{,}536$ the same per-coordinate drift multiplies the log-ratio spread by $\sqrt{32768}\approx181$.

The credit-assignment problem, quantified

The paper's introduction identifies the core inefficiency of reverse-process RL. Its policy gradient updates each transition along its own sampling noise, "weighted by a trajectory-level advantage that this transition only partly determines" (the paper, §1). We now make that statement quantitative.

A linear-Gaussian picture

Fix a prompt. The endpoint is a function of the initial noise and all the injected noises, $x_0=F(x_1,\xi_0,\dots,\xi_{T-1})$. Linearize the reward around its mean:

\[ R\approx\bar R+a^\top x_1+\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}b_k^\top\xi_k,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(R)=\lVert a\rVert^2+\sum_k\lVert b_k\rVert^2 . \]

Take the advantage $A=(R-\bar R)/\sigma_R$. The per-transition estimator is $\hat g_k=A\,\xi_k$, up to the scalar $h(1+\nu^2/2)/s$ and the Jacobian $\partial v/\partial\theta$, which do not change the signal-to-noise ratio. By Stein's lemma, $\mathbb E[f(\xi)\xi]=\mathbb E[\nabla_\xi f(\xi)]$ for $\xi\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$, so

\[ \mathbb E[\hat g_k]=\frac{b_k}{\sigma_R},\qquad \operatorname{tr}\operatorname{Cov}(\hat g_k)\approx\mathbb E[A^2]\,\mathbb E\lVert\xi_k\rVert^2=D. \]

If the $T$ steps contribute equally to the part of the reward variance that the initial noise does not explain, $\lVert b_k\rVert^2=(1-f)\sigma_R^2/T$ with $f=\lVert a\rVert^2/\sigma_R^2$, and

\[ \mathrm{SNR}_k=\frac{\lVert\mathbb E\hat g_k\rVert}{\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}\operatorname{Cov}\hat g_k}}\approx\sqrt{\frac{1-f}{T\,D}} . \]

Each transition receives a signal that shrinks as $1/\sqrt T$ (it owns only $1/T$ of the variance), carried on a noise vector whose energy grows with $D$ (it is pushed along all $D$ coordinates of its own $\xi_k$). The fraction $f$ explained by the initial noise is lost entirely, because no action controls $x_1$. Summing over $T$ transitions recovers some signal, but the low-noise steps near $t=0$ carry large score coefficients $\propto\sqrt{(1-t)/t}$ and add disproportionate variance.

The toy measurement

To measure this without approximations, use data $x_0\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$ in $D=4$ dimensions. The exact velocity is then known in closed form: $v^\star(x,t)=\mathbb E[x_0-\epsilon\mid x_t=x]=\frac{1-2t}{(1-t)^2+t^2}\,x$. Let the policy be $v^\star+\theta$ with a learnable bias $\theta\in\mathbb R^4$, and let the reward be $R=x_0^{(1)}$. Because the rollout is reparameterized, autograd through the sampler gives the true gradient $\nabla_\theta\mathbb E[R]$, against which we compare the GRPO-style estimator for $T=1,\dots,50$, with $\nu=0.8$ and groups of $K=8$.

Interpretation, column by column:

  • The estimator is unbiased in direction. Averaged over 5,000 groups, it points exactly along the true gradient (cosine 1.000). The problem is its variance, not its bias.
  • Single-transition SNR falls as $1/\sqrt T$. From $T=1$ to $T=50$ it drops from 0.40 to 0.058, a factor of 6.9; $\sqrt{50}=7.07$. The formula $\sqrt{(1-f)/(TD)}$ predicts 0.46, 0.16 and 0.070 for $T=1,10,50$ against the measured 0.40, 0.13 and 0.058. The steps do not contribute exactly equally, so the agreement is approximate.
  • A whole group still gets worse with $T$. Even summing all $K\cdot T$ transitions, SNR falls from 1.25 to 0.57. Finer schedules add low-noise steps near $t=0$ whose large score coefficients add variance faster than signal. In a real trainer each transition costs a full forward and backward pass, so the SNR per unit of compute falls much faster still.
  • The initial noise explains little here. With $\nu=0.8$ the injected noise dominates (in this toy $x_1$ explains 2–14% of $\operatorname{Var}R$). With a smaller $\nu$, closer to the ODE, $f$ grows: more of the reward is decided by $x_1$, which no action controls, and the transitions are left with less signal to share. Reducing $\nu$ to make rollouts "more like deployment" therefore costs gradient signal.
  • An endpoint-level direction does not degrade. The last column estimates the reward–endpoint covariance $\overline{\operatorname{Cov}}(R,x_0)$ from each group of 8 endpoints. Its SNR stays at about 1.18 for every $T$, because it never looks at individual transitions. Here $\theta$ is a velocity bias, so parameter and output space coincide and the two columns are comparable. This is a preview: aggregating the group before assigning credit to states is the idea behind DiffusionART's guidance $G(c)$ (the paper, §4.1), which Step 4 develops.

Key idea

Reverse-process RL asks each of the $T$ transitions to discover, from its own random noise vector, which part of a single trajectory-level scalar it was responsible for. In $D$ dimensions with $T$ steps, each transition's share of the signal is about $1/\sqrt{TD}$ of its gradient. More steps, more pixels and less injected noise all make this worse. LLM RL has the same structure, with a scalar reward spread over tokens, but each token is a discrete choice whose effect on the reward is often large and localized. A denoising step is a tiny, global, continuous nudge.

Two families: reverse-process and forward-process RL

Reverse-process RL

Reverse-process RL takes the MDP literally. Roll out with an SDE so that each transition has a density, store the trajectory, group-normalize the terminal rewards, and apply clipped PPO to every selected transition (the paper, Appendix "Reverse-Process Policy Optimization"):

\[ \mathcal J_{\mathrm{FG}}=\mathbb E_{i,k}\Big[\min\big(\rho_{i,k}A_i,\ \operatorname{clip}(\rho_{i,k},1-\epsilon_{\text{clip}},1+\epsilon_{\text{clip}})A_i\big)-\lambda_{\mathrm{KL}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(p_\theta(\cdot\mid x_k^{(i)},c)\,\Vert\,p_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x_k^{(i)},c)\big)\Big]. \]

Its strength is that it trains on states the generator actually visits, with a principled trust region. Its weakness is everything this step derived: it needs the ODE→SDE conversion, it needs stored trajectories and replay, its ratios are extremely sharp, and its per-transition gradient has poor credit assignment. DDPO, Flow-GRPO, DanceGRPO, Flow-GRPO-Fast (CPS) and Flow-DPPO all belong here. They differ in which steps are stochastic, how the SDE is parameterized, and how the trust region is enforced (Step 3).

Forward-process RL

Forward-process RL never computes a transition likelihood. It samples endpoints with any solver, including a fast deterministic ODE solver, and keeps only the endpoints and rewards. It then reuses the pretraining recipe: pick a fresh $t$ and $\epsilon$, form $x_t=(1-t)x_0+t\epsilon$, and regress $v_\theta(x_t)$ toward a target that leans toward $u_t=x_0-\epsilon$ for high-reward endpoints and away from it for low-reward ones. In DiffusionNFT the target is $v_{\text{tgt}}=a\,u_t+(1-a)\,v_{\text{old}}$ with $a=(2r-1)/\beta$ and $r\in[0,1]$ a clipped, centred reward (the paper, §2). This is supervised learning with reward weights, which is efficient and stable per step, and it needs no SDE. The catch, which the paper diagnoses in §3, is that the states $x_t$ are forward-noised endpoints, not states the sampler visits. The resulting mismatch forces DiffusionNFT to rely on joint positive–negative training and an EMA rollout policy whose lag must be tuned per task.

The systems view

Where the rollout time goes

One rollout sample costs $T$ forward passes of the diffusion transformer (doubled if you use CFG; the paper trains without CFG, on CFG-distilled video models, or with FLUX's built-in guidance embedding), one VAE decode, and one or more reward-model forwards. There is no KV cache and no prefix sharing: every step processes the full latent. For video, the VAE decode of 53 frames and a VLM reward (VideoReward is scored batch-one at 4 FPS) are substantial costs of their own. This is why $K$ is modest (8 for video) and why GPU-hours, not steps, are the paper's headline metric: for example, VQ 5.5 on HunyuanVideo took 63.4 GPU hours with DiffusionART versus 1,401.4 with DiffusionNFT (EMA) (the paper, §5.2).

Memory of stored trajectories

Reverse-process training replays transitions, so the rollout must keep, for every selected step, the state, the next state and the old log-prob, and in practice also the old mean and old velocity (Rollout in src/visual_rl/training/rollout.py; caches are detached FP32 CPU copies). A back-of-envelope estimate with four latent-sized FP32 tensors per cached step:

  • SD3 at $512^2$: $D=65{,}536$, 256 KiB per tensor, 9 selected steps, so 9 MiB per trajectory. The SD3 Flow-GRPO config samples $48\times24=1{,}152$ trajectories per rollout, about 10 GiB.
  • HunyuanVideo, $D\approx8.1\times10^5$, 3.1 MiB per tensor, all 16 steps cached: 197 MiB per trajectory; 256 videos per rollout, about 49 GiB.

Forward-process methods store only endpoints ($1/(4\times\text{steps})$ of that), but pay an extra forward pass of the old policy at each re-noised state, and keep an extra copy of the trainable weights as the EMA rollout policy (src/visual_rl/training/nft.py, PolicyShadow).

Why ODE rollouts can reuse cached velocities

During an ODE rollout the sampler computes $v_{\text{old}}(s_{t_k})$ at every visited state anyway, because that is how it takes the step. If the training objective is defined on those same states and only needs $v_{\text{old}}$ there, which is the case for DiffusionART's target $v_{\text{old}}+G(c)$, then the rollout's outputs are the old-policy predictions. No extra old-policy forward pass, no likelihood and no stored noise are needed, and the deterministic sampler makes the cached states reproducible from $x_1$. The repo's rollout cache says so explicitly: no extra sampling call is used to compute old-policy predictions on trajectory states, and re-noised NFT states need a different API (src/visual_rl/training/rollout.py, L58–62). Forward-process methods cannot reuse the cache, because their training states $x_t$ are new points that the sampler never visited.

Microbatches and the unit of work

The training unit in a diffusion trainer is a (trajectory, step) pair, not a sequence. Each pair is one full forward and backward pass at a single noisy latent, and pairs from different steps of the same trajectory are independent items that can be shuffled into different microbatches (shuffle_rows: true in the SD3 configs). The number of passes per update is therefore $(\text{prompts})\times K\times(\text{selected steps})$. The main cost lever is selecting fewer steps: Flow-GRPO-Fast and DanceGRPO train on a subset, while full Flow-GRPO uses 9 of 10. There is no padding or packing problem, since every item has the same shape. Rollout microbatching must be invariant to the physical batch size, so the repo draws all innovations for a logical batch before any model call (src/visual_rl/training/grpo_sampling.py, docstring).

LoRA vs full fine-tuning

The paper's image experiments train LoRA adapters (rank 32, $\alpha=64$, learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$); the video experiments fine-tune all parameters of 13–14B models at $10^{-5}$ (the paper, §5). LoRA makes the auxiliary policies cheap: the reference policy is the base model with adapters disabled, and an old or EMA policy is a copy of the adapter only. With full fine-tuning, every extra policy is another full copy of the weights to shard, so a method that needs no EMA policy and no separate old-policy forward saves real memory and time at video scale.

From LLM RL

In miles, the expensive part of rollout is autoregressive decoding in SGLang, and the trainer recomputes logprobs in one packed forward. In diffusion RL, generation is a short loop of full-size forwards, and the trainer's cost scales with the number of steps you train on, each a full forward and backward at a different noise level. The weight-sync problem between engine and trainer largely disappears, because the same module usually runs both. The train–inference mismatch problem remains, in a new form: sampler precision, latent rounding, and SDE-vs-ODE sampling.

Pitfalls checklist

  • Velocity sign. Diffusers/SD3 code predicts $\epsilon-x_0$ and steps with a negative dt. Our notation predicts $x_0-\epsilon$ and steps with $+h$. Mixing the two flips the score-correction term and silently produces wrong log-probs.
  • Sum vs mean log-prob. The repo averages over coordinates. A clip value copied from LLM RL (0.2) never binds on that quantity, and a clip value copied from the repo ($10^{-5}$) clips almost everything if you sum.
  • Terminal and initial steps. $s^2\propto t/(1-t)$ diverges at $t=1$, and the score coefficient diverges as $t\to0$. Use the repo's guard at $t=1$, and watch ratio statistics on the last steps.
  • Replay must reuse the stored next latent. Re-drawing the noise during training gives a different action and a meaningless ratio. The repo enforces "exactly one of innovation or observed".
  • Near-tied groups. Per-group std normalization turns reward-model noise into unit-size advantages. Prefer global std, or monitor the within-group reward spread.
  • Reward units. A fixed divisor (PickScore /26) changes the effect of $\varepsilon$ and the meaning of logged rewards. Label plots with the score space.

Exercises

Exercise 2.1 — Derive the distribution of the Gaussian log-ratio

Let $p_{\text{old}}=\mathcal N(\mu_{\text{old}},s^2I)$ and $p_\theta=\mathcal N(\mu_\theta,s^2I)$ in $D$ dimensions, and let $x'\sim p_{\text{old}}$. Show that $\log\rho=\log p_\theta(x')-\log p_{\text{old}}(x')\sim\mathcal N(-\mathrm{KL},2\mathrm{KL})$ with $\mathrm{KL}=\lVert\Delta\rVert^2/(2s^2)$, and verify that $\mathbb E[\rho]=1$.

Solution. The normalizers $-D\log s-\tfrac D2\log2\pi$ are equal and cancel. Writing $x'=\mu_{\text{old}}+s\xi$:

\[ \log\rho=\frac{\lVert s\xi\rVert^2-\lVert s\xi-\Delta\rVert^2}{2s^2}=\frac{2s\,\xi^\top\Delta-\lVert\Delta\rVert^2}{2s^2}=\frac{\xi^\top\Delta}{s}-\frac{\lVert\Delta\rVert^2}{2s^2}. \]

$\xi^\top\Delta\sim\mathcal N(0,\lVert\Delta\rVert^2)$, so $\log\rho$ has mean $-\lVert\Delta\rVert^2/(2s^2)=-\mathrm{KL}$ and variance $\lVert\Delta\rVert^2/s^2=2\mathrm{KL}$. The KL between two Gaussians with equal isotropic covariance is $\lVert\Delta\rVert^2/(2s^2)$, which confirms the identification. For a Gaussian $L\sim\mathcal N(m,v)$, $\mathbb E[e^L]=e^{m+v/2}=e^{-\mathrm{KL}+\mathrm{KL}}=1$.

Exercise 2.2 — Ratio sensitivity to dimension

With $t=0.5$, $h=0.1$, $\nu=0.8$ and a uniform per-coordinate velocity change of $0.01$ (so $\delta=5.22\times10^{-3}$), compute the fraction of transitions outside the clip band for (a) the summed log-ratio with $D=65{,}536$ and $\epsilon=0.2$; (b) the coordinate-averaged log-ratio with $\epsilon=10^{-5}$. (c) What happens to (b) when $D=8.06\times10^5$?

Solution. (a) $\log\rho\sim\mathcal N(-0.892,1.336^2)$. The band is $[\log0.8,\log1.2]=[-0.223,0.182]$. $P(\log\rho>0.182)=1-\Phi\big((0.182+0.892)/1.336\big)=1-\Phi(0.80)=0.21$ and $P(\log\rho<-0.223)=\Phi\big((-0.223+0.892)/1.336\big)=\Phi(0.50)=0.69$. Total $\approx0.90$; the Monte-Carlo run gave 0.896.

(b) $\tfrac1D\log\rho\sim\mathcal N(-\delta^2/2,\delta^2/D)=\mathcal N(-1.36\times10^{-5},(2.04\times10^{-5})^2)$. The band is about $[-10^{-5},10^{-5}]$. $P(>10^{-5})=1-\Phi(1.16)=0.12$ and $P(<-10^{-5})=\Phi(0.18)=0.57$, for a total of $0.69$.

(c) The standard deviation shrinks to $5.8\times10^{-6}$, but the mean $-\delta^2/2=-1.36\times10^{-5}$ does not depend on $D$ and already lies outside the band, so about 73% are clipped, now mostly on the low side. The averaged log-ratio has a deterministic bias of $-\mathrm{KL}/D$. With tiny clip values that bias, not the noise, decides clipping in high dimension.

Exercise 2.3 — Compute GRPO advantages

A prompt has $K=4$ GenEval scores $R=(1,0,1,1)$. Compute the advantages with (a) per-group sample std, (b) per-group population std, both with $\varepsilon=10^{-4}$. (c) Another prompt in the same batch has $R=(1,1,1,1)$. What does it contribute, and what should the trainer do with it?

Solution. Mean $0.75$; deviations $(0.25,-0.75,0.25,0.25)$; sum of squares $0.75$. (a) Sample std $=\sqrt{0.75/3}=0.5$, so $A=(0.5,-1.5,0.5,0.5)$. (b) Population std $=\sqrt{0.75/4}=0.433$, so $A=(0.577,-1.732,0.577,0.577)$. Both sum to zero, and the single failure gets three times the magnitude of each success. (c) All deviations are zero, so $A=0$ for all four. Every transition of those trajectories contributes a zero gradient but still costs forward and backward passes. This is why the repo filters zero-advantage groups (zero_advantage_mode: filter_pad), as dynamic sampling does in LLM GRPO.

Exercise 2.4 — Per-transition signal via Stein's lemma

Assume $R=\bar R+a^\top x_1+\sum_k b_k^\top\xi_k$ exactly, with independent standard normal $x_1,\xi_k\in\mathbb R^D$. Show that $\mathbb E[(R-\bar R)\xi_k]=b_k$, independent of $a$, and compute $\operatorname{tr}\operatorname{Cov}[(R-\bar R)\xi_k]$. What happens to the SNR if the initial noise explains $f=90\%$ of the reward variance?

Solution. $\mathbb E[(R-\bar R)\xi_k]=\mathbb E[\xi_k\xi_k^\top]b_k+\mathbb E[\xi_k x_1^\top]a+\sum_{j\ne k}\mathbb E[\xi_k\xi_j^\top]b_j=b_k$, since all cross terms vanish by independence. This is Stein's lemma for a linear function. For the covariance, $\mathbb E[(R-\bar R)^2\lVert\xi_k\rVert^2]$: split $R-\bar R=b_k^\top\xi_k+Z$ with $Z$ independent of $\xi_k$ and $\operatorname{Var}Z=\sigma_R^2-\lVert b_k\rVert^2$. Then $\mathbb E[Z^2\lVert\xi_k\rVert^2]=D(\sigma_R^2-\lVert b_k\rVert^2)$ and $\mathbb E[(b_k^\top\xi_k)^2\lVert\xi_k\rVert^2]=(D+2)\lVert b_k\rVert^2$. Subtracting $\lVert b_k\rVert^2$ for the mean gives $\operatorname{tr}\operatorname{Cov}=D\sigma_R^2+\lVert b_k\rVert^2\approx D\sigma_R^2$. So $\mathrm{SNR}_k\approx\lVert b_k\rVert/(\sigma_R\sqrt D)$. With equal shares, $\lVert b_k\rVert^2=(1-f)\sigma_R^2/T$; at $f=0.9$ the SNR drops by $\sqrt{0.1}\approx0.32$ relative to $f=0$. The 90% of the reward variance set by $x_1$ enters every transition's gradient as pure noise.

Exercise 2.5 — Budget a reverse-process rollout

You want to run Flow-GRPO on Wan2.1-I2V at $400\times640$, 53 frames, $T=4$ steps, 32 prompts × $K=8$. Assume 16 latent channels, 4× temporal and 8× spatial compression, FP32 caches, and four latent-sized tensors per cached step with all 4 steps cached. Estimate the trajectory cache. How many transformer forward+backward passes does one pass over the data take, and how does that compare with DiffusionART's training on the same visited states?

Solution. The latent is $16\times(52/4+1)\times(400/8)\times(640/8)=16\times14\times50\times80=896{,}000$ coordinates, 3.42 MiB in FP32. Per trajectory: $4\times4\times3.42\approx54.7$ MiB; for 256 videos about 13.7 GiB, held on CPU. One pass over the data is $256\times4=1{,}024$ forward+backward passes at single-latent batch size. DiffusionART also trains on visited states, so it does the same number of passes per epoch if it uses all steps, but it needs only states and cached old velocities (two latent tensors per step, about 6.8 GiB), no log-probs and no SDE noise. Its advantage is not cheaper passes but more reward improvement per pass, which is the credit-assignment argument above.

Takeaways

  • Sampling is an MDP: state $(c,t_k,x_{t_k})$, action = next latent, deterministic transitions, one terminal reward $R(x_0,c)$, horizon $T$ = 4–16 in the paper.
  • Likelihood-based RL requires an SDE sampler. The Flow-GRPO step has mean $x_t+h[(1+\nu^2/2)v-\nu^2x_t/(2(1-t))]$ and variance $\nu^2 t h/(1-t)$; the repo writes the same thing with the opposite velocity sign and negative dt.
  • The importance ratio depends only on the mean shift and the stored noise: $\log\rho=\xi^\top\Delta/s-\lVert\Delta\rVert^2/(2s^2)\sim\mathcal N(-\mathrm{KL},2\mathrm{KL})$. In $D\sim10^5$–$10^6$ dimensions the true ratio is extremely sharp, so practical code averages the log-density over coordinates and clips at $10^{-5}$.
  • Visual rewards are black-box, noisy, differently scaled scorers on decoded pixels. Group normalization removes scale and prompt difficulty, but amplifies noise in near-tied groups; multi-reward runs normalize each component before combining.
  • A per-transition policy gradient pushes each step along its own noise with a shared trajectory-level advantage. Its SNR scales like $\sqrt{(1-f)/(TD)}$, which is the credit-assignment problem. An endpoint-level, group-aggregated direction does not degrade with $T$.
  • Reverse-process RL (trains on visited states, needs likelihoods) and forward-process RL (re-noises endpoints, needs no likelihoods but needs EMA) trade efficiency against stability. DiffusionART (Step 4) takes the states of the first and the regression of the second.

A map of the design space

Step 2 ended with a split. Reverse-process methods train on the states that the sampler actually visits while it denoises from $t=1$ to $t=0$. Forward-process methods take the finished clean samples (endpoints), noise them again with the forward process $x_t=\alpha_t x_0+\sigma_t\epsilon$, and train on those re-noised states. Every baseline in this chapter sits somewhere on two axes:

  • What feedback it consumes. A scalar black-box reward on the endpoint (DDPO, Flow-GRPO, DiffusionNFT), the gradient of a differentiable reward model (ReFL), or a fixed dataset of preference pairs (Diffusion-DPO).
  • Where the update lands. On sampled reverse transitions through a likelihood ratio (DDPO, Flow-GRPO), or on forward-noised states through a regression target (DiffusionNFT, Diffusion-DPO), or on the final denoising step through the chain rule (ReFL).

The paper's introduction frames its own contribution in exactly these terms. Online RL "evaluates clean samples from the evolving generator and can use black-box rewards", unlike ReFL (needs reward gradients) and Diffusion-DPO (offline). Inside online RL, reverse-process methods are stable but credit-inefficient, and forward-process methods are efficient but need two stabilizers whose strength must be tuned per task. Keep that sentence in mind; each section below fills in one half of it.

Throughout, $c$ is the prompt, $K$ is the number of samples per prompt, $R_i=R(x_0^{(i)},c)$ is the raw reward of endpoint $i$, $\bar R_c$ is the prompt's mean reward, $u_t=x_0-\epsilon$ is the rectified-flow velocity target, $v_\theta$ is the trainable velocity and $v_{\text{old}}=v_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ is the rollout policy's velocity. The repository code uses the SD3 sign convention (predicts $\epsilon-x_0=-u_t$ and integrates with x + (t_next - t) * v); every code block below says how it maps back.

DDPO: denoising as a multi-step MDP

Motivation

Before 2023, the standard way to make a diffusion model produce "better" images was supervised: filter or reweight a dataset by a reward, then fine-tune on it (reward-weighted regression). Black et al. (DDPO, 2023) asked the RL question directly: if the reward is only a black-box function of the final image, such as JPEG compressibility, an aesthetic predictor or a VLM's judgment of prompt alignment, how do you compute a policy gradient for a diffusion sampler? Their answer, which every reverse-process method since inherits, is to stop treating the sampler as one big generative model and to treat each denoising step as an action.

The objective and its gradient

DDPO uses a stochastic DDPM/DDIM sampler, so each step is a Gaussian transition with a mean predicted by the network and a fixed, schedule-determined variance:

\[ p_\theta(x_{k-1}\mid x_k,c)=\mathcal N\!\big(x_{k-1};\ \mu_\theta(x_k,k,c),\ \sigma_k^2 I\big),\qquad k=T,\dots,1. \]

The MDP has state $s_k=(c,k,x_k)$, action $a_k=x_{k-1}$, deterministic "environment" (the next state is the action), zero reward until the end, and terminal reward $R(x_0,c)$. The objective is $\mathcal J(\theta)=\mathbb E_{c}\,\mathbb E_{x_{T:0}\sim p_\theta}[R(x_0,c)]$. Because $x_T\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$ does not depend on $\theta$, the trajectory log-density is a sum of step log-densities plus a constant, and the score-function identity gives

\[ \nabla_\theta\mathcal J=\mathbb E\Big[R(x_0,c)\sum_{k=1}^{T}\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x_{k-1}\mid x_k,c)\Big]. \]

This is DDPOSF (score function): plain REINFORCE, valid only on-policy, one gradient step per batch of samples. DDPOIS reuses each batch for several steps by importance sampling each transition with the ratio $\rho_k=p_\theta(x_{k-1}\mid x_k,c)/p_{\text{old}}(x_{k-1}\mid x_k,c)$ and PPO-style clipping. DDPO also normalizes rewards per prompt (subtract the prompt's running mean, divide by its standard deviation), which is the ancestor of GRPO's group normalization.

One iteration

  1. Sample a batch of prompts; for each, run the full stochastic sampler (the DDPO paper used 50 DDIM steps with $\eta=1$, so every step is stochastic) and store every $(x_k,x_{k-1},\log p_{\text{old}})$.
  2. Score each endpoint with the reward; normalize per prompt to get an advantage $A_i$.
  3. For a few inner epochs, recompute $\log p_\theta$ of every stored transition, form $\rho_k$, and maximize $\min(\rho_kA_i,\operatorname{clip}(\rho_k,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A_i)$ summed over all $T$ steps.

Hyperparameters that matter, strengths, failure modes

The knobs are the number of sampler steps (cost is linear in it, since each step is a forward pass at rollout and a forward-backward pass at training), the clip range (DDPO used a very small one, on the order of $10^{-4}$, for the dimension-scaling reason you met in Step 2), and the number of inner epochs. Its strength is generality: any reward, no differentiability, a clean MDP. Its weaknesses are cost (every step is trained), variance (one trajectory-level scalar multiplies $T$ high-dimensional score vectors), and reward over-optimization with no explicit regularizer.

DPOK (Fan et al., 2023, contemporaneous with DDPO and not in the paper's bibliography) uses the same MDP but adds KL regularization to the pretrained model. The KL between two diffusion trajectories' endpoint distributions is intractable, so DPOK upper-bounds it by the sum of per-step KLs between Gaussian transitions, each of which is closed-form ($\lVert\mu_\theta-\mu_{\text{ref}}\rVert^2/2\sigma_k^2$). Flow-GRPO's KL term below is the same construction.

In LLM terms

DDPO is PPO with a terminal outcome reward and no value function, where each "token" is one denoising step and the "vocabulary" is $\mathbb R^D$. Two things differ. The horizon is fixed (always $T$ steps), so there is no length bias. And each action is a continuous Gaussian vector of dimension $D\approx10^5$, so its log-probability is a sum of $D$ terms; that is why the clip range is $10^{-4}$, not $0.2$.

Flow-GRPO: GRPO on a flow model's SDE

Motivation: a flow model has no transition density

Modern image and video generators (SD3, FLUX, HunyuanVideo, Wan) are rectified-flow models sampled with a deterministic ODE: $x_{t-\Delta}=x_t+\Delta\,v_\theta(x_t,t,c)$. A deterministic step has no density, so $\log p_\theta(x_{t-\Delta}\mid x_t)$ is undefined and DDPO cannot be applied. Flow-GRPO (Liu et al., 2025) fixes this in two moves: replace the ODE by an SDE with the same marginals, and replace DDPO's running per-prompt statistics with GRPO's group normalization over $K$ samples of the same prompt.

ODE to SDE, derived

Step 1 showed that for rectified flow the ideal velocity is $v(x_t)=\mathbb E[x_0-\epsilon\mid x_t]$. Since $x_t=(1-t)\mathbb E[x_0\mid x_t]+t\,\mathbb E[\epsilon\mid x_t]$, eliminate $\mathbb E[x_0\mid x_t]$:

\[ v=\frac{x_t-t\hat\epsilon}{1-t}-\hat\epsilon=\frac{x_t-\hat\epsilon}{1-t}\ \Rightarrow\ \hat\epsilon:=\mathbb E[\epsilon\mid x_t]=x_t-(1-t)v,\qquad \nabla\log p_t(x_t)=-\frac{\hat\epsilon}{t}=-\frac{x_t-(1-t)v}{t}. \]

Moving backward in time with step $h>0$, the ODE drift is $v$. Adding Gaussian noise of scale $\sigma_t\sqrt h$ would spread the distribution, so the drift must gain a Langevin correction $\tfrac{\sigma_t^2}{2}\nabla\log p_t$ that pulls it back; the resulting family keeps every marginal $p_t$ unchanged for any choice of $\sigma_t$. Substituting the score and Flow-GRPO's choice $\sigma_t^2=\nu^2 t/(1-t)$ (so $\sigma_t^2/2t=\nu^2/2(1-t)$ and $\sigma_t^2(1-t)/2t=\nu^2/2$) gives exactly the reference transition the paper writes in its appendix:

\[ x_{t-h}\sim\mathcal N\big(\mu(s_t,v),\ \sigma_{\text{ref}}^2(t)I\big),\quad \mu(s_t,v)=x_t+h\Big[\big(1+\tfrac{\nu^2}{2}\big)v-\frac{\nu^2}{2(1-t)}x_t\Big],\quad \sigma_{\text{ref}}^2(t)=\nu^2\frac{t}{1-t}h. \]

$\nu$ is the noise level (0.7 to 0.8 in practice). The term $\nu^2 t/(1-t)$ blows up at $t=1$, so implementations replace $t$ in the denominator by the next grid time on the first step. Now each step has a Gaussian log-density, and the velocity network enters only through the mean.

The objective

The paper's appendix ("Reverse-Process Policy Optimization") writes Flow-GRPO as follows. For reverse trajectories $x^{(i)}_{T:0}\sim P^{\text{rev}}_{\text{old}}(\cdot\mid c)$, $i=1,\dots,K$, compute the group-normalized terminal advantage $A_i=(R_i-\bar R_c)/\operatorname{std}(R)$ and assign it to every transition $k$ of trajectory $i$. Then maximize

\[ \mathcal J_{\text{FG}}=\mathbb E_{i,k}\Big[\min\!\big(\rho_{i,k}A_i,\ \operatorname{clip}(\rho_{i,k},1-\epsilon_{\text{clip}},1+\epsilon_{\text{clip}})A_i\big)-\lambda_{\text{KL}}D_{\text{KL}}\big(p_\theta(\cdot\mid x_k^{(i)},c)\,\Vert\,p_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x_k^{(i)},c)\big)\Big], \]
\[ \rho_{i,k}=\frac{p_\theta(x^{(i)}_{k-1}\mid x^{(i)}_k,c)}{p_{\text{old}}(x^{(i)}_{k-1}\mid x^{(i)}_k,c)}. \]

$\epsilon_{\text{clip}}>0$ is the clip radius, $\lambda_{\text{KL}}\ge0$ weights the penalty and $p_{\text{ref}}$ is a frozen reference transition (usually the pretrained model). Because both transitions share the variance $\sigma_{\text{ref}}^2$, the KL is closed-form: $\lVert\mu_\theta-\mu_{\text{ref}}\rVert^2/2\sigma_{\text{ref}}^2$. The DiffusionART experiments set $\lambda_{\text{KL}}=0$ for every method, so in the comparisons the only trust region is the clip.

One iteration, step by step

  1. Rollout. For each prompt draw $K$ trajectories with the SDE sampler under $\theta_{\text{old}}$ (frozen weights; in practice the current weights before the first update). Store, for every trained step, the state $x_k$, the sampled next state $x_{k-1}$, and $\log p_{\text{old}}$.
  2. Reward. Decode endpoints, score them, and normalize within each prompt group. The repository's image path centers per prompt but divides by the standard deviation of the whole rollout batch (plus $10^{-4}$), in FP64.
  3. Replay. For each stored transition recompute the mean with the current $v_\theta$ and evaluate the log-density of the same stored $x_{k-1}$. This is the "replay" step; you never resample.
  4. Loss. Clipped surrogate per transition, averaged over a fixed denominator, one or more optimizer steps per rollout (the repo's paper configs use two disjoint windows per rollout).

Here is the whole loop at toy scale: a 2D velocity MLP, $K=8$ SDE trajectories of six steps, a reward that prefers endpoints near a target point, group-normalized advantages, and two clipped updates on the same rollout.

Two things in that output are worth a second look. The first update's loss value is exactly zero: every ratio is 1 and the advantages sum to zero within the group, yet the gradient is not zero (it is $\sum_i A_i\nabla\log p_\theta$). Do not monitor this loss as a progress signal. And a single Adam step at learning rate $10^{-2}$ already pushes some ratios to 0.58 and 1.82, so 19% of transitions are clipped on the second update. In real models the per-step log-probability sums over $\sim10^5$ dimensions, which is why the repo instead averages over latent elements and uses ratio_clip: 1e-5 (SD3 Flow-GRPO, Fast and image Dance) or 1e-4 (video DanceGRPO).

Hyperparameters that matter

  • Noise level $\nu$: too small and $\sigma_{\text{ref}}\to0$ makes ratios explode and exploration vanish; too large and the SDE samples degrade. The repo's configs use 0.7 (FLUX Flow-DPPO) and 0.8 (SD3, video).
  • Group size $K$ and prompts per rollout: the SD3 GenEval configs use 48 prompts × 24 samples; the video configs 32 × 8.
  • Training vs. inference steps: the Flow-GRPO paper rolls out with fewer sampler steps during training than at inference ("denoising reduction"); the repo's SD3 configs roll out with 10.
  • Ratio clip and advantage clip (1e-5 and 5 in the repo), $\lambda_{\text{KL}}$ (0 in the paper's runs), and which steps are trained (next).

Variants: Flow-GRPO-Fast, DanceGRPO, Flow-DPPO

Flow-GRPO-Fast. Training every one of $T$ steps costs $T$ forward-backward passes per sample. Fast runs the SDE only inside a short window of steps and the deterministic ODE elsewhere, and trains only the windowed steps. In the repo's SD3 GenEval config the window is 3 contiguous steps drawn from the first 5 of 10 (the high-noise end, where exploration matters most); the video config trains 2 of the first 6 of 16. The paper's image experiments pair Fast with CPS (coefficients-preserving sampling, Wang and Yu 2025), the else branch in the code above: it re-mixes the predicted $\hat x_0=x-t\,v_{\text{sd3}}$ and predicted noise $\hat x_1=x+(1-t)v_{\text{sd3}}$ so that the next state's noise level exactly matches the schedule, and it scores transitions with an unnormalized negative squared error (likelihood: mse) rather than a Gaussian density.

DanceGRPO (Xue et al., 2025) applies the same group-normalized clipped objective with a differently parameterized SDE (the dance branch: constant diffusion $\eta$, drift $v+\tfrac{\eta^2}{2}\nabla\log p_t$), shares the initial noise within a prompt group, trains a random subset of timesteps per sample, and was the first to scale this recipe to video.

Flow-DPPO (Ping et al., 2026) replaces the ratio clip with a KL-based mask: compute the per-transition KL between current and old Gaussians, and drop a sample's surrogate only if the KL exceeds a threshold and the ratio is moving in the direction the advantage wants. DiffusionART borrows this "mask outward drift, keep inward corrections" idea for its trust-region mask in Step 4.

The credit-assignment weakness, made precise

The paper's diagnosis of reverse-process RL is one sentence: its policy gradient "updates each selected transition along its own transition-level sampling noise, weighted by a trajectory-level advantage that this transition only partly determines." You can derive both halves in three lines. A sampled transition is $x_{k-1}=\mu_\theta(x_k)+\sigma_k\xi_k$ with $\xi_k\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$. The derivative of its log-density with respect to the mean, at the sampled point, is

\[ \nabla_{\mu}\log\mathcal N(x_{k-1};\mu,\sigma_k^2I)\Big|_{\mu=\mu_{\text{old}}}=\frac{x_{k-1}-\mu_{\text{old}}}{\sigma_k^2}=\frac{\xi_k}{\sigma_k}. \]

So at the on-policy point the output-space push that trajectory $i$ applies at its step $k$ is $A_i\,\xi_{i,k}/\sigma_k$ (times $h(1+\nu^2/2)$ to convert from mean to velocity). Direction: the noise that happened to be drawn at that step, a random vector in $\mathbb R^D$. Weight: an advantage computed from the endpoint, which depends on all $T$ noise draws, not just this one. Averaged over many samples, only the component of $\xi_{i,k}$ that is correlated with reward survives; per state, most of the push is noise.

A linear-Gaussian toy makes the dilution visible. Let the endpoint be $x_0=\sum_{k=1}^{N}s\,\xi_k$ and the reward $R=\langle x_0,w\rangle$ for a unit vector $w$, which is the true improving direction. Compare the per-step push $\sum_iA_i\xi_{i,k}$ against the endpoint covariance $\sum_iA_i(x_0^{(i)}-\bar x_0)$, which pools all steps before looking at the direction:

With one step the two coincide. With ten steps, each step's own push is about $\sqrt{10}\approx3.2$ times less aligned, because each step's noise explains only a tenth of the reward variance. Be careful with what this toy does and does not show. If every state mapped to the parameters through the same Jacobian, summing the per-step parameter gradients would recover the pooled direction. Real networks map different states through different Jacobians, so each state's update really is driven by its own noisy push. The point to keep: the reward-relevant direction is a property of the endpoints and rewards; reverse-process RL only reaches it indirectly, by averaging likelihood gradients whose per-state directions are mostly sampling noise. DiffusionART computes that direction first and hands it to every state (Step 4).

In LLM terms

Flow-GRPO is GRPO with the sequence-level advantage broadcast to every token, exactly as in LLM GRPO. What carries over: group normalization, the clipped ratio, the off-by-one-update staleness, zero-advantage filtering. What breaks: an LLM token's log-prob gradient pushes toward a single sampled vocabulary item, which carries meaning on its own. Here the "token" is a Gaussian noise draw in $\mathbb R^D$, and its gradient pushes along that draw, which is mostly meaningless for the reward. Also, the rollout sampler must be changed (ODE to SDE) only so that a likelihood exists; the deployed sampler is still the ODE. Imagine having to add temperature noise to greedy decoding only so you can compute a ratio.

Pitfall

The trained SDE policy and the deployed ODE sampler share a velocity network but are different samplers. Improving the SDE's reward improves the ODE's reward only to the extent that the two share their marginals, which holds exactly only for the ideal velocity. Also note which convention your log-prob uses: the repo's is a per-element mean, so a "ratio" of $1+10^{-5}$ already corresponds to a sizeable change in the full-dimensional density.

DiffusionNFT: online RL on the forward process

DiffusionNFT (Zheng et al., 2025) is the baseline DiffusionART is built against, and the paper's entire analysis (§2, §3) is about it. Read this section slowly.

Motivation

Flow-GRPO pays three taxes: it must swap the ODE for an SDE, it must store and replay trajectories with exact log-densities, and its per-state signal is noisy. DiffusionNFT avoids all three by going back to the objective the model was pretrained with. Flow matching trains $v_\theta$ by regressing $u_t=x_0-\epsilon$ on forward-noised states $x_t=(1-t)x_0+t\epsilon$. DiffusionNFT keeps that regression and changes only which $x_0$ it regresses toward and with what weight. The rollout can use any black-box sampler (the repo uses a second-order DPM-Solver for SD3), needs only the clean endpoints and their rewards, and needs no likelihood. The NFT paper reports large efficiency gains over Flow-GRPO, and the DiffusionART paper confirms that NFT with a tuned EMA is the strongest baseline on SD3.5-M.

The goodness weight $r$

NFT maps each raw reward to a number in $[0,1]$, read as "how much this sample belongs to the good set":

\[ r_i=\tfrac12\Big[1+\operatorname{clip}\Big(\frac{R_i-\bar R_c}{Z_c},-1,1\Big)\Big]\in[0,1],\qquad \bar R_c=\frac1K\sum_iR_i,\quad Z_c>0. \]

A sample at the group mean gets $r=\tfrac12$ (neutral). Without clipping, $2r_i-1=(R_i-\bar R_c)/Z_c$ is a centered advantage, so $\sum_i(2r_i-1)=0$. In the repo the advantage is first standardized, $A_i=(R_i-\bar R_c)/(\operatorname{std}_{\text{batch}}+10^{-4})$, clipped to $\pm5$, and then probability = (A/5)/2 + 0.5; that is the formula above with $Z_c=5(\operatorname{std}_{\text{batch}}+10^{-4})$. With a typical standardized advantage of $\pm1$, $r$ lands near 0.4 or 0.6: NFT's weights are mild by default.

Worked example. $K=4$ rewards $R=(0.9,0.5,0.3,0.7)$: $\bar R_c=0.6$, population std $=\sqrt{0.05}=0.2236$, $A=(1.342,-0.447,-1.342,0.447)$, so with $Z_c=5\cdot0.2236$: $r=(0.634,0.455,0.366,0.545)$ and $2r-1=(0.268,-0.089,-0.268,0.089)$, which sums to zero.

Positive and negative endpoint distributions

Reweighting the rollout distribution by $r$ and by $1-r$ splits it into two normalized distributions:

\[ p_+(x_0\mid c)=\frac{r(x_0,c)\,p_{\text{old}}(x_0\mid c)}{Z_+(c)},\quad p_-(x_0\mid c)=\frac{[1-r(x_0,c)]\,p_{\text{old}}(x_0\mid c)}{Z_-(c)},\quad Z_+=\mathbb E_{p_{\text{old}}}[r],\ Z_-=\mathbb E_{p_{\text{old}}}[1-r]. \]

Since $r+(1-r)=1$, the old distribution is exactly the mixture $p_{\text{old}}=Z_+p_++Z_-p_-$ with $Z_++Z_-=1$. The aim of RL is to move $p_{\text{old}}$ toward $p_+$ and away from $p_-$.

Collinearity of the ideal velocities, derived

Every endpoint distribution $P_b$ ($b\in\{+,\text{old},-\}$) induces, through forward noising, a joint law $Q_b$ over $(x_0,\epsilon,x_t,u_t)$ and an ideal forward velocity $v^b_{\text{fwd}}(s_t)=\mathbb E_{Q_b}[u_t\mid s_t]$ at each state $s_t=(x_t,t,c)$. This is the velocity a perfectly trained flow model of $P_b$ would output. Fix a state and define

\[ q:=\mathbb E_{Q_{\text{old}}}[r(x_0,c)\mid s_t],\qquad g:=\operatorname{Cov}_{Q_{\text{old}}}[r(x_0,c),u_t\mid s_t]. \]

$q$ is the posterior mean goodness of the endpoints that could have produced $x_t$; $g$ is how goodness co-varies with the velocity target at that state. The paper's Lemma 1 states $q(v^+_{\text{fwd}}-v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}})=(1-q)(v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}}-v^-_{\text{fwd}})=g$. The proof is short and worth doing yourself:

  1. $P_+$ reweights only $x_0$, and $\epsilon$ is independent of $x_0$, so the whole joint law is reweighted the same way: $Q_+=\frac{r}{Z_+}Q_{\text{old}}$.
  2. Marginalize onto $x_t$: $Q_+(x_t)=\frac{q}{Z_+}Q_{\text{old}}(x_t)$, by the definition of $q$ as the conditional mean of $r$.
  3. Divide (Bayes): $Q_+(\cdot\mid s_t)=\frac{r}{q}\,Q_{\text{old}}(\cdot\mid s_t)$. Likewise $Q_-(\cdot\mid s_t)=\frac{1-r}{1-q}\,Q_{\text{old}}(\cdot\mid s_t)$.
  4. Take expectations of $u_t$: $q\,v^+_{\text{fwd}}=\mathbb E_{\text{old}}[r\,u_t\mid s_t]$ and $(1-q)v^-_{\text{fwd}}=\mathbb E_{\text{old}}[(1-r)u_t\mid s_t]$.
  5. Subtract $q\,v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}}=q\,\mathbb E_{\text{old}}[u_t\mid s_t]$: $q(v^+_{\text{fwd}}-v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}})=\mathbb E[ru_t]-\mathbb E[r]\mathbb E[u_t]=g$. The negative side is identical with $1-r$.

So the three ideal velocities lie on one line through $v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}}$ with direction $g$: the positive one at distance $g/q$ ahead, the negative one at $g/(1-q)$ behind. "Move toward $p_+$" and "move away from $p_-$" are the same instruction with different step sizes. The implicit parameterization and the loss

We would like to train a positive model toward $v^+_{\text{fwd}}$ and a negative model toward $v^-_{\text{fwd}}$, but we only want one network. NFT therefore defines both implicitly from the single trainable $v_\theta$ and the frozen $v_{\text{old}}$, for a scalar $\beta>0$:

\[ v_{\theta,+}=v_{\text{old}}+\beta(v_\theta-v_{\text{old}}),\qquad v_{\theta,-}=v_{\text{old}}-\beta(v_\theta-v_{\text{old}}). \]

They are mirror images around $v_{\text{old}}$. Each is fit to the same flow-matching target $u_t$, weighted by goodness:

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{NFT}}=\mathbb E_{\mathcal D_{\text{old}}}\Big[r\,\lVert v_{\theta,+}-u_t\rVert_2^2+(1-r)\,\lVert v_{\theta,-}-u_t\rVert_2^2\Big], \]

where $\mathcal D_{\text{old}}$ draws $c$, $x_0\sim p_{\text{old}}(\cdot\mid c)$ with its $r$, a time $t$ and fresh noise $\epsilon$. Why mirror images? Minimizing the $r$-weighted term alone at a fixed state sets $v_{\theta,+}=\mathbb E[r\,u_t\mid s_t]/q=v^+_{\text{fwd}}$; minimizing the $(1-r)$-weighted term alone sets $v_{\theta,-}=v^-_{\text{fwd}}$. Collinearity says these two are reachable by one $v_\theta$ moving along $g$. At the joint optimum (if the forward and reverse fields agreed) the network lands at $v_\theta^\ast=v_{\text{old}}+\tfrac{2}{\beta}g$: a step along the reward-tilt direction whose length is set by $1/\beta$.

Equivalence to a single-target MSE, derived in full

The paper's Eq. (1) rewrites the two-branch loss as one regression. Fix one sample and write $d=v_\theta-v_{\text{old}}$ (the drift), $h=u_t-v_{\text{old}}$ (the training residual) and $A=2r-1$. The two residuals are $v_{\theta,+}-u_t=\beta d-h$ and $v_{\theta,-}-u_t=-\beta d-h$. Expand:

\[ \begin{aligned} \ell_{\text{NFT}}&=r\lVert\beta d-h\rVert^2+(1-r)\lVert\beta d+h\rVert^2\\ &=\beta^2\lVert d\rVert^2+\lVert h\rVert^2-2\beta\big[r-(1-r)\big]\langle d,h\rangle\\ &=\beta^2\lVert d\rVert^2-2\beta A\langle d,h\rangle+\lVert h\rVert^2. \end{aligned} \]

Complete the square in $d$: $\beta^2\lVert d\rVert^2-2\beta A\langle d,h\rangle=\beta^2\lVert d-\tfrac{A}{\beta}h\rVert^2-A^2\lVert h\rVert^2$. Hence

\[ \ell_{\text{NFT}}=\beta^2\Big\lVert d-\frac{A}{\beta}h\Big\rVert^2+(1-A^2)\lVert h\rVert^2,\qquad 1-A^2=4r(1-r). \]

Now $d-\tfrac{A}{\beta}h=v_\theta-\big(v_{\text{old}}+\tfrac{A}{\beta}h\big)$. Define $a=(2r-1)/\beta$ and the frozen target

\[ v_{\text{tgt}}=v_{\text{old}}+a\,(u_t-v_{\text{old}})=a\,u_t+(1-a)\,v_{\text{old}}. \]

Take the expectation over the frozen $\mathcal D_{\text{old}}$ (none of $r,u_t,v_{\text{old}}$ depends on $\theta$):

\[ \boxed{\ \mathcal L_{\text{NFT}}=\beta^2\,\mathbb E_{\mathcal D_{\text{old}}}\big[\lVert v_\theta-v_{\text{tgt}}\rVert_2^2\big]+\mathrm{const},\qquad \mathrm{const}=\mathbb E\big[4r(1-r)\lVert u_t-v_{\text{old}}\rVert^2\big].\ } \]

Three readings of this result matter later. First, the target interpolates (or extrapolates) along the line from $v_{\text{old}}$ to the sample's own $u_t$: a good sample ($a>0$) pulls toward its $u_t$, a bad one ($a<0$) pushes away from it, a neutral one ($r=\tfrac12$) pins $v_\theta$ to $v_{\text{old}}$. Second, $v_{\text{old}}$ stays inside the target as an anchor; the paper calls this the old-policy anchor. Third, differentiate the per-sample loss: $-\nabla_{v_\theta}\ell_{\text{NFT}}=2\beta A h-2\beta^2 d$. At the on-policy point $d=0$:

\[ -\nabla_{v_\theta}\ell_{\text{NFT}}\Big|_{v_\theta=v_{\text{old}}}=2\beta\,(2r-1)\,(u_t-v_{\text{old}}). \]

High- and low-reward samples drive the same kind of residual, $u_t-v_{\text{old}}$, in opposite directions. The term $-2\beta^2d$ is a restoring force toward $v_{\text{old}}$ that is zero at the on-policy point; the paper's Proposition 2 writes the loss as exactly these two pieces, $-2\beta(2r-1)\langle d,h\rangle+\beta^2\lVert d\rVert^2$, and Step 4 shows that the second piece is what keeps NFT stable.

What $\beta$ does: a worked example

Take one coordinate with $u_t=1.0$ and $v_{\text{old}}=0.2$, so $h=0.8$.

  • $r=0.9,\ \beta=1$: $a=0.8$, $v_{\text{tgt}}=0.8\cdot1.0+0.2\cdot0.2=0.84$. The initial force is $2\beta(2r-1)h=1.28$.
  • $r=0.1,\ \beta=1$: $a=-0.8$, $v_{\text{tgt}}=-0.8+1.8\cdot0.2=-0.44$, pushing away from $u_t$.
  • $r=0.9,\ \beta=0.1$: $a=8$, $v_{\text{tgt}}=8-7\cdot0.2=6.6$. The target is far beyond $u_t$, but the loss carries a $\beta^2=0.01$ prefactor, so the initial force is only $0.128$.

The force at $d=0$ scales with $\beta$, while the target offset scales with $1/\beta$. With Adam, a uniform rescaling of the loss is largely normalized away, so in practice smaller $\beta$ behaves like a more aggressive target: the equilibrium drift is $d^\ast=(2r-1)h/\beta$. This is why the repo's OCR recipe ($\beta=0.1$) needs a much stronger anchor (EMA retention up to 0.999) than its GenEval recipe ($\beta=1$, retention up to 0.5). One training iteration, step by step

  1. Rollout under $\theta_{\text{old}}$. Sample $K$ endpoints per prompt with a deterministic solver using the old (EMA) weights. Nothing is stored except the endpoints.
  2. Reward and $r$. Score endpoints, standardize within each prompt, convert to $r\in[0,1]$.
  3. Re-noise. For each endpoint pick several times $t$ (the repo uses 9 or 10 per endpoint, chosen at random per row) and draw fresh noise: $x_t=(1-t)x_0+t\epsilon$. These states are generally not the states the sampler visited.
  4. Cache $v_{\text{old}}$ at those states with the old weights, under no-grad.
  5. Update. Minimize $\mathcal L_{\text{NFT}}$ with one or two optimizer steps (two disjoint halves of the rollout in the repo's OCR recipe).
  6. EMA refresh. $\theta_{\text{old}}\leftarrow\eta\,\theta_{\text{old}}+(1-\eta)\theta$, once per rollout.

Pitfall: the implementation is not literally Eq. (1)

Because the two branches get different detached weights $w_\pm$, the branch weights are no longer exactly $r$ and $1-r$. With $\mathsf a=r/w_+$ and $\mathsf b=(1-r)/w_-$ the same completing-the-square argument gives a single-target MSE with effective coefficient $A_{\text{eff}}=(\mathsf a-\mathsf b)/(\mathsf a+\mathsf b)$ in place of $2r-1$. Running the repo's nft_loss on random inputs confirms this to $3\times10^{-16}$; in that run $2r-1=(0.062,-0.224,0.314,-0.365,-1)$ became $A_{\text{eff}}=(0.065,-0.234,0.293,-0.365,-1)$. The structure (one frozen target on the line through $v_{\text{old}}$ and $u_t$) is unchanged, which is all the paper's analysis needs. Exercise 3.5 asks you to derive it.

The EMA rollout policy and why it needs per-task tuning

After each rollout NFT updates the rollout weights by an exponential moving average, $\theta_{\text{old}}\leftarrow\eta\,\theta_{\text{old}}+(1-\eta)\theta$. With $\eta=0$ the rollout policy is the current policy (fully on-policy); with $\eta\to1$ it barely moves (nearly offline). The NFT default ramps $\eta=\min(0.001\,i,0.5)$ with iteration $i$. The repo implements this as decay = min(max(n - delay, 0) * rate, maximum) on the completed-update count:

The schedule is a per-task hyperparameter, and the paper documents the cost. The NFT paper itself reports that fully on-policy updates collapse and nearly offline updates converge impractically slowly, and that its default schedule is unstable on OCR, which needs a maximum retention of 0.999. The repo's SD3 configs make this concrete: GenEval uses rate 0.001, maximum 0.5, β = 1; OCR uses rate 0.0075, maximum 0.999, delay 75, β = 0.1, two updates per rollout; HunyuanVideo uses rate 0.00125, maximum 0.6. When the paper transfers the GenEval-tuned schedule and $\beta$ to FLUX.1-dev without retuning, NFT's training reward fluctuates strongly. On HunyuanVideo, on-policy NFT drives the text-alignment reward to $-4.48$ while EMA-NFT keeps it at 2.10.

A natural explanation is that EMA helps because the rollout data are "off-policy" and change slowly. Step 4 shows, with the paper's ablations, that this is not the mechanism: what matters is that $v_{\text{old}}$ in the target lags behind $v_\theta$, so the $\beta^2\lVert d\rVert^2$ restoring term is active. For now, record the open puzzle the paper opens §3 with.

The puzzle (resolved in Step 4)

Remove EMA so that $v_\theta=v_{\text{old}}$ at every update. Train with only the positive branch ($r\lVert v_{\theta,+}-u_t\rVert^2$), or only the negative branch. By Lemma 1 the two ideal directions are collinear, so either branch alone should improve reward. In the paper's Figure 2(a), consistent with the NFT paper, each branch alone collapses, while the two together learn. What hidden term does each branch carry that the other cancels? Hint: the targets are built from forward-noised endpoints, but $v_{\text{old}}$ is the field the reverse sampler used. Look again at the open circle in the diagram.

Strengths and failure modes

  • Strengths. No likelihoods, any solver, the pretraining loss shape (MSE in velocity), dense supervision (every re-noised state of every sample gets a target), and in the paper's T2I benchmarks, the strongest baseline efficiency when tuned.
  • Failure modes. Collapse without joint branches; collapse without enough restoration (on-policy); slow learning with too much restoration; per-task tuning of $\eta$ schedule and $\beta$; and on I2V, an early plateau the paper attributes to the residual consistency force diluting guidance.

In LLM terms

The positive branch is reward-weighted supervised fine-tuning on your own samples (think rejection-sampling fine-tuning or ReST); the negative branch is unlikelihood training on bad samples. Unlikelihood-only training is famously unstable in LLMs too. The $v_{\text{old}}$ anchor plays the role a KL-to-old-policy term plays in PPO. The sharpest analogy is teacher forcing: forward-process training conditions on states built from the finished sample, not on states the model generated step by step. The forward–reverse gap $e$ is the diffusion version of exposure bias, and Step 4 shows it is exactly what makes single-branch NFT fail.

ReFL: backpropagate through the reward

Motivation and objective

If the reward model is a differentiable network (an aesthetic predictor, ImageReward, a VLM-based video reward), the most direct way to increase it is gradient ascent on the reward with respect to the generator's parameters. ReFL (Reward Feedback Learning), introduced with ImageReward (Xu et al., 2023), does this cheaply: run the sampler without gradients until a late step, take one denoising step with gradients to get a clean estimate $\hat x_0$, decode it, and maximize the reward. Gradients through all $T$ steps would cost $T$ times the activation memory and are numerically fragile; a single late step works because, near $t=0$, one step already determines the image well. The objective is

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{ReFL}}(\theta)=-\,\mathbb E_{c,\ x_t\sim\text{sampler}}\Big[R\big(\operatorname{Dec}(\hat x_0(x_t;\theta)),c\big)\Big],\qquad \hat x_0=x_t-t\,v_{\text{sd3}}=x_t+t\,v_\theta \text{ (paper sign)}, \]

usually combined with the pretraining loss as a regularizer. The paper's comparison run uses $K=1$ sample per prompt, gradients through the frozen reward model and VAE to the final denoising step only, earlier steps detached, no KL and no EMA.

Strengths, failure modes, and why DiffusionART avoids it

ReFL's signal is the richest of all: a full gradient vector per sample, no variance from sampling, so it learns from $K=1$. Its costs are structural. It needs the reward to be differentiable, which rules out GenEval's detector pipeline, OCR string matching, external VLM APIs, human or pairwise judgments and any rule-based check. It needs memory to backpropagate through a VAE decoder and a reward network, which for video is substantial. And it optimizes the reward model's gradient field directly, which is the easiest path to adversarial reward hacking. DiffusionART's design goal is to keep RL's "reward values only" contract while getting a per-state direction like ReFL's; in the paper's matched HunyuanVideo comparison, DiffusionART improves both rewards with fewer optimizer updates while using reward values alone.

In LLM terms

There is no LLM counterpart because text is discrete: you cannot backpropagate a reward model's score through a sampled token. ReFL exists only because images live in a continuous space. The closest LLM idea is a straight-through or Gumbel-softmax relaxation, which LLM RL abandoned for the same reasons (bias, hacking, memory).

Diffusion-DPO: offline preferences

Diffusion-DPO (Wallace et al., 2023) ports DPO to diffusion models. It starts from a fixed dataset of preference triples $(c,x_0^w,x_0^l)$ (winner, loser), such as Pick-a-Pic. DPO needs $\log p_\theta(x_0\mid c)$, which a diffusion model does not provide. Diffusion-DPO replaces it with the trajectory likelihood, bounds it with Jensen's inequality, and approximates the reverse posterior over trajectories with the forward noising process. Each step's log-ratio then becomes a difference of denoising errors. Written in this tutorial's velocity notation (Wallace et al. use $\epsilon$-prediction; the structure is identical):

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{D-DPO}}=-\,\mathbb E\,\log\sigma\Big(-\beta_{\text{D}}\big[\big(\lVert u^w_t-v_\theta(x^w_t)\rVert^2-\lVert u^w_t-v_{\text{ref}}(x^w_t)\rVert^2\big)-\big(\lVert u^l_t-v_\theta(x^l_t)\rVert^2-\lVert u^l_t-v_{\text{ref}}(x^l_t)\rVert^2\big)\big]\Big), \]

with $x^{w}_t,x^{l}_t$ the forward-noised winner and loser at a shared random $t$, $\sigma$ the logistic function, and $\beta_{\text{D}}$ absorbing DPO's $\beta$ and the time weighting. In words: lower the winner's denoising error relative to the reference model more than you lower the loser's.

It is offline because the pairs come from a fixed dataset generated by other models (or humans), never from the policy being trained, so nothing tells you how the current model's own samples fare. Online RL samples from the evolving generator every iteration, which is what lets it find and fix the current model's failure modes and use arbitrary black-box rewards. Notice also that Diffusion-DPO is a forward-process method: like NFT, it trains on forward-noised clean samples with an anchor model ($v_{\text{ref}}$ instead of $v_{\text{old}}$). DiffusionART-Pairwise (Step 4) shows how pairwise labels can instead feed an online method.

In LLM terms

This is exactly DPO, with "log-likelihood of the response" replaced by "negative denoising error of the noised image." Everything you know about offline DPO carries over: no exploration, distribution shift between the preference data and the policy, and sensitivity to $\beta$.

What is left unsolved

The paper's introduction ends its survey with "Neither family is thus both efficient and stable without per-task tuning." You can now unpack that sentence into three concrete gaps, each of which one component of DiffusionART closes in Step 4.

  1. Reverse-process RL has the right states but the wrong signal. Flow-GRPO trains on states the sampler actually visits, which is what you want, but each state's update is a scalar advantage times its own random noise draw, through a likelihood that exists only because the sampler was made stochastic. In the paper's SD3.5-M runs, DiffusionART reaches Flow-GRPO's step-500 reward in 10.0×, 5.2× and 9.6× fewer steps on GenEval, OCR and PickScore. Question for Step 4: can each visited state receive an explicit reward-improving direction instead of a scalar passed through a likelihood ratio?
  2. Forward-process RL has the right signal shape but the wrong states. NFT's regression target is a direction in velocity space, which is efficient, but it is built from forward-noised endpoints, implicitly assuming the sampler's field equals the forward field. That assumption hides a force that each branch carries alone and that joint training cancels only on average across samples. Question: can the target be built so that this force is absent by construction, for every state?
  3. Stability comes from a knob that must be retuned. NFT is stable only with an EMA schedule (and a $\beta$) tuned per task: 0.5 retention for GenEval, 0.999 for OCR, untransferable to FLUX. Question: what does EMA actually provide, and can it be replaced by something that adapts to each state's drift automatically?

DiffusionART's answers are aggregation-first covariance guidance $G(c)$ applied to reverse-rollout states, a second MSE update whose restoring force is proportional to each state's drift, and a trust-region mask borrowed in spirit from Flow-GRPO's SDE and Flow-DPPO's mask. Every piece reuses something from this chapter.

Exercises

Exercise 3.1 — Derive NFT's single-target form. Starting from $\ell=r\lVert v_{\theta,+}-u_t\rVert^2+(1-r)\lVert v_{\theta,-}-u_t\rVert^2$ with $v_{\theta,\pm}=v_{\text{old}}\pm\beta(v_\theta-v_{\text{old}})$, show $\ell=\beta^2\lVert v_\theta-v_{\text{tgt}}\rVert^2+4r(1-r)\lVert u_t-v_{\text{old}}\rVert^2$ with $v_{\text{tgt}}=au_t+(1-a)v_{\text{old}}$, $a=(2r-1)/\beta$.

Let $d=v_\theta-v_{\text{old}}$, $h=u_t-v_{\text{old}}$, $A=2r-1$. Then $v_{\theta,+}-u_t=\beta d-h$ and $v_{\theta,-}-u_t=-\beta d-h$. Expanding,

\[ \ell=r(\beta^2\lVert d\rVert^2-2\beta\langle d,h\rangle+\lVert h\rVert^2)+(1-r)(\beta^2\lVert d\rVert^2+2\beta\langle d,h\rangle+\lVert h\rVert^2)=\beta^2\lVert d\rVert^2-2\beta A\langle d,h\rangle+\lVert h\rVert^2. \]

Completing the square, $\beta^2\lVert d\rVert^2-2\beta A\langle d,h\rangle=\beta^2\lVert d-\tfrac A\beta h\rVert^2-A^2\lVert h\rVert^2$, so $\ell=\beta^2\lVert d-\tfrac A\beta h\rVert^2+(1-A^2)\lVert h\rVert^2$. Since $d-\tfrac A\beta h=v_\theta-(v_{\text{old}}+a h)=v_\theta-(au_t+(1-a)v_{\text{old}})$ and $1-A^2=(1-A)(1+A)=(2-2r)(2r)=4r(1-r)$, the claim follows. The second term does not depend on $\theta$ because $r$, $u_t$ and $v_{\text{old}}$ are frozen.

Exercise 3.2 — On-policy descent direction. (a) Show $-\nabla_{v_\theta}\ell|_{v_\theta=v_{\text{old}}}=2\beta(2r-1)(u_t-v_{\text{old}})$. (b) Suppose all $K$ samples of a prompt shared the same residual $h$ at some state. What is the summed force, and what does that say about which part of $h$ NFT learns from?

(a) From $\ell=\beta^2\lVert d\rVert^2-2\beta A\langle d,h\rangle+\lVert h\rVert^2$, $\nabla_{v_\theta}\ell=\nabla_d\ell=2\beta^2d-2\beta Ah$. At $d=0$ the negative gradient is $2\beta Ah=2\beta(2r-1)(u_t-v_{\text{old}})$.

(b) The summed force is $2\beta h\sum_i(2r_i-1)$, which is zero without clipping because $\sum_i(2r_i-1)=0$. So the part of the residual that is common to all samples cancels, and only the part that co-varies with reward survives: writing $h_i=\bar h+(h_i-\bar h)$, the sum is $2\beta\sum_i(2r_i-1)(h_i-\bar h)$, a covariance between goodness and residual. This is the per-sample version of Lemma 1's $g$. The cancellation is exact only across samples; Step 4 shows what happens to a term that is not common to all samples at a given state.

Exercise 3.3 — GRPO by hand. A group has rewards $R=(0.9,0.5,0.3,0.7)$; use population std and $\epsilon_{\text{clip}}=0.2$. Compute the clipped surrogate for (i) a transition of sample 1 with $\rho=1.3$, (ii) a transition of sample 3 with $\rho=0.7$, (iii) a transition of sample 3 with $\rho=1.3$. Which of the three passes a gradient?

$\bar R=0.6$, std $=\sqrt{(0.09+0.01+0.09+0.01)/4}=\sqrt{0.05}=0.2236$, so $A=(1.342,-0.447,-1.342,0.447)$.

  • (i) $A=1.342$, $\rho=1.3$: $\min(1.3\cdot1.342,\ 1.2\cdot1.342)=\min(1.745,1.610)=1.610$. The clipped branch is active, so the gradient is zero: the transition has already become more likely than the trust region allows.
  • (ii) $A=-1.342$, $\rho=0.7$: $\min(-0.939,\ 0.8\cdot(-1.342)=-1.074)=-1.074$. Clipped again; zero gradient. The bad transition has already been made less likely enough.
  • (iii) $A=-1.342$, $\rho=1.3$: $\min(-1.745,\ -1.610)=-1.745$. The unclipped branch is the minimum, so the gradient flows and pushes $\rho$ down. PPO never clips a move in the wrong direction.

Only (iii) passes a gradient. In the repo's loss form, these values are negated: $\max(-A\rho,-A\operatorname{clip}(\rho))$.

Exercise 3.4 — The Gaussian transition. (a) For $x'=\mu_{\text{old}}+\sigma\xi$, show $\nabla_\mu\log\mathcal N(x';\mu,\sigma^2I)|_{\mu=\mu_{\text{old}}}=\xi/\sigma$. (b) Using the paper's reference transition, show the per-step KL between current and old policy is $\frac{h(1+\nu^2/2)^2(1-t)}{2\nu^2t}\lVert v_\theta-v_{\text{old}}\rVert^2$. How does the same velocity change cost in KL near $t=1$ versus near $t=0$?

(a) $\log\mathcal N=-\lVert x'-\mu\rVert^2/2\sigma^2+\text{const}$, so $\nabla_\mu=(x'-\mu)/\sigma^2$. At $\mu=\mu_{\text{old}}$, $x'-\mu=\sigma\xi$, giving $\xi/\sigma$. The update direction is the sampled noise.

(b) Both transitions have variance $\sigma^2_{\text{ref}}=\nu^2th/(1-t)$, and their means differ by $h(1+\nu^2/2)(v_\theta-v_{\text{old}})$ (the $x_t$ term cancels). The KL between equal-variance Gaussians is $\lVert\Delta\mu\rVert^2/2\sigma^2$:

\[ D_{\text{KL}}=\frac{h^2(1+\nu^2/2)^2\lVert v_\theta-v_{\text{old}}\rVert^2}{2\nu^2th/(1-t)}=\frac{h(1+\nu^2/2)^2(1-t)}{2\nu^2t}\lVert v_\theta-v_{\text{old}}\rVert^2. \]

The factor $(1-t)/t$ is small near $t=1$ (lots of injected noise, so a velocity change is barely detectable) and grows without bound as $t\to0$ (almost no noise, so the same change makes the observed next state very unlikely). A fixed ratio clip therefore constrains late steps much more tightly than early ones. DiffusionART's KL proxy in Step 4 is this exact quantity divided by $D$.

Exercise 3.5 — The repo's per-branch weights. The repo divides each branch's squared residual by its own detached weight $w_\pm>0$: $\ell=r\lVert\beta d-h\rVert^2/w_++(1-r)\lVert\beta d+h\rVert^2/w_-$ (overall constants dropped). Show that this is still a single-target MSE and find the target.

Let $\mathsf a=r/w_+$ and $\mathsf b=(1-r)/w_-$. Expanding as in Exercise 3.1,

\[ \ell=(\mathsf a+\mathsf b)\beta^2\lVert d\rVert^2-2\beta(\mathsf a-\mathsf b)\langle d,h\rangle+(\mathsf a+\mathsf b)\lVert h\rVert^2=(\mathsf a+\mathsf b)\beta^2\Big\lVert d-\frac{A_{\text{eff}}}{\beta}h\Big\rVert^2+\text{const}, \]

with $A_{\text{eff}}=(\mathsf a-\mathsf b)/(\mathsf a+\mathsf b)\in[-1,1]$. The target is $v_{\text{old}}+\tfrac{A_{\text{eff}}}{\beta}(u_t-v_{\text{old}})$: the same line, with $2r-1$ replaced by $A_{\text{eff}}$, and a per-sample loss weight $(\mathsf a+\mathsf b)\beta^2$. When $w_+=w_-$ you recover $A_{\text{eff}}=2r-1$. The check script tutorial/scratch/step3/repo_nft_check.py runs the repo's nft_loss and matches this gradient to $3.3\times10^{-16}$.

Takeaways

  • Reverse-process methods (DDPO, Flow-GRPO and its variants) treat each denoising step as a Gaussian action, need a stochastic sampler only to have a likelihood, and push each visited state along its own sampled noise, scaled by a trajectory-level advantage.
  • Flow-GRPO's SDE is the ODE plus a Langevin correction $\tfrac{\sigma_t^2}{2}\nabla\log p_t$; its objective is GRPO's clipped surrogate per transition, with an optional closed-form per-step KL. Fast, Dance and DPPO change which steps are stochastic, how the SDE is parameterized, and how the trust region is enforced.
  • DiffusionNFT trains on forward-noised endpoints. Its positive, old and negative ideal velocities are collinear along $g=\operatorname{Cov}(r,u_t\mid s_t)$, and its loss equals $\beta^2\lVert v_\theta-v_{\text{tgt}}\rVert^2$ with $v_{\text{tgt}}=v_{\text{old}}+\tfrac{2r-1}{\beta}(u_t-v_{\text{old}})$, whose on-policy force is $2\beta(2r-1)(u_t-v_{\text{old}})$.
  • NFT is efficient but stable only with both branches and a lagged EMA rollout policy whose schedule (and $\beta$) must be tuned per task. Why each branch alone collapses is the puzzle Step 4 resolves.
  • ReFL needs reward gradients; Diffusion-DPO needs a fixed preference dataset. DiffusionART keeps online RL's "reward values only" contract.

The DiffusionART paper, section by section

After this step you can derive every equation in the paper, explain why NFT-style training needs both of its stabilizers and why DiffusionART needs neither, write the algorithm from memory, and judge which experimental claims are strongly supported and which are not.

The paper in one paragraph

Online diffusion RL has two families. Reverse-process methods such as Flow-GRPO treat each denoising step as an action, convert the ODE sampler into an SDE so that the step has a log-probability, and push every step of a trajectory by that trajectory's scalar advantage. They train on the states the sampler actually visits, but credit assignment is poor: each step is nudged along its own sampled noise, weighted by an advantage it only partly caused. Forward-process methods such as DiffusionNFT re-noise the finished samples and regress velocities toward reward-weighted targets. They need no likelihoods and learn fast, but they are unstable unless you train positive and negative branches jointly and roll out from a carefully tuned EMA copy of the policy. DiffusionART (Diffusion Reinforcement with Aggregation-First Reward Tilting) first explains both stabilizers. Joint training partially cancels a hidden forward–reverse consistency force; the EMA works through restoration toward a lagged reference, not through off-policy data. It then removes the consistency force by construction. It collapses each prompt's $K$ samples and rewards into one normalized direction $G(c)$, the empirical reward–endpoint covariance, and regresses every visited reverse-process state toward its cached velocity plus $G(c)$. It replaces the EMA with a second minibatch update whose restoration grows with drift, gated by a trust-region mask built from a KL proxy.

The abstract's headline claim, stated exactly: on HunyuanVideo-13B with equally weighted visual and motion quality rewards, DiffusionART reaches the late-stage mean reward levels of DiffusionNFT and Flow-GRPO-Fast with 12.02× and 11.72× fewer GPU hours, respectively, while achieving 28.8% and 53.0% higher mean training rewards. The contributions box adds that DiffusionART matches the strongest baseline's efficiency on SD3.5-M without EMA or per-task tuning, and that on image-to-video it reaches MQ 6 with 4.8× fewer GPU hours than Flow-GRPO-Fast. The paper also claims that DiffusionART needs no differentiable reward model, works with fewer samples per prompt, and supports pairwise reward models (DiffusionART-Pairwise).

The paper has four contributions, and this chapter follows them in order:

  1. Diagnosis 1 (§3.1): the NFT objective contains a consistency force that joint positive–negative training cancels only across samples, not at each state.
  2. Diagnosis 2 (§3.2): EMA stabilizes NFT through restoration, and its strength must be tuned per task.
  3. Method (§4): aggregation-first guidance (§4.1), two-update restoration (§4.2), a trust-region mask (§4.3), and a pairwise variant (§4.4).
  4. Evidence (§5): SD3.5-M, FLUX.1-dev, HunyuanVideo-13B and Wan2.1-I2V-14B.

From LLM RL

In GRPO, a sequence's scalar advantage $A_i$ multiplies the gradient of the log-probability of the tokens that sequence actually sampled. Bad samples have their own tokens pushed down. DiffusionART has no per-sample sign. Every state on every trajectory of prompt $c$, including the worst sample's trajectory, is pulled in the same reward-improving direction $G(c)$. The group is still the unit of comparison, as in GRPO, but the group's output is a vector in sample space rather than $K$ scalars. Nothing in LLM RL quite matches this, because you cannot add a vector to a discrete token. The closest analogy is distillation toward a reward-tilted target policy rather than policy-gradient ascent.

§3.1 The puzzle: each NFT branch alone collapses

Setup and symbols

Recall from Step 3 that DiffusionNFT samples $K$ endpoints per prompt from the frozen rollout policy $p_{\text{old}}(x_0\mid c)$ and maps each raw reward $R_i$ to a goodness

\[ r_i=\tfrac12\Bigl[1+\operatorname{clip}\Bigl(\tfrac{R_i-\bar R_c}{Z_c},-1,1\Bigr)\Bigr]\in[0,1], \]

where $\bar R_c$ is the prompt's mean reward and $Z_c>0$ a scale. Without clipping, $\sum_i(2r_i-1)=0$. Weighting $p_{\text{old}}$ by $r$ and by $1-r$ defines a positive and a negative endpoint distribution, $p_+\propto r\,p_{\text{old}}$ and $p_-\propto(1-r)\,p_{\text{old}}$. NFT builds $v_{\theta,\pm}=v_{\text{old}}\pm\beta(v_\theta-v_{\text{old}})$ and minimizes

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{NFT}}=\mathbb E_{\mathcal D_{\text{old}}}\bigl[r\lVert v_{\theta,+}-u_t\rVert^2+(1-r)\lVert v_{\theta,-}-u_t\rVert^2\bigr]=\beta^2\,\mathbb E\bigl[\lVert v_\theta-v_{\text{tgt}}\rVert^2\bigr]+\text{const},\qquad v_{\text{tgt}}=a\,u_t+(1-a)\,v_{\text{old}},\ a=\tfrac{2r-1}{\beta}. \]

$\mathcal D_{\text{old}}$ is the training distribution of $(c,x_0,r,t,\epsilon)$ with $x_0\sim p_{\text{old}}$ and $\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$. The states NFT trains on are forward-noised endpoints, $x_t=(1-t)x_0+t\epsilon$, not states the sampler visited.

The paper starts from an experiment. It removes EMA, so $v_\theta=v_{\text{old}}$ at every update, and trains SD3.5-M on GenEval with only the positive branch, only the negative branch, or both. Figure 2(a) of the paper shows the outcome. Both single-branch runs fall from about 0.2 to roughly zero GenEval within the first handful of steps and stay there. The joint run climbs to about 0.9 by step 100. This is puzzling, because DiffusionNFT is motivated by the fact that the positive, original and negative ideal velocity fields are collinear. If they all point the same way, either branch alone should suffice.

To make "ideal" precise, the paper introduces, for $b\in\{+,\text{old},-\}$:

  • $P_b$: the endpoint distribution $p_b(x_0\mid c)$.
  • $Q_b$: the joint forward law of $(x_0,\epsilon,x_t,u_t)$, obtained by drawing $x_0\sim P_b$ and $\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$ independently, then setting $x_t=(1-t)x_0+t\epsilon$ and $u_t=x_0-\epsilon$.
  • $s_t=(x_t,t,c)$: a state.
  • $v^b_{\text{fwd}}(s_t):=\mathbb E_{Q_b}[u_t\mid s_t]$: the ideal forward velocity induced by $P_b$. This is the field that flow matching would learn if trained to convergence on samples of $P_b$ (Step 1).
  • $v^{\text{old}}_{\text{rvs}}:=v_{\text{old}}$: the field the reverse sampler actually integrates to produce $P_{\text{old}}$.

If the model were a perfect flow-matching fit of its own samples, $v^{\text{old}}_{\text{rvs}}=v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}}$. The paper calls this forward–reverse compatibility. After even one RL update, nothing enforces it.

Lemma 1: ideal NFT guidance is a conditional covariance

Fix a state $s_t$. Define the statewise expected goodness and the statewise reward–velocity covariance

\[ q:=\mathbb E_{Q_{\text{old}}}[r(x_0,c)\mid s_t],\qquad g:=\operatorname{Cov}_{Q_{\text{old}}}[r(x_0,c),u_t\mid s_t]. \]

$q$ answers the question "given what I can see in this noisy state, how good do I expect the endpoint to be?" $g$ is the direction in velocity space along which good endpoints differ from bad ones, among endpoints consistent with this state. Lemma 1 says that, if $0<q<1$,

\[ q\bigl(v^+_{\text{fwd}}-v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}}\bigr)=(1-q)\bigl(v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}}-v^-_{\text{fwd}}\bigr)=g. \]

So the step from the old field to the positive field and the step from the negative field to the old field both point along $g$; they differ only in length ($g/q$ versus $g/(1-q)$).

Proof, step by step (paper, Appendix B, "Conditional Covariance Identity for Ideal NFT").

  1. Reweighting the endpoint reweights the whole forward tuple. $P_+$ is $P_{\text{old}}$ reweighted by $r(x_0,c)/Z_+(c)$ with $Z_+=\mathbb E_{P_{\text{old}}}[r\mid c]$. Because $\epsilon$ is drawn independently of $x_0$ and $(x_t,u_t)$ are deterministic functions of $(x_0,\epsilon)$, the same factor reweights the joint law: $Q_+(\cdot\mid t,c)=\tfrac{r(x_0,c)}{Z_+(c)}\,Q_{\text{old}}(\cdot\mid t,c)$. Likewise $Q_-=\tfrac{1-r}{Z_-}Q_{\text{old}}$ with $Z_-=\mathbb E_{P_{\text{old}}}[1-r\mid c]$.
  2. Marginalize onto $x_t$. Integrating the reweighting factor over everything except $x_t$ replaces $r(x_0,c)$ by its conditional mean given $x_t$, which is $q$: $Q_+(x_t\mid t,c)=\tfrac{q}{Z_+}Q_{\text{old}}(x_t\mid t,c)$ and $Q_-(x_t\mid t,c)=\tfrac{1-q}{Z_-}Q_{\text{old}}(x_t\mid t,c)$.
  3. Bayes' rule. Divide step 1 by step 2. The normalizers $Z_\pm$ cancel, and the posterior over endpoints given the state becomes $Q_+(\cdot\mid s_t)=\tfrac{r}{q}Q_{\text{old}}(\cdot\mid s_t)$ and $Q_-(\cdot\mid s_t)=\tfrac{1-r}{1-q}Q_{\text{old}}(\cdot\mid s_t)$. In words: among the endpoints compatible with $s_t$, the positive distribution up-weights each one by its goodness relative to the local average goodness.
  4. Take conditional expectations of $u_t$. $q\,v^+_{\text{fwd}}=\mathbb E_{Q_{\text{old}}}[r\,u_t\mid s_t]$ and $(1-q)\,v^-_{\text{fwd}}=\mathbb E_{Q_{\text{old}}}[(1-r)\,u_t\mid s_t]$.
  5. Subtract $v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}}=\mathbb E_{Q_{\text{old}}}[u_t\mid s_t]$. $q(v^+_{\text{fwd}}-v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}})=\mathbb E[r u_t\mid s_t]-\mathbb E[r\mid s_t]\,\mathbb E[u_t\mid s_t]=g$. The negative side is the same algebra with $1-r$, and $\operatorname{Cov}(1-r,u_t)=-g$, which gives $(1-q)(v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}}-v^-_{\text{fwd}})=g$. ∎

The forward–reverse gap and Proposition 1

Define the gap at a state as

\[ e:=v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}}(s_t)-v^{\text{old}}_{\text{rvs}}(s_t). \]

$e$ measures how far the sampler's velocity is from the velocity that flow matching on the sampler's own outputs would produce. Proposition 1 computes the NFT force at the pure on-policy point $v_\theta=v^{\text{old}}_{\text{rvs}}$. Here "force" means the negative output-space gradient, normalized by $2\beta$:

\[ \mathcal F_{\text{NFT}}(s_t):=-\frac{1}{2\beta}\nabla_{v_\theta(s_t)}\mathcal L_{\text{NFT}}(s_t)\Big|_{v_\theta=v^{\text{old}}_{\text{rvs}}}=\underbrace{2g}_{\text{reward-improving}}+\underbrace{(2q-1)\,e}_{\text{consistency force}}. \]

Proof, step by step (Appendix B, "NFT Force and Restoration Decompositions"). Two short computations are needed.

(i) The MSE form and the per-sample force. Write $d=v_\theta-v_{\text{old}}$ (drift), $h=u_t-v_{\text{old}}$ (training residual) and $A=2r-1$. The positive residual is $v_{\theta,+}-u_t=\beta d-h$ and the negative residual is $-\beta d-h$. Expanding:

\[ \ell_{\text{NFT}}=r\lVert\beta d-h\rVert^2+(1-r)\lVert-\beta d-h\rVert^2=\beta^2\lVert d\rVert^2-2\beta A\langle d,h\rangle+\lVert h\rVert^2=\beta^2\Bigl\lVert d-\tfrac{A}{\beta}h\Bigr\rVert^2+(1-A^2)\lVert h\rVert^2 . \]

The cross terms $\pm2\beta\langle d,h\rangle$ carry weights $r$ and $1-r$, so they combine into $-2\beta(2r-1)\langle d,h\rangle$. Completing the square gives the last form. Since $v_{\text{old}}+(A/\beta)h=v_{\text{tgt}}$, the first term is $\beta^2\lVert v_\theta-v_{\text{tgt}}\rVert^2$, and $(1-A^2)\lVert h\rVert^2=4r(1-r)\lVert h\rVert^2$ does not depend on $\theta$. Differentiating in $v_\theta$ gives $-\nabla_{v_\theta}\ell=2\beta A h-2\beta^2 d$. At $d=0$ the per-sample descent direction is $2\beta(2r-1)(u_t-v_{\text{old}})$. High- and low-reward samples drive the same residual in opposite directions.

(ii) Condition on the state. Now fix $s_t$ and average over all $(x_0,\epsilon)$ compatible with it under $Q_{\text{old}}$. Since $h=u_t-v^{\text{old}}_{\text{rvs}}(s_t)$, and $v^{\text{old}}_{\text{rvs}}(s_t)$ is a constant once $s_t$ is fixed:

\[ \mathcal F_{\text{NFT}}(s_t)=\mathbb E[(2r-1)h\mid s_t]=2\operatorname{Cov}(r,h\mid s_t)+\bigl(2\,\mathbb E[r\mid s_t]-1\bigr)\,\mathbb E[h\mid s_t]. \]

This is the identity $\mathbb E[XY]=\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)+\mathbb E[X]\mathbb E[Y]$ with $X=2r-1$ and $Y=h$. Subtracting a constant does not change a covariance, so $\operatorname{Cov}(r,h\mid s_t)=\operatorname{Cov}(r,u_t\mid s_t)=g$. The mean residual is $\mathbb E[h\mid s_t]=v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}}-v^{\text{old}}_{\text{rvs}}=e$, and $\mathbb E[r\mid s_t]=q$. Substituting gives $2g+(2q-1)e$. ∎

Each branch alone, and why both are needed

Run the same computation with only the positive term $r\lVert\beta d-h\rVert^2$ and only the negative term $(1-r)\lVert-\beta d-h\rVert^2$. At $d=0$, the normalized forces are $\mathcal F_+=\mathbb E[r\,h\mid s_t]$ and $\mathcal F_-=-\mathbb E[(1-r)\,h\mid s_t]$ (Exercise 4.4 does the algebra), which work out to

\[ \mathcal F_+=g+q\,e=q\,\bigl(v^+_{\text{fwd}}-v^{\text{old}}_{\text{rvs}}\bigr),\qquad \mathcal F_-=g-(1-q)\,e=(1-q)\,\bigl(v^{\text{old}}_{\text{rvs}}-v^-_{\text{fwd}}\bigr), \]

and $\mathcal F_++\mathcal F_-=\mathcal F_{\text{NFT}}$. The second form of each follows from Lemma 1: $q\,v^+_{\text{fwd}}=q\,v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}}+g$. The geometry is now clear. The positive branch pulls the sampler's velocity toward $v^+_{\text{fwd}}$, and so toward $v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}}$ with weight $q$. The negative branch pushes it away from $v^-_{\text{fwd}}$, and so away from $v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}}$ with weight $1-q$. Along $g$ the two reinforce each other. Along $e$ they oppose each other. A single branch therefore carries an uncancelled $qe$ or $-(1-q)e$, a force that has nothing to do with reward.

Why joint training is not enough

Joint training leaves the residual $(2q-1)e$. It vanishes at a state only if $q=1/2$ at that state. Prompt-level centering guarantees something weaker: without clipping, $\sum_i(2r_i-1)=0$ across the $K$ samples. That balances rewards across samples. It does not balance them per state, and it does not imply $\sum_i(2r_i-1)e_i\approx0$, where $e_i$ is the one-sample Monte Carlo estimate of $e$. The time dependence is the key:

  • At $t=1$ the state is pure noise and reveals nothing about $x_0$, so $q=\mathbb E[r]=1/2$ (without clipping) and the residual vanishes.
  • As $t\to0$ the state reveals more and more of the endpoint. $q$ moves toward 0 or 1, and the weight $\lvert 2q-1\rvert$ on the gap grows.

States with $q>1/2$ are systematically pulled toward $v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}}$; states with $q<1/2$ are pushed away. The residual dilutes the reward-improving guidance in the aggregate gradient, and something else must hold training together. That something is the EMA.

Worked example. The toy later in this chapter computes $q$, $g$ and $e$ exactly (with a kernel posterior) for a 2D flow model. At initialization it measures a mean $\lvert2q-1\rvert$ of 0.18 for $t\ge0.7$ and 0.75 for $t\le0.4$, which matches the time dependence above. The RMS of $2g$ is 0.934 and the RMS of $(2q-1)e$ is 0.172, so at the start the reward term dominates. During on-policy NFT training with $K=2$, the residual grows to 1.250 while $2g$ is 0.602 (iteration 100), then to 29.3 against 0.825 (iteration 150). The gradient norm reaches $1.17\times10^3$ at that point, and the run later diverges.

Key idea

NFT's target is built from forward-noised endpoints, so it implicitly assumes $v_{\text{rvs}}=v_{\text{fwd}}$. The gap $e$ enters the force weighted by the statewise reward imbalance $2q-1$. Joint training cancels it only on average across samples. The paper's design principle is to remove the force statewise rather than rely on cross-sample cancellation (Insight 1).

§3.2 Off-policy EMA: stability through restoration

Even with joint training, pure on-policy NFT collapses after a sharp rise in its pre-clipping gradient norm. In the paper's Figure 3(a), on OCR, the one-update run reaches OCR 0.9 at step 21, then collapses, and its gradient norm (dashed) spikes as the reward falls. DiffusionNFT uses an EMA rollout policy, $\theta_{\text{old}}\leftarrow\eta\,\theta_{\text{old}}+(1-\eta)\theta$. In this section, and only here, $\eta$ is the EMA decay, not the tilt strength. The EMA could stabilize training through two different mechanisms:

  1. a slower-moving rollout distribution (off-policy data), or
  2. the lagged reference $v_{\text{old}}$ that stays inside $v_{\text{tgt}}$ in the loss.

Proposition 2: the loss splits into guidance plus restoration

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{NFT}}=\mathbb E_{\mathcal D_{\text{old}}}\Bigl[-2\beta(2r-1)\langle d,h\rangle+\beta^2\lVert d\rVert^2\Bigr]+\text{const},\qquad d=v_\theta-v_{\text{old}},\ h=u_t-v_{\text{old}}. \]

Proof. It is line two of the expansion in (i) above, $\beta^2\lVert d\rVert^2-2\beta(2r-1)\langle d,h\rangle+\lVert h\rVert^2$. Take the expectation and absorb $\lVert h\rVert^2$, which does not depend on $\theta$, into the constant. ∎

The linear term carries the reward guidance and the consistency force (its on-policy gradient is exactly $\mathcal F_{\text{NFT}}$). The quadratic term $\beta^2\lVert d\rVert^2$ pulls $v_\theta$ back toward $v_{\text{old}}$. Its gradient, $2\beta^2 d$, is zero at $d=0$. Pure on-policy NFT therefore has no restoration at all, and the residual consistency force acts unopposed. An EMA keeps $\theta_{\text{old}}$ lagging behind $\theta$, so $d\ne0$ and restoration is active.

From LLM RL

This is the role PPO's ratio clipping and KL penalty play, seen from a different side. A quadratic penalty toward the behavior policy (here $\beta^2\lVert v_\theta-v_{\text{old}}\rVert^2$) is the Gaussian analogue of a KL to the rollout policy. The difference is that NFT gets the penalty only when the rollout policy lags. With fully on-policy updates and one gradient step, the penalty is exactly zero, much as the PPO clip never activates on the first minibatch of an epoch.

Two ablations that separate the mechanisms

  • EMA rollouts, no anchor. Keep EMA-policy rollouts but replace $v_{\text{old}}$ by $v_\theta$ (stop-gradient) inside $v_{\text{tgt}}$. Off-policy data remains, but restoration is gone. In Figure 3(b), on GenEval, this run tracks NFT (EMA) for about 70 steps and then collapses to zero. Off-policy data alone does not stabilize NFT.
  • No EMA, two updates. Split each rollout batch into two disjoint minibatches and update once on each with $\beta=1$. The first update starts at $v^{(0)}_\theta=v_{\text{old}}$, where restoration is zero. The second starts from $v^{(1)}_\theta$ and therefore gets restoration toward $v^{(0)}_\theta$. In Figure 3(a), this run avoids collapse over the observed window but reaches OCR 0.9 only at step 320 instead of 21: 15.2× slower.

Restoration is the stabilizer. In NFT, though, it is expensive.

Why a small restoration can dominate the parameter gradient

Figure 3(c) measures both terms at the second update, averaged over rollouts 0–17. Restoration has a 9.4× smaller output-force RMS than guidance, yet a 4.9× larger aggregated parameter-gradient norm. The paper's explanation is that "heterogeneous per-sample guidance largely cancels across states, while restoration survives aggregation", and it defers a detailed analysis. Here is one way to see why this happens. It is my gloss, not the paper's analysis. The parameter gradient is a sum over states of Jacobian-transposed output forces, $\nabla_\theta\mathcal L=-\sum_s J_s^\top f_s$ with $J_s=\partial v_\theta(s)/\partial\theta$.

  • Guidance per state is $f_s=(2r-1)h$. The sign of $2r-1$ varies across samples, and $h=u_t-v_{\text{old}}$ contains the sample noise $\epsilon$. The terms $J_s^\top f_s$ point in many directions, and their sum grows roughly like the square root of the number of states.
  • Restoration per state is $f_s=-\beta^2 d_s$. The drift was itself produced by a parameter change $\Delta\theta$, so $d_s\approx J_s\Delta\theta$ and $J_s^\top d_s\approx J_s^\top J_s\,\Delta\theta$. Every term is a positive semidefinite matrix applied to the same $\Delta\theta$, so the terms add coherently and their sum grows linearly in the number of states.

A small but coherent output force can therefore beat a large but incoherent one after aggregation, which is consistent with Figure 3(c). The design consequence is the one the paper draws. Guidance must also be coherent across states, or restoration will swamp it.

Why EMA is not enough

The EMA controls restoration indirectly, through the lag. Too little lag gives too little restoration and risks collapse; too much slows reward improvement. The paper cites DiffusionNFT's own results. Fully on-policy updates collapse, nearly offline updates converge impractically slowly, and the default schedule $\eta=\min(0.001i,0.5)$ is unstable on OCR, which needs a maximum retention of 0.999. In the paper's FLUX experiments, transferring NFT's GenEval-tuned EMA and $\beta$ makes training unstable (Figure 6(a)). Per-task tuning limits scalability.

Key idea

Restoration stabilizes NFT-style training. EMA requires task-specific tuning, and two updates per rollout avoid the tuning but slow reward improvement about 15×, because NFT's incoherent guidance cancels while restoration does not. A better design adapts restoration to each state's drift and keeps guidance coherent enough to stay dominant (Insight 2).

§4.1 Aggregation-first guidance

Motivation

The consistency force enters because NFT's target is built from $u_t$, whose conditional mean is the forward velocity $v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}}$, and that differs from the reverse field by $e$. The fix is to build a target that never mentions $u_t$. DiffusionART aggregates each prompt's endpoints and rewards into one direction first, then assigns that direction to the reverse-process states the sampler actually visited.

The reward-tilted endpoint distribution

At each iteration, tilt the current rollout distribution (not a fixed pretrained one) exponentially:

\[ p_\tau(x_0\mid c)\ \propto\ p_{\text{old}}(x_0\mid c)\,\exp\bigl(\tau R(x_0,c)\bigr),\qquad \tau\ge0 . \]

The paper writes the tilt strength as $\tau$, because $\eta$ already names the EMA decay. The repo README writes the same formula with $\eta$. Setting $\tau=0$ recovers $p_{\text{old}}$. Larger $\tau$ prefers higher-reward endpoints. This is the familiar KL-regularized RL optimum, $\arg\max_p\ \mathbb E_p[R]-\tfrac1\tau\mathrm{KL}(p\Vert p_{\text{old}})$, applied one iteration at a time.

Lemma 2: the first-order tilt is a covariance

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}\mathbb E_{p_\tau}[x_0\mid c]\Big|_{\tau=0}=\operatorname{Cov}_{p_{\text{old}}}(R,x_0\mid c). \]

Proof (Appendix B, "Covariance as the First-Order Reward Tilt"). Suppress $c$. Let $M(\tau)=\mathbb E_{p_{\text{old}}}[x_0e^{\tau R}]$ and $Z(\tau)=\mathbb E_{p_{\text{old}}}[e^{\tau R}]$. The tilted density is $p_{\text{old}}e^{\tau R}/Z(\tau)$, so the tilted mean is $M(\tau)/Z(\tau)$. Differentiating under the expectation gives $M'(\tau)=\mathbb E[x_0Re^{\tau R}]$ and $Z'(\tau)=\mathbb E[Re^{\tau R}]$. At $\tau=0$, $Z(0)=1$, $M(0)=\mathbb E[x_0]$, $M'(0)=\mathbb E[x_0R]$ and $Z'(0)=\mathbb E[R]$. The quotient rule gives

\[ \Bigl(\tfrac{M}{Z}\Bigr)'(0)=\frac{M'(0)Z(0)-M(0)Z'(0)}{Z(0)^2}=\mathbb E[x_0R]-\mathbb E[x_0]\,\mathbb E[R]=\operatorname{Cov}(R,x_0). \]

Differentiation under the expectation is valid if, for example, $\mathbb E[(1+\lVert x_0\rVert)(1+\lvert R\rvert)e^{a\lvert R\rvert}]<\infty$ for some $a>0$. ∎

The check below verifies the identity numerically with central finite differences on a 2D mixture, plus a 1D Gaussian with a closed form. For $p_{\text{old}}=\mathcal N(\mu,s^2)$ and $R(x)=x$, the tilted law is $\mathcal N(\mu+\tau s^2,s^2)$, so the derivative is $s^2=\operatorname{Var}(x)=\operatorname{Cov}(R,x)$.

From endpoints to every state, under rectified flow

Under rectified flow, $u_t=x_0-\epsilon$ with $\epsilon$ independent of $x_0$, so $\operatorname{Cov}_{Q_{\text{old}}}(R,u_t\mid c)=\operatorname{Cov}(R,x_0\mid c)-\operatorname{Cov}(R,\epsilon\mid c)=\operatorname{Cov}_{p_{\text{old}}}(R,x_0\mid c)$ at every noise level. The same vector is therefore the first-order change in the average velocity target at every $t$. It is also the prompt-level counterpart of the ideal NFT guidance $g$ from Lemma 1. At $t=1$ the state carries no endpoint information, so conditioning on $s_t$ does nothing. Without clipping, $r=\tfrac12+\tfrac{R-\bar R_c}{2Z_c}$ is affine in $R$, so $g=\operatorname{Cov}(r,u_t)=\tfrac{1}{2Z_c}\operatorname{Cov}(R,x_0)$, a positive multiple of the same direction.

The estimator and its normalization

Each prompt has $K$ endpoints and rewards. The paper estimates and normalizes:

\[ \boxed{\ \overline{\operatorname{Cov}}(R,x_0\mid c)=\frac1K\sum_{i=1}^K(R_i-\bar R_c)\bigl(x_0^{(i)}-\bar x_{0,c}\bigr),\qquad G(c)=\frac{\overline{\operatorname{Cov}}}{\operatorname{RMS}(\overline{\operatorname{Cov}})}\ } \]

$\bar x_{0,c}$ is the group mean endpoint, and $\operatorname{RMS}(z)=\sqrt{D^{-1}\sum_j z_j^2}$ over the $D$ coordinates of the latent. Normalization fixes the guidance magnitude at unit RMS across prompts and reward models. This removes differences in reward scale and spread without a guidance-strength coefficient. A reward multiplied by 100, or a prompt with a tiny reward spread, produces the same $\lVert G\rVert$. Only the direction carries information.

Worked example: $G(c)$ for $K=4$ in 2D

Take endpoints $x^{(1..4)}=(0,0),(2,0),(0,2),(2,2)$ with rewards $R=1,3,2,6$.

  • Means: $\bar R=3$, $\bar x=(1,1)$. Centered rewards: $-2,0,-1,3$. Centered endpoints: $(-1,-1),(1,-1),(-1,1),(1,1)$.
  • Products: $(2,2)$, $(0,0)$, $(1,-1)$ and $(3,3)$, which sum to $(6,4)$. Then $\overline{\operatorname{Cov}}=(6,4)/4=(1.5,1.0)$.
  • $\operatorname{RMS}=\sqrt{(1.5^2+1.0^2)/2}=\sqrt{1.625}=1.2748$, so $G=(1.1767,0.7845)$. Its RMS is 1 and its Euclidean norm is $\sqrt2$.
  • First-order tilted mean at $\tau=0.05$: $\bar x+\tau\,\overline{\operatorname{Cov}}=(1.075,1.050)$. Exact self-normalized tilted mean: $(1.0761,1.0518)$. At $\tau=0.2$, first order gives $(1.30,1.20)$ and exact gives $(1.3092,1.2251)$. The agreement degrades as $\tau$ grows, as a first-order approximation should.

Now apply it. A visited state of this prompt with cached velocity $v^{(0)}=(0.3,-0.5)$ gets the frozen target $v^{(0)}+G=(1.4767,0.2845)$. At the start of the update the loss is $\tfrac wD\lVert G\rVert^2=w$ (unit RMS means $\lVert G\rVert^2=D$), and the output-space descent direction is $\tfrac{2w}{D}G=w\,G$.

The guided target and why the consistency force disappears

For every state $s_t$ visited by the ODE rollout of prompt $c$, freeze

\[ v^{\text{ART}}_{\text{tgt}}(s_t)=v^{(0)}_\theta(s_t)+G(c), \]

where $v^{(0)}_\theta=v_{\text{old}}$ is the prediction cached during the rollout. No extra forward pass is needed: the sampler had to compute it anyway. The loss is $w(s_t)\operatorname{MSE}(v_\theta(s_t),v^{\text{ART}}_{\text{tgt}}(s_t))$. At $v_\theta=v^{(0)}_\theta$ its negative gradient is $\tfrac{2w}{D}G(c)$. Compare this with Proposition 1. NFT's force has the form $\mathbb E[(2r-1)(u_t-v_{\text{rvs}})\mid s_t]$, and the $u_t$ inside it is what produces $e$. ART's target contains $v^{(0)}_\theta$ (the reverse field itself) and $G$ (a constant), and nothing else. There is no $u_t$, so there is no $\mathbb E[u_t\mid s_t]$, so there is no $e$. The force is removed by construction, not by cancellation.

Two more things change relative to NFT:

  • States. ART trains on the states the sampler visited, as reverse-process RL does, not on re-noised endpoints. It inherits Flow-GRPO's on-trajectory supervision without needing a likelihood.
  • Credit assignment. Each state receives an explicit vector, not a scalar advantage passed through a likelihood ratio along that step's own sampled noise.

The CFG analogy

Classifier-free guidance (Step 1) samples with $v=v(x_t,t,\varnothing)+w\,[v(x_t,t,c)-v(x_t,t,\varnothing)]$: it adds a direction to the velocity at sampling time. Distilling CFG means training a single model to output the guided velocity directly. The README describes DiffusionART the same way. The reward-improving direction plays the role of the guidance term, and the model learns to internalize it. The analogy is useful but loose. CFG's direction is statewise, a function of $x_t$ and $t$. $G(c)$ is a single vector shared by all states and all times of a prompt.

What a shared direction gives up

The exact statewise tilt direction at $s_t$ would be $\operatorname{Cov}(R,u_t\mid s_t)$, which varies with the state. Estimating it needs posterior samples of $x_0$ given $s_t$, which is expensive. $G(c)$ uses the $t=1$ (unconditional) value everywhere. The paper says plainly that "a prompt-level direction need not match the posterior guidance at every state" (Appendix B). That mismatch is why the second update and its statewise restoration exist (next section).

Pitfall

Unit-RMS normalization means $G(c)$ never shrinks. A prompt whose $K$ rewards differ only by reward-model noise still receives a full-size push, in a random direction. The repo handles the exactly-constant case explicitly (zero_signal_mode in src/visual_rl/algorithms/covariance.py), but near-constant rewards still yield unit guidance. Nothing in the objective says "stop": convergence relies on the push directions averaging out and on restoration.

§4.2 Two-update restoration

Instead of tuning an EMA, DiffusionART splits each rollout batch into two disjoint minibatches and takes one optimizer step on each. Let $v^{(0)}_\theta$ and $v^{(1)}_\theta$ be the fields before and after the first update. Both updates regress toward the same frozen target $v^{\text{ART}}_{\text{tgt}}=v^{(0)}_\theta+G(c)$. Per state, $\mathcal L_\theta(s_t)=w(s_t)\operatorname{MSE}(v_\theta(s_t),v^{\text{ART}}_{\text{tgt}}(s_t))$ with $\operatorname{MSE}(a,b)=D^{-1}\lVert a-b\rVert^2$, where $w$ combines timestep weighting and the mask. Let $d(s_t)=v_\theta(s_t)-v^{(0)}_\theta(s_t)$. Before each update:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{update 1:}\quad &\mathcal L^{(0)}=\tfrac wD\lVert G(c)\rVert^2, && -\nabla_{v_\theta}\mathcal L\big|_{v^{(0)}_\theta}=\tfrac{2w}{D}\,G(c),\\ \text{update 2:}\quad &\mathcal L^{(1)}=\tfrac wD\lVert G(c)-d(s_t)\rVert^2, && -\nabla_{v_\theta}\mathcal L\big|_{v^{(1)}_\theta}=\tfrac{2w}{D}\bigl(\underbrace{G(c)}_{\text{guidance}}\ \underbrace{-\,d(s_t)}_{\text{restoration}}\bigr). \end{aligned} \]

The derivation is one line: $v^{(1)}_\theta-v^{\text{ART}}_{\text{tgt}}=(v^{(0)}_\theta+d)-(v^{(0)}_\theta+G)=d-G$. The second update keeps the original guidance and adds a pull back toward the rollout prediction whose strength is exactly the drift. Note that the drift $d(s_t)$ on the second minibatch was caused by an update computed on the first minibatch. These states were not in that batch, so their displacement is collateral movement from parameter sharing. This is the generalization behaviour that restoration controls.

Why this is better than NFT's two updates

NFT with two updates also gets restoration, and §3.2 showed it costs 15.2× in speed. The difference lies in the guidance. NFT's per-state guidance $(2r-1)h$ varies in sign and is noisy, so it cancels in parameter space and restoration takes over. ART's guidance is the same vector for every state of a prompt, so it aggregates coherently, just as restoration does. Guidance therefore remains dominant while restoration acts as a statewise brake that is weak where drift is small and strong where drift is large. There is no lag schedule and no $\beta$.

The paper's ablation (Figure 6(b), OCR on SD3.5-M) compares three runs. ART: 1 update rises to about 0.95 within a few dozen steps, then deteriorates steadily, ending near 0.27 by step 800. ART: 2 updates reaches the same level and stays near 0.97 throughout. NFT: 2 updates with a carefully tuned $\beta=0.1$ learns, drops to near zero around step 270 and recovers. In the plotted curve it then falls to zero again around step 440 and stays there through step 800. The text describes only the first, brief collapse. Appendix B explains the single-update deterioration: a prompt-level direction need not match the posterior guidance at each state, and without statewise correction the deviations accumulate.

Pitfall

The two updates must be on disjoint minibatches of the same rollout, with guidance computed from the complete group of $K$ trajectories before splitting. The README recommends splitting within each prompt, so that both halves keep prompt coverage: for example, two batches of 32 prompts with $K/2$ trajectories each, not 16 prompts with $K$ each. It also warns that shrinking the update window to save memory creates more optimizer steps per rollout, which changes the restoration dynamics. Reduce the micro-batch and accumulate instead.

§4.3 Trust-region masking for ODE rollouts

The problem: no KL for a deterministic sampler

Guidance acts in both updates, so one rollout batch can still produce an excessive policy change. PPO would limit this through the likelihood ratio. An ODE step, however, is deterministic, so its transition is a point mass and there is no finite KL between the old and new step. DiffusionART borrows Flow-GRPO's ODE-to-SDE construction only as a measuring device. Rollouts stay ODE trajectories; the SDE defines reference Gaussian transitions for the current and old policies:

\[ q_\theta(\cdot\mid s_t)=\mathcal N\bigl(\mu(s_t,v_\theta(s_t)),\sigma^2_{\text{ref}}(t)I\bigr),\qquad q_{\text{old}}(\cdot\mid s_t)=\mathcal N\bigl(\mu(s_t,v^{(0)}_\theta(s_t)),\sigma^2_{\text{ref}}(t)I\bigr). \]

With reverse step $h=t-t_{\text{next}}>0$ and $0<t<1$ (Appendix C, "Reference Transitions"):

\[ \mu(s_t,v)=x_t+h\Bigl[\Bigl(1+\tfrac{\nu^2}{2}\Bigr)v-\tfrac{\nu^2}{2(1-t)}x_t\Bigr],\qquad \sigma^2_{\text{ref}}(t)=\nu^2\,\tfrac{t}{1-t}\,h, \]

with $\nu=0.8$. At $t=1$ the paper uses $\mu=x_t+hv$ and $\sigma^2_{\text{ref}}(1)=\nu^2$. The means differ only through $v$, so $\mu(s_t,v_\theta)-\mu(s_t,v^{(0)}_\theta)=h(1+\nu^2/2)\,d(s_t)$. The KL between two isotropic Gaussians with equal variance is $\lVert\Delta\mu\rVert^2/(2\sigma^2)$, and the per-coordinate KL proxy is

\[ m(s_t)=\frac1D D_{\text{KL}}\bigl(q_\theta\,\Vert\,q_{\text{old}}\bigr)=\frac{\lVert\mu(s_t,v_\theta)-\mu(s_t,v^{(0)}_\theta)\rVert^2}{2D\,\sigma^2_{\text{ref}}(t)}=\frac{h^2(1+\nu^2/2)^2\,\lVert d(s_t)\rVert^2}{2D\,\sigma^2_{\text{ref}}(t)}. \]

The mask: block large outward moves, keep inward corrections

\[ w(s_t)=t\Bigl[1-\mathbf 1\{m(s_t)\ge\delta\}\;\mathbf 1\{\langle d(s_t),\,G(c)-d(s_t)\rangle>0\}\Bigr],\qquad\delta=10^{-5}. \]
  • The first indicator detects excessive drift.
  • The second detects an outward update: the second update's descent direction $G-d$ has a positive component along the existing drift $d$, so taking the step would move the state further from the rollout policy.
  • A state is dropped only if both hold. A large drift whose update points back inward, $\langle d,G-d\rangle\le0$, is kept, because that update is restoration. The rule is inspired by Flow-DPPO.
  • The factor $t$ up-weights high-noise states. It is the velocity_linear_sigma loss weighting in src/visual_rl/algorithms/art.py.

The mask is recomputed before each update and treated as a constant in backprop. At the first update $d=0$, so $m=0<\delta$ and nothing is masked: in effect the mask constrains only the second update.

From LLM RL

This is PPO's clip in different clothes. PPO zeroes the gradient of a token when the ratio is already outside $[1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon]$ in the direction the advantage pushes, and keeps it when the gradient would move the ratio back inside. Here the "ratio" is the KL proxy $m$, and "the direction the advantage pushes" is the sign of $\langle d,G-d\rangle$. The main difference is that PPO's trust region is per token with a likelihood the sampler really has, whereas this one is per state with a likelihood borrowed from a reference SDE.

Worked example

Use the $G=(1.1767,0.7845)$ from the earlier example, a 10-step schedule ($h=0.1$), $t=0.5$, $D=2$, $\nu=0.8$. Then $\sigma^2_{\text{ref}}=0.64\cdot1\cdot0.1=0.064$ and $h(1+\nu^2/2)=0.132$.

  • $d=(0.4,0.1)$: $m=0.132^2\cdot0.17/(2\cdot2\cdot0.064)=0.0116\ge\delta$. $G-d=(0.777,0.685)$ and $\langle d,G-d\rangle=0.379>0$, so the update is outward. Masked ($w=0$).
  • $d=(1.5,1.0)$ (overshoot): $m=0.221\ge\delta$, but $\langle d,G-d\rangle=-0.700<0$, so the update is inward. Kept, with $w=t=0.5$: the second update pulls this state back.
  • $d=(0.01,0)$: $m=6.8\times10^{-6}<\delta$. Kept.

Solving $m=\delta$ for this $t$ and $h$ gives a threshold of $\operatorname{RMS}(d)\approx0.0086$ in velocity units, against velocities of order 1. In practice, any state with noticeable drift and an outward-pointing update is masked. $\delta$ acts nearly as a switch on the sign condition, and the threshold scales with $h$ and $t$.

Evidence for the mask

  • Video (HunyuanVideo-13B, VQ:MQ 1:1). The no-mask run drops around step 200 and then recovers; the masked run improves steadily. In the plotted curves the masked run reaches VQ near 15 by about step 300, while the unmasked run sits near 10–12.5 (Figure 8).
  • Diagnostics. Over the shared first 300 steps, the maximum first-update gradient norm is 1.96 with masking and 3.33 without. The velocity drift measured before second updates shows much larger spikes without the mask. Masking the second update therefore also keeps later first updates well behaved.
  • Images. On SD3.5-M (three tasks) and FLUX.1-dev, training stays effective without the mask and can improve slightly faster. The FLUX no-mask run reaches a training GenEval of 0.971 by step 180. The mask is a stability safeguard that matters more for video.

§4.4 Pairwise reward models

Visual quality has no verifier, so preferences between pairs are a natural reward signal. DiffusionART-Pairwise splits each prompt's $K$ endpoints into $M=K/2$ disjoint pairs. It queries a pairwise judge and assigns labels $y_i=1$ to winners and $y_i=0$ to losers, or $y_i=\tfrac12$ to both for a tie. The labels replace $R_i$ in the otherwise unchanged covariance. Everything downstream (normalization, targets, two updates, mask) is identical.

The pairwise covariance identity (Appendix C)

Every pair's labels average to $\tfrac12$ (including ties), so the global label mean is $\tfrac12$. Because $\sum_i(y_i-\tfrac12)=0$, subtracting $\bar x_{0,c}$ changes nothing:

\[ \overline{\operatorname{Cov}}=\frac1K\sum_{i=1}^K\bigl(y_i-\tfrac12\bigr)\bigl(x_0^{(i)}-\bar x_{0,c}\bigr)=\frac1K\sum_{i=1}^K\bigl(y_i-\tfrac12\bigr)x_0^{(i)}=\frac{1}{2K}\sum_{j\ \text{non-tied}}\bigl(x^{\text{win}}_{0,j}-x^{\text{lose}}_{0,j}\bigr). \]

A winner contributes $+\tfrac12x^{\text{win}}$, a loser $-\tfrac12x^{\text{lose}}$, and a tie contributes zero. A single pair's own covariance, with denominator 2, is $\tfrac14(x^{\text{win}}-x^{\text{lose}})$, and averaging $M$ of these gives the same expression with coefficient $\tfrac{1}{4M}=\tfrac{1}{2K}$. The coefficient disappears after RMS normalization. For $K=2$:

\[ G(c)=\frac{x^{\text{win}}_0-x^{\text{lose}}_0}{\operatorname{RMS}(x^{\text{win}}_0-x^{\text{lose}}_0)}, \]

a unit-RMS vector from the worse sample to the better one, and zero for a tie.

One consequence the paper does not state: for $K=2$ with scalar rewards, $\overline{\operatorname{Cov}}=\tfrac14(R_1-R_2)(x_1-x_2)$, which after normalization is the same vector. At $K=2$, scalar DiffusionART and DiffusionART-Pairwise produce identical guidance whenever the judge agrees with the reward's ordering. The widget shows a 0.0° angle between them at $K=2$.

In the repository, the pairwise labels are produced by comparing scalar scores (art_pairwise_covariance in src/visual_rl/algorithms/covariance.py). They do not test a separately trained pairwise judge, whose noise and intransitivity could behave differently.

The full algorithm

The paper's Algorithm 1, reproduced exactly. Equation 5 is the boxed covariance estimator in §4.1 above.

Algorithm 1: DiffusionART, one training iteration.
Rollout stage
1Sample with an ODE solver using $v_\theta^{(0)}=v_\theta$.
For each prompt $c$, collect $K$ endpoints with rewards $R$, states $s_t$, and predictions $v_\theta^{(0)}(s_t)$.
2Compute $G(c)$ by Equation 5 for each prompt $c$.
3Freeze $v_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathrm{ART}}(s_t)=v_\theta^{(0)}(s_t)+G(c)$ for each state $s_t$ of prompt $c$.
Training stage
4Split the samples into two equal-sized disjoint minibatches $B_1,B_2$.
5For each $B\in\{B_1,B_2\}$, recompute $w(s_t)$ and update $\theta$ with one step on
$\mathcal L(B;\theta)=\dfrac{1}{|B|}\sum_{s_t\in B}w(s_t)\operatorname{MSE}\bigl(v_\theta(s_t),v_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathrm{ART}}(s_t)\bigr)$.

Line by line:

  1. Line 1. Roll out with the deterministic ODE sampler of the current policy, so $v^{(0)}_\theta=v_\theta$: there is no separate old or EMA copy. Store, for each visited state, the latent $x_t$, the time $t$, the step size and the velocity the sampler used. That cached velocity is $v^{(0)}_\theta(s_t)$, and reusing it saves the extra old-policy forward pass on re-noised states that NFT needs. Rewards are black-box calls on the endpoints. Unlike Flow-GRPO, the sampler is not converted to an SDE, so samples come from the model's actual inference sampler.
  2. Line 2. One reduction per prompt over its complete group of $K$: center rewards and endpoints, average their products, divide by RMS. With several reward models, combine their weighted contributions before normalizing (README); normalizing each separately changes their relative influence.
  3. Line 3. Every state on every one of the prompt's $K$ trajectories gets the same offset $G(c)$ added to its cached velocity. Targets are frozen tensors.
  4. Line 4. Two equal, disjoint halves, preferably split within each prompt.
  5. Line 5. Before each update, run the current model on the half's states to get $v_\theta$ and compute $d=v_\theta-v^{(0)}_\theta$, the KL proxy $m$ and the outward test. Then set $w=t$ or 0 and take one optimizer step on the weighted MSE. For $B_1$, $d=0$ and $w=t$ everywhere. For $B_2$, the first step has moved the model, so the mask and the restoration term are live.

Algorithm 2 (Pairwise) is identical except that line 2 becomes three lines: form $M=K/2$ disjoint pairs and query the pairwise reward model; assign $y_i\in\{1,0,\tfrac12\}$; compute the covariance with $y_i$ in place of $R_i$.

The same iteration as algorithm-level pseudocode, in the paper's sign convention. The repo's loss (src/visual_rl/algorithms/art.py) uses the SD3/diffusers convention, in which the network predicts $\epsilon-x_0=-v$. There the target is written old_velocity - displacement, which is $v^{(0)}+G$ after flipping signs. Step 5 walks through that code.

This does not contradict the paper's large-scale results, but it is a real limitation of a shared, prompt-level direction. Here $p_{\text{old}}$ for each prompt is an 8-mode ring, and improving the reward means moving mass between modes. A single translation $G(c)$ applied to every trajectory moves all eight modes together, which is the wrong shape of update. With $K=2$, each pair's $G$ is the direction from one sample to the other, local to those two trajectories, so the update behaves more like a per-pair correction. This is my reading of the toy, not a claim from the paper. The paper's FLUX experiment found the opposite preference (prompt-global pooling beat pair-local, 0.91 against 0.78), presumably because variance reduction dominates there. Critical reading

Assumptions

  • The theory is at the on-policy point and in expectation. Proposition 1 holds at $v_\theta=v^{\text{old}}_{\text{rvs}}$ with exact conditional expectations. Practical NFT uses one-sample estimates per state, and after the first step of an epoch it is no longer at $d=0$. The decomposition is not measured in the paper's experiments; Figure 2(b) is a schematic. (The toy above measures it in 2D.)
  • Clipping is ignored. Statements such as "$q=1/2$ at $t=1$" and "$g$ is a positive multiple of $\operatorname{Cov}(R,x_0)$" need the unclipped goodness. NFT clips.
  • Rectified flow. The step $\operatorname{Cov}(R,u_t)=\operatorname{Cov}(R,x_0)$ uses $u_t=x_0-\epsilon$ with $\epsilon\perp x_0$. Other schedules give $u_t=-\dot\alpha_tx_0-\dot\sigma_t\epsilon$, whose covariance with $R$ is $-\dot\alpha_t\operatorname{Cov}(R,x_0)$, the same direction with a $t$-dependent scale.
  • First order. $G(c)$ is the derivative of the tilted mean at $\tau=0$. It cannot express a reward that wants more or less spread (try "prefer far from origin" in the widget), and for a multimodal $p_{\text{old}}(x_0\mid c)$ a shared translation is a poor proxy for reweighting modes (the $K=8$ toy).

Limitations the paper acknowledges or implies

  • The prompt-level direction need not match the statewise posterior guidance (Appendix B). Restoration controls the accumulated mismatch but does not remove it.
  • Results depend on reward quality, as for ReFL and other reward-based fine-tuning (README). No KL to a reference model is used, and no diversity or out-of-distribution metric is reported. Reward hacking is plausible, but untested here.
  • Evaluation largely reuses the training reward models (GenEval, VideoReward), so improvements are measured by the same instrument being optimized.

Things to watch for

  • Text versus figure. In Figure 6(b), NFT: 2 updates collapses to zero a second time near step 440 and stays there through step 800. The text describes only a brief collapse and recovery.
  • Seeds and variance. No error bars or seed counts are given. The non-monotone pairwise results ($K=4$ below both $K=2$ and $K=8$) are a hint that single-run differences of this size may be noise.
  • Baseline tuning. On FLUX, NFT's hyperparameters were transferred deliberately to show tuning sensitivity. That is the point being made, but it also means the FLUX comparison is against an untuned NFT.
  • Fixed $\delta$. $\delta=10^{-5}$ is "fixed throughout", but its meaning in drift units scales with $h$ (the number of steps) and $t$. A 4-step I2V schedule and a 16-step T2V schedule enforce different effective radii.

What I would test first

  1. Log $\lvert2q-1\rvert$ and $(2q-1)e$ during a real NFT run, using a cheap estimator (for example, re-noise a batch of endpoints and compare $v_\theta$ with the batch-posterior average of $u_t$ at large $t$). Check whether its growth precedes the gradient-norm spike, as it does in the toy.
  2. Statewise guidance: compute $G$ separately per time bucket, or per noise level with a small posterior estimate, and compare with the shared $G(c)$. If the single-update deterioration disappears, the Appendix B explanation is confirmed.
  3. Diversity under ART: per-prompt endpoint variance, LPIPS diversity or mode coverage, against NFT and Flow-GRPO, since a shared translation per prompt might collapse diversity faster.
  4. Three seeds for the pairwise $K$ sweep, and a true pairwise judge rather than binarized scalar scores.
  5. A $\delta$ sweep across step counts, to see whether the mask is really a sign test in practice.

Pitfall

Do not read "removes the consistency force by construction" as "ART's model stays forward–reverse consistent". It does not: the toy's $\lvert e\rvert$ grows to about 5 under ART. The claim is that ART's update contains no term proportional to $e$. Whether the inconsistency matters downstream (for example, if you later switch samplers or step counts) is untested.

Exercises

Exercise 4.1: Derive the first-order tilt, then the second-order term

Let $p_\tau\propto p_{\text{old}}e^{\tau R}$ and $m(\tau)=\mathbb E_{p_\tau}[x_0]$. Show that $m'(0)=\operatorname{Cov}(R,x_0)$, and find $m'(\tau)$ for general $\tau$.

Solution. Write $m=M/Z$ with $M=\mathbb E_{\text{old}}[x_0e^{\tau R}]$ and $Z=\mathbb E_{\text{old}}[e^{\tau R}]$. Then $m'=M'/Z-MZ'/Z^2$. Note $M'/Z=\mathbb E_{p_\tau}[x_0R]$, $M/Z=\mathbb E_{p_\tau}[x_0]$ and $Z'/Z=\mathbb E_{p_\tau}[R]$. Hence $m'(\tau)=\operatorname{Cov}_{p_\tau}(R,x_0)$ for every $\tau$, not only at 0, and at $\tau=0$ it is $\operatorname{Cov}_{p_{\text{old}}}(R,x_0)$. Differentiating once more gives $m''(\tau)=\mathbb E_{p_\tau}[(R-\bar R_\tau)^2(x_0-m(\tau))]$, a third mixed central moment. If the reward is roughly symmetric around its mean within $p_{\text{old}}$, this term is small, and the first-order direction stays accurate for larger $\tau$. The worked example confirms this: at $\tau=0.05$ first order is within 0.001 of exact; at $\tau=0.2$ the error is about 0.025.

Exercise 4.2: Why the consistency term vanishes for ART, and how to bring it back

(a) Compute the on-policy output-space force of the ART loss at a state, conditioned on $s_t$. (b) Suppose someone "simplifies" ART by training on forward-noised states with target $v_{\text{tgt}}=u_t+G(c)$. Compute the conditional on-policy force and identify the consistency term.

Solution. (a) The ART target $v^{(0)}_\theta(s_t)+G(c)$ is a deterministic function of $s_t$ and $c$. With $\mathcal L=\tfrac wD\lVert v_\theta-v^{(0)}_\theta-G\rVert^2$, $-\nabla_{v_\theta}\mathcal L|_{v_\theta=v^{(0)}_\theta}=\tfrac{2w}{D}G(c)$. Conditioning on $s_t$ changes nothing, because nothing random remains. No $u_t$ appears, so there is no $\mathbb E[u_t\mid s_t]=v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}}$, and no $e$.
(b) Now $-\tfrac{D}{2w}\nabla=\mathbb E[u_t+G-v_{\text{rvs}}^{\text{old}}\mid s_t]=G+\bigl(v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}}-v^{\text{old}}_{\text{rvs}}\bigr)=G+e$. The consistency force returns with weight 1, larger than NFT's $\lvert2q-1\rvert\le1$. The variant also regresses toward $v_{\text{fwd}}$, which is plain flow matching on the model's own samples. The lesson: the force appears exactly when the target's conditional mean involves $\mathbb E[u_t\mid s_t]$.

Exercise 4.3: Compute G(c) by hand for K = 4 in 1D, and for K = 2

(a) Endpoints $x=(-1,0,1,2)$, rewards $R=(0,1,3,2)$. Compute $\overline{\operatorname{Cov}}$, $G$, the first-order tilted mean at $\tau=0.1$ and the exact tilted mean. (b) Show that for $K=2$ the scalar-reward $G$ equals the pairwise $G$.

Solution. (a) $\bar R=1.5$, $\bar x=0.5$. Centered rewards are $(-1.5,-0.5,1.5,0.5)$ and centered endpoints $(-1.5,-0.5,0.5,1.5)$. The products are $2.25,0.25,0.75,0.75$, which sum to 4, so $\overline{\operatorname{Cov}}=1.0$. In 1D, $\operatorname{RMS}(z)=\lvert z\rvert$, so $G=\operatorname{sign}(\overline{\operatorname{Cov}})=+1$: in one dimension, normalization keeps only the sign. First order: $0.5+0.1\cdot1.0=0.6$. Exact: weights $e^{0.1R}=(1,1.10517,1.34986,1.22140)$, sum 4.67643, $\sum w_ix_i=2.79267$, mean $0.59718$.
(b) With $K=2$, $\bar R=(R_1+R_2)/2$, so $R_1-\bar R=(R_1-R_2)/2=-(R_2-\bar R)$, and likewise for $x$. Both terms of $\tfrac12\sum_i(R_i-\bar R)(x_i-\bar x)$ equal $\tfrac{R_1-R_2}{2}\cdot\tfrac{x_1-x_2}{2}$, so $\overline{\operatorname{Cov}}=\tfrac12\cdot2\cdot\tfrac{(R_1-R_2)(x_1-x_2)}{4}=\tfrac14(R_1-R_2)(x_1-x_2)$. After RMS normalization this is $\operatorname{sign}(R_1-R_2)\,(x_1-x_2)/\operatorname{RMS}(x_1-x_2)$, the unit-RMS vector from loser to winner, which is the pairwise $G$. Scalar magnitudes are discarded at $K=2$.

Exercise 4.4: Branch forces and when the residual dominates

(a) Derive $\mathcal F_+=g+qe$ and $\mathcal F_-=g-(1-q)e$ at the on-policy point. (b) At a low-noise state, $q=0.8$, $g$ has norm 0.1 and $e$ has norm 0.5 and is parallel to $g$. Compare the reward-improving and consistency parts of the joint force, and of each branch alone.

Solution. (a) Positive branch: $\ell_+=r\lVert\beta d-h\rVert^2$, so $-\tfrac1{2\beta}\nabla_d\ell_+|_{d=0}=r\,h$. Conditioning gives $\mathbb E[rh\mid s_t]=\operatorname{Cov}(r,h)+\mathbb E[r]\mathbb E[h]=g+qe$. Negative branch: $\ell_-=(1-r)\lVert\beta d+h\rVert^2$, so $-\tfrac1{2\beta}\nabla_d\ell_-|_{d=0}=-(1-r)h$, and $\mathbb E[-(1-r)h\mid s_t]=-\operatorname{Cov}(1-r,h)-(1-q)e=g-(1-q)e$.
(b) Joint: $2\lVert g\rVert=0.2$ against $(2q-1)\lVert e\rVert=0.6\cdot0.5=0.3$, so the consistency term is 1.5× the guidance at this state. Positive alone: $0.1$ against $0.8\cdot0.5=0.4$ (4×). Negative alone: $0.1$ against $0.2\cdot0.5=0.1$ (equal magnitude, opposite sign along $e$). Joint training helps but does not save this state; only states with $q$ near $1/2$, which are high-noise states, are clean.

Exercise 4.5: The trust-region mask by hand

Take $D=2$, $\nu=0.8$, a 10-step schedule ($h=0.1$), $t=0.5$, $G=(1.1767,0.7845)$. For $d=(0.4,0.1)$, $(1.5,1.0)$ and $(0.01,0)$, compute $m(s_t)$, decide whether the state is masked, and find the drift RMS at which $m=\delta=10^{-5}$.

Solution. $\sigma^2_{\text{ref}}=\nu^2\tfrac{t}{1-t}h=0.064$, and $\Delta\mu=h(1+\nu^2/2)d=0.132\,d$, so $m=0.132^2\lVert d\rVert^2/(2\cdot2\cdot0.064)=0.06806\lVert d\rVert^2$.

  • $d=(0.4,0.1)$: $\lVert d\rVert^2=0.17$, so $m=0.01157\ge\delta$. $\langle d,G-d\rangle=0.4\cdot0.7767+0.1\cdot0.6845=0.379>0$. Masked.
  • $d=(1.5,1.0)$: $m=0.2212\ge\delta$, but $\langle d,G-d\rangle=1.5\cdot(-0.3233)+1.0\cdot(-0.2155)=-0.700<0$. Kept, $w=0.5$.
  • $d=(0.01,0)$: $m=6.8\times10^{-6}<\delta$. Kept.

Threshold: $m=0.06806\cdot D\cdot\operatorname{RMS}(d)^2=0.1361\operatorname{RMS}(d)^2=10^{-5}$ gives $\operatorname{RMS}(d)\approx0.0086$. In general, $m=\tfrac{h(1+\nu^2/2)^2(1-t)}{2\nu^2t}\operatorname{RMS}(d)^2$. With more steps (smaller $h$) or larger $t$, the same $\delta$ tolerates more drift.

Takeaways

  • NFT's on-policy force is $2g+(2q-1)e$: reward-improving guidance plus a consistency force from the gap between the sampler's velocity and the ideal forward velocity of its own samples. Joint training cancels it across samples, not per state, and $\lvert2q-1\rvert$ grows as noise falls.
  • NFT's loss is guidance plus $\beta^2\lVert v_\theta-v_{\text{old}}\rVert^2$. On-policy, the restoration term is zero; EMA keeps it alive, and that, not off-policy data, is what stabilizes NFT.
  • DiffusionART aggregates first: $G(c)=\overline{\operatorname{Cov}}(R,x_0\mid c)/\operatorname{RMS}$, the normalized first-order reward tilt of the endpoint mean. It adds $G(c)$ to the cached velocity of every visited state. The target never uses $u_t$, so the consistency force is absent by construction.
  • A second disjoint-minibatch update adds restoration $-d$ proportional to each state's drift, with no EMA schedule. A KL-proxy trust-region mask drops only large outward moves.
  • Pairwise labels plug into the same covariance. At $K=2$, $G$ points from loser to winner and equals the scalar-reward $G$.
  • The large-scale evidence is strong on efficiency: 12.02× and 11.72× fewer GPU hours on T2V (1:1), and 4.8× on I2V. The mechanistic claims are supported by targeted ablations but not by direct measurement of the consistency force. In a multimodal 2D toy, a shared prompt-level direction is a weak instrument at larger $K$.

Why read the implementation at all

Steps 1 to 4 gave you the algorithm as three equations: a covariance guidance $G(c)$, a frozen target $v_{\text{tgt}} = v_\theta^{(0)} + G(c)$ on visited ODE states, and two disjoint MSE updates with a trust-region mask. The paper's Algorithm 1 fits in eight lines. The repository that produced the paper's numbers is roughly 17,000 lines of Python. Most of that code is not the algorithm. It is model adapters, reward wrappers, asset preparation, distributed bookkeeping, checkpointing and evaluation, and it encodes dozens of decisions that the paper does not mention: a sign convention, an $\varepsilon$ floor, an unbiased estimator, a gradient clip, a loss denominator that ignores microbatching, a rule for prompts whose $K$ rewards are all equal.

Those decisions matter when you reproduce a curve, compare against a baseline, or port the method into your own stack. This chapter is a guided tour. We start from a map, trace one iteration of the CPU smoke test with real printed tensors, read the three core files line by line, and finish with commands for real GPU runs, a debugging plan, intuition-building experiments and porting notes for a Megatron + SGLang style framework.

From LLM RL

The shape of the code will feel familiar: a rollout phase that produces a cache, a scoring phase, an advantage-like statistic computed per prompt group, and a training phase with a fixed number of optimizer steps per rollout. The differences are in what the cache holds (continuous latent tensors and velocities, not token ids and logprobs), what the "advantage" is (a direction in latent space shared by all states of a prompt, not a scalar per sample), and what the loss is (a regression, not a ratio objective).

Repository map

The repository installs as the package visual-diffusion-rl with one console script, visual-rl, that points to visual_rl.train:main. Everything under src/visual_rl/ splits into four layers: pure kernels with no model knowledge, model-independent training plumbing, backends that own a concrete model family, and the supporting cast (models, rewards, evaluation, assets).

The split between algorithms/ and training/ is the most useful thing to internalize. A kernel like art_loss never sees a model, an optimizer or a rank. It receives a batch of current predictions and frozen tensors and returns a per-item loss vector of shape [N]. The caller owns the denominator, the microbatching and the optimizer boundary. This is the same separation a good LLM RL framework draws between "compute per-token loss" and "reduce and step".

  • Windows. Each window holds 12 items, one per (trajectory, cached timestep) pair: 4 trajectories × 3 timesteps. Window 2 starts at trajectory 4, so in this fixture window 1 is all of prompt 0 and window 2 is all of prompt 1. We come back to this split later.
  • Update 1 loss is exactly the mean of $t$. At update 1 the parameters equal $\theta_{\text{old}}$, so $v_\theta = v_\theta^{(0)}$ on every cached state and the residual is exactly $G(c)$, whose MSE is $\operatorname{rms}(G)^2 = 1$. The loss is the $t$-weighted mean: $\tfrac13(1 + 0.75 + 0.5) = 0.75$. This identity is a free sanity check on any real run (see the GPU section).
  • Update 2. The first update moved the shared MLP, so on window 2's states the drift $d$ is nonzero. The KL proxy reaches $1.41\times10^{-5}$, above $\delta=10^{-5}$, and 1 of 12 items is masked. The loss fell to $0.708$ because the displacement $d$ already points partly along $G$, so $\lVert G-d\rVert^2 < \lVert G\rVert^2$ on most items.

How the rollout works

The sampler and the cache

collect_rollout in training/rollout.py is the single rollout function used by ART and, with stochastic steps enabled, by the GRPO family. For ART every step is deterministic Euler. The model is called once per step per microbatch, the transition is applied, and if the step index is one of the selected training timesteps, the input state, the predicted velocity, the next state and the transition mean are copied to CPU in fp32.

Variable to paper mapping: noise is $x_1 = \epsilon$; state at loop index $i$ is $x_{t_i}$, with t = sigmas[index] $=t_i$; velocity is the cached rollout prediction, the paper's $v_\theta^{(0)}(s_t)$ up to sign (next callout); states[row, slot] is the visited state $s_t$; endpoints is $x_0^{(i)}$. Microbatching here is purely physical: inputs and innovations are fixed before the loop, so the cache does not depend on sampling.microbatch.

Pitfall: the code's velocity has the opposite sign

The repo follows diffusers/SD3: the network predicts $\tilde v = \epsilon - x_0$, and flow_transition integrates with mean = x + dt * v where dt = sigma_next - sigma is negative. Writing $h = t - t_{\text{next}} > 0$, the code's step is $x_{t-h} = x_t - h\tilde v$. The paper's step is $x_{t-h} = x_t + h v$ with $v = x_0 - \epsilon$. So everywhere in the code $\tilde v = -v$, dt $= -h$, the cached old_velocities are $-v_\theta^{(0)}$, and a code target old - G is the paper's $v_\theta^{(0)} + G$. Keep this one substitution in mind and every sign in art.py lines up with the paper.

What is reused, and what it costs in memory

ART's efficiency claim (README, "Reuse rollout predictions") is that training re-uses the rollout states and their old predictions. NFT, by contrast, re-noises each endpoint to fresh states and needs an extra no-grad forward of the old policy on those new states. In ART the only model calls per iteration are the rollout forwards (no grad) and one forward plus backward of the current policy per trained (trajectory, timestep) item.

The price is CPU memory. The cache holds four tensors of shape $[N_{\text{rank}}, S, \text{latent}]$ in fp32: states, old_velocities, next_states, old_means. A worked example for the two paper recipes:

covariance.py: computing $G(c)$

Recall the paper's estimator (§4.1):

\[ \overline{\operatorname{Cov}}(R,x_0\mid c)=\frac1K\sum_{i=1}^{K}(R_i-\bar R_c)\,(x_0^{(i)}-\bar x_{0,c}), \qquad G(c)=\frac{\overline{\operatorname{Cov}}}{\operatorname{rms}(\overline{\operatorname{Cov}})}, \qquad \operatorname{rms}(a)=\sqrt{\tfrac1D\textstyle\sum_j a_j^2}. \]

Where the code adds detail to the paper

  • The MAD pre-division and the $\varepsilon$ floor. In the default direction_mode="historical", the covariance is first divided by the reward MAD and only then RMS-normalized with a floor: $G = S/\max(\operatorname{rms}(S),\varepsilon)$ with $S = \overline{\operatorname{Cov}}/\text{MAD}$. Dividing by MAD first makes the floor independent of the reward scale: multiplying all rewards by 100 changes neither $S$ nor whether the floor binds. When $\operatorname{rms}(S) \geq 10^{-5}$ (essentially always), the result equals the paper's $\overline{\operatorname{Cov}}/\operatorname{rms}(\overline{\operatorname{Cov}})$ exactly, because the scalar MAD cancels. direction_mode="normalized_aggregate" skips MAD and normalizes in float64; in the toy experiments below both modes give bit-identical training.
  • Second normalization. art_loss RMS-normalizes the direction again (direction_normalization="rms"). If the floor ever did bind, this second normalization restores unit RMS. The final displacement is magnitude * direction / rms(direction).
  • Exact zero signal. Runners always pass zero_signal_mode="exact" for scalar ART (sd3.py L917, hunyuan.py L241). If all $K$ rewards of a prompt are bit-identical, summing them in fp32 can still round the mean away from the common value, making centered tiny but nonzero and producing a pure-noise direction after normalization. The exact mode zeroes such groups.
  • Dtype. Endpoints and rewards are cast to fp32 here even when the model runs in bf16 or fp16. FLUX with bf16 casts the directions back to bf16 before the loss (sd3.py L920–923) to reproduce a historical run.

The pairwise variant

DiffusionART-Pairwise (§4.4 and Appendix) replaces $R_i$ by labels $y_i \in \{0, \tfrac12, 1\}$ from disjoint pairs, then calls the same scalar kernel:

Mapping: pair is a seeded random partition of the group's $K$ rows into $M=K/2$ pairs; labels are $y_i$; the scalar kernel then computes $\overline{\operatorname{Cov}}(y, x_0\mid c)$, which by the paper's pairwise identity is proportional to $\sum_{j\,\text{non-tied}}(x^{\text{win}}_{0,j}-x^{\text{lose}}_{0,j})$, aggregated over all pairs of the prompt before normalization (prompt-global, not pair-local, guidance). The seed is seed + 1000003·(round + group_offset + group), private to the call, so resuming reproduces the same pairs.

One implementation detail goes beyond Algorithm 2 in the paper. The algorithm says every state of prompt $c$ gets target $v^{(0)}+G(c)$. In the code, row_guidance_magnitudes is multiplied by preference, so rows that belong to a tied pair get zero guidance. They become row_restore_anchor rows instead, which train toward $v^{(0)}$ itself (pure restoration), but only if some non-tied pair exists somewhere on any rank (any_preference_active, MAX-reduced across ranks in training/video_pairwise.py). Pairwise is wired only into the Hunyuan backend and requires VQ-only fixed-normalized scores.

art.py: frozen targets and the trust-region mask

art_loss takes the current prediction for a batch of items and everything else frozen. The first half builds the frozen target.

With the paper's recipe (loss_space="velocity_linear_sigma") only the else branch at L90–93 runs. Substitute the sign convention ($\tilde v=-v$):

\[ \texttt{target} = \tilde v^{(0)}_\theta - G = -\bigl(v^{(0)}_\theta + G(c)\bigr) = -v^{\text{ART}}_{\text{tgt}},\qquad \texttt{residual} = \texttt{target}-\tilde v_\theta = v_\theta - v^{\text{ART}}_{\text{tgt}},\qquad \lVert\texttt{residual}\rVert^2=\lVert v_\theta-v^{\text{ART}}_{\text{tgt}}\rVert^2 . \]

So unmasked = weight * mean(residual**2) (first line of the next quote) is exactly $w\,\operatorname{MSE}(v_\theta, v^{\text{ART}}_{\text{tgt}})$ with $w=t$ before masking. Mapping of the rest: v $=\tilde v_\theta(s_t)$ (graph-connected), old $=\tilde v_\theta^{(0)}(s_t)$, s $=t$, raw/direction $=G(c)$, magnitude $=1$, displacement $=G(c)$, weight $=t$. The x0 loss space is an alternative that regresses $\hat x_0 = x_t - t\tilde v$ onto $\hat x_0^{(0)} + G$; it is not used by the paper recipes.

The second half implements the mask of Equation (art_trust_mask):

Mapping, again with $\tilde v=-v$ and dt $=-h$:

  • movement = v - old $= -d(s_t)$, the drift since the rollout; desired_step = residual $= -(G-d)$. Hence alignment $=\tfrac1D\langle -d, -(G-d)\rangle = \tfrac1D\langle d, G-d\rangle$, the paper's outwardness test.
  • For the default velocity_mask_metric="scheduler_reference_metric" (identical to "q2_sde_kl"), L115–122 compute std$^2 = \nu^2\tfrac{t}{1-t}h = \sigma^2_{\text{ref}}(t)$ and coefficient $= -h(1+\nu^2/2)$, so
    \[ \texttt{energy} = \frac{h^2(1+\nu^2/2)^2\,\tfrac1D\lVert d\rVert^2}{2\,\sigma^2_{\text{ref}}(t)} = \frac{\lVert \mu(s_t,v_\theta)-\mu(s_t,v^{(0)}_\theta)\rVert^2}{2D\sigma^2_{\text{ref}}(t)} = m(s_t), \]
    the per-coordinate KL proxy. At $t=1$ (initial) the endpoint convention $\sigma_{\text{ref}}^2=\nu^2$, coefficient $-h$ is used, as in the paper's appendix. noise $=\nu=0.8$, threshold $=\delta=10^{-5}$.
  • masked $=\mathbf 1\{m\ge\delta\}\,\mathbf 1\{\langle d,G-d\rangle>0\}$, and the final weight is $t\,[1-\text{masked}]$, which is the paper's $w(s_t)$.

Details the code adds

  • The mask cannot fire on update 1. At update 1, movement is zero, so energy is zero (the trace printed $2.5\times10^{-16}$). The mask is effectively a second-update mechanism, exactly as the paper's diagnostics section says.
  • Activity and anchors. Rows with zero guidance (active false) are dropped from the loss unless flagged as restore anchors. Dropped rows stay in the denominator: the caller divides by the original item count. Video recipes set restore_zero_signal: true, so constant-reward prompts become anchors that train toward $v^{(0)}$; image recipes set it false.
  • Everything frozen is detached, including the mask (.detach() on L134–135), so the mask is a constant during backpropagation, as the appendix specifies.
  • Optional reference MSE (reference_prediction, reference_mse_beta) exists for a KL-like regularizer; paper recipes use none.

objectives.py and update.py: two disjoint updates

The kernel is connected to a model by ARTObjective, a callable that maps a list of (row, slot) items to a per-item loss vector:

Mapping: items is a set of states $s_t\in B$; state, sigma, old are read from the rollout cache; velocity = self.predict(...) is the only graph-connected tensor; dt is $-h$ for the cached step; self.row_directions[rows] broadcasts the prompt's $G(c)$ to each of its states. The two flags matter for FLUX and Hunyuan: Hunyuan reconstructs the old velocity from the cached transition mean, old = (old_mean - state)/dt, because the next state is rounded to bf16 in its sampler.

disjoint_windows(rows, S, updates=2) splits trajectories into two contiguous halves and puts all $S$ cached timesteps of a trajectory in the same window. In the paper's notation, $B_1$ and $B_2$ are disjoint sets of trajectories. How rows are ordered before the split differs by backend:

  • Tensor fixture and Hunyuan use the rows in prompt-major order. With several prompts per rank, window 1 holds the first half of the prompts and window 2 the second half. With one prompt per rank (Hunyuan on 32 GPUs), each window holds $K/2$ videos of that prompt.
  • SD3/FLUX shuffle the rows with a seeded randperm first (_art_update, quoted below), so each window holds a random half of the trajectories. The README recommends splitting within each prompt ($K/2$ per prompt per window) for better prompt coverage and states explicitly that this is "not a guarantee of every configuration's current split implementation".

Finally accumulated_step turns a window into exactly one optimizer step, however many physical microbatches it takes:

Each microbatch then does (losses.sum() * scale).backward() (L181–182) with scale = world / total; DDP averages gradients over world ranks, so the effective gradient is $\nabla \tfrac{1}{N_{\text{global}}}\sum_{s_t} w\,\operatorname{MSE}$, the paper's $\mathcal L(B;\theta)$ with $|B|$ counted over all ranks. After the backward passes it clips the gradient norm to training.grad_clip (1.0 in every recipe; not in Algorithm 1) and calls optimizer.step() (AdamW, weight decay $10^{-4}$ in paper recipes). For fp16 SD3 a GradScaler replays the same window on overflow.

Key idea

Microbatch size is a memory knob; the window is an algorithm knob. Halving training.microbatch produces bit-for-bit the same gradient (up to float summation order). Halving the window (more updates per rollout) changes the algorithm: it creates more chances for drift and restoration per rollout, which is precisely the dynamics the two-update design controls.

Checklist for a fair comparison

  • Start from the paper recipes in configs/paper/image/ or configs/paper/video/, which pin $P$, $K$, steps, learning rate, LoRA rank and evaluation cadence per method. The configs/examples/ files are four-update integration checks (the SD3 example even uses CFG 7, which the paper does not).
  • Compare on cost/training_gpu_hours as well as train/optimizer_step; methods differ in rollouts per optimizer step (NFT and ReFL: 1 update per rollout, the rest: 2).
  • Keep $P$, $K$ and the number of decoded samples per update fixed when the question is "which objective", and vary them only when the question is sample efficiency.
  • NFT has two recipes per benchmark: *_nft_ema (the tuned EMA schedule) and *_nft_onpolicy (old_policy: {rate: 0, maximum: 0}). The paper's stability claim is about the second.

The config system

Configs are YAML with three features: extends: (a path or list, merged left to right, child last), environment-variable references (a dollar sign followed by {NAME}, as in the paper recipes' model paths) that fail loudly when the variable is unset, and strict schemas (unknown keys and duplicate keys are rejected). Changing algorithm.name, model.name or reward.name in a child replaces that whole section, so options of the parent algorithm never leak into another algorithm (config.py L40–60). reward.weights is always replaced, never merged.

Pitfall: the 2026-10-14 microbatch fix

The README changelog records that Hunyuan paper recipes once inherited microbatch: 1 from the example for both rollout and update, instead of 8 (ReFL stays 1/1). That is why the paper recipe sets sampling.microbatch: 8 and training.microbatch: 8 explicitly (lines 13–18 above). With 8 videos and 16 steps per rank, microbatch 1 means 128 batch-1 transformer calls per rollout instead of 16 batch-8 calls, and 36 instead of 5 backward calls per window. The math is identical (the denominator is the window size), but GPU-hours, the paper's main metric, are not. Use a checkout that contains commit 0fb23a7 or later.

Porting notes for an LLM RL framework

If you maintain a Megatron + SGLang style stack, this is what a diffusion integration needs, in order of surprise.

  • Rollout engine. Generation is a fixed-length loop of $T$ full-sequence transformer calls on a latent tensor, with no KV cache and no sampling. The engine must return, per sample, the selected intermediate states and predicted velocities, not just the final output. Batching is trivial (all samples have the same shape); CFG, if used, doubles the batch.
  • Data plane. You ship latents, not token ids: about 1.4 GiB of fp32 cache per SD3 rank, 0.9 GiB per Hunyuan rank (worked out above). If the rollout engine and trainer are disaggregated, plan for tensor transport (NCCL or RDMA, pinned buffers), not JSON. Only states and old_velocities are needed for ART, so you can halve the repo's footprint; bf16 storage halves it again at some fidelity risk.
  • No logprobs, but you still need parity. ART needs no transition likelihood, so the classic rollout/training logprob mismatch disappears. It is replaced by a velocity mismatch: if the engine's $v^{(0)}$ differs from the trainer's forward at $\theta_{\text{old}}$, update 1 already sees a spurious drift $d\neq0$, which biases the target and can trip the mask. Either make the forwards bitwise aligned, or recompute $v^{(0)}$ in the trainer (one extra no-grad forward per item, which is what ART was designed to avoid).
  • Reward services. Rewards need a VAE decode, then a scorer (PickScore, a VLM-based VideoReward, OCR or detector workers). These look like generative reward models in LLM RL: GPU-heavy, best run as separate services with their own batching. The repo encodes videos to MP4 before scoring, so the codec is part of the reward.
  • Group placement. Guidance needs all $K$ endpoints of a prompt in one place. The repo keeps each prompt's group on one data-parallel rank and computes $G(c)$ locally; this is cheaper than gathering endpoint tensors across ranks, and it is the analogue of computing GRPO group advantages, except the statistic is a $D$-dimensional vector.
  • Loss and steps. Per-item MSE with a fixed denominator, two optimizer steps per rollout over disjoint trajectory halves, global grad clip. In a Megatron trainer this is a custom loss function plus "two minibatches per rollout"; data and pipeline parallelism need nothing special. The covariance and target can be precomputed on the rollout side and shipped as a per-sample target, so the trainer only sees (state, $t$, condition, target, weight).
  • Old-policy shadows. ART needs no old-policy weights at all. NFT baselines do: training/fsdp_shadow.py keeps a CPU copy of each rank's FSDP local shards and swaps them in for old-policy forwards, without ever materializing a second full model on GPU. The Megatron equivalent is a CPU copy of the local TP/PP shards.
  • Weight sync. After both updates, push weights to the rollout engine as usual; with LoRA (image recipes) only the adapters change.

Pitfalls collected

Pitfall: computing guidance on a microbatch

The covariance must use the complete group of $K$ endpoints. art_scalar_covariance's docstring says "Complete-group covariance, never a microbatch estimator", and the distributed runners keep groups rank-local. If you shard a group across ranks or compute $G$ per update window, you get a noisier and differently normalized direction, and the two windows no longer share one frozen target.

Pitfall: examples are not recipes

configs/examples/*.yaml are four-update integration checks with deliberately small $P$, $K$ and sometimes different sampling (CFG 7 in the SD3 example, microbatch 1 in Hunyuan). Comparisons belong in configs/paper/, and even those are labeled as candidates, not certified reproductions (see each file's provenance block).

Pitfall: world size and P

sampling.prompts_per_rollout is global and must be divisible by the number of workers (train.py L114–117). Doubling GPUs with the same $P$ halves the per-rank groups, which changes how the two windows are composed in prompt-major backends but not the guidance itself.

Exercises

Exercise 5.1 — Find every place where $K$ enters the guidance computation. Does the magnitude of $G(c)$ depend on $K$?

In art_scalar_covariance, $K$ is counts (L66). It enters at three places: the group means (L69, L70), the MAD (L80, divided by counts) and the covariance denominator (L82, counts - 1 for the unbiased reduction). The direction is signal / signal_rms (L103) with signal = covariance / mad: any positive scalar that multiplies the covariance or the MAD cancels in this ratio, so both $1/(K-1)$ and the $1/K$ inside MAD disappear. The magnitude is the constant 1 for unit_normalized_covariance (L90), and art_loss normalizes again. So $\operatorname{rms}(G(c))=1$ for every $K\ge2$ whenever the group is active; $K$ affects only the estimator's noise (which direction you get). Outside the kernel, $K$ also enters disjoint_windows (rows $=PK$ must split evenly), the loss denominator (items per window $= PK S/2$) and, for pairwise, the pair partition (group_size, which must be 2, 4 or 8). With scale_mode: raw_covariance, the magnitude becomes rms, which does depend on $K$ through $1/(K-1)$.

Exercise 5.2 — Modify the CPU smoke config to run Flow-GRPO-Fast. Why is the second update's gradient almost zero?

Create a child config next to the example (paths in extends are relative to the child file) and run it:

extends: cpu_smoke.yaml
name: cpu-smoke-fast
algorithm: flow_grpo_fast
runtime:
  output_dir: outputs/cpu-smoke-fast
uv run --locked visual-rl --config configs/examples/cpu_smoke_fast.yaml

Because the algorithm name differs from the parent's, the whole algorithm section is replaced (no ART options leak in). The fixture then samples with one Flow-SDE step at index 0 and ODE elsewhere (tensor_fixture.py L163–165), computes advantages with group_advantages(mode="global_population_std", eps=1e-4), and uses clipped_grpo_loss with ratio_clip=1e-5 (L196–211). The real run above logged gradient norms 0.309, 0.0, 0.226, 0.0023. At update 1 all ratios are exactly 1 and the gradient is $-\nabla\tfrac1N\sum A_i\rho_i$. After one AdamW step, almost every ratio has moved by more than $10^{-5}$. The PPO surrogate $\max(-A\rho, -A\operatorname{clip}(\rho))$ has zero gradient whenever $\rho$ moved past the clip boundary in the advantage's favorable direction, which is what the first update just did. So the second window is almost entirely clipped. This is the LLM-RL intuition carried over unchanged, and it shows how conservative $10^{-5}$ is; the paper's credit-assignment argument is that this clipped, per-transition signal is a slow way to move a continuous policy.

Exercise 5.3 — All $K$ rewards of every prompt are equal. Trace what happens to guidance, loss and the optimizer in an image run and in a video run.

Kernel. Runners pass zero_signal_mode="exact". In art_scalar_covariance, maxima == minima marks the group constant (L73–75), so centered is forced to exactly 0 (L77). Then mad is 0 and covariance is 0, so active is false (L94), magnitudes become 0 (L96) and directions become 0 (L108). Without exact mode, the fp32 mean of identical values could differ from them by one ulp, giving a nonzero centered whose normalized direction would be pure rounding noise; exact mode exists to prevent that.

Loss. In art_loss, active is false for every item (L127). Unless an item is a restore anchor, keep is false and its per_item_loss is 0 (L135–136), while it still counts in the denominator.

Image runner. Image recipes use restore_zero_signal: false, so no anchors. ARTObjective.has_guidance is false; sd3.py (L837–845) logs system/skipped_zero_guidance: 1 with the rewards, does not step the optimizer, does not advance train/optimizer_step, and samples a new rollout. Under DDP the flag is MAX-reduced, so a rank with no signal still runs both windows when another rank has signal (image_ddp.py L357–358). NoSignalGuard warns at exponentially spaced counts and, if runtime.max_consecutive_no_signal_rollouts is set (default: unset, no limit), stops training after that many consecutive skips.

Video runner. Hunyuan recipes use restore_zero_signal: true, so the inactive rows become anchors (hunyuan.py L242). For an anchor the displacement is 0, so the target is the old velocity and the loss is $t\,\operatorname{MSE}(v_\theta, v^{(0)})$. On update 1 this is 0 with zero gradient; on update 2 it pulls the drift caused by update 1 back toward $v^{(0)}$: pure restoration, no guidance. The windows are executed (anchor-only rollouts are not skipped).

Mixed case. If only some prompts are constant, the active prompts train normally and the constant ones contribute zeros that still dilute the mean, so train/loss on update 1 equals mean $t$ times the active fraction. That is a way to read the fraction of saturated prompts off the loss curve.

Exercise 5.4 — Add train/art/masked_fraction and a drift metric to metrics.jsonl.

art_loss already computes the values; ARTObjective.__call__ discards them. Keep a list on the objective and reduce it after each window. The smallest change to training/objectives.py:

@dataclass
class ARTObjective:
    ...
    reconstruct_old_from_mean: bool = False
    diagnostics: list = field(default_factory=list)          # new

    def __call__(self, items):
        ...
        terms = art_loss(...)                                # unchanged
        self.diagnostics.append({k: terms[k].detach().cpu()  # new
                                 for k in ("masked", "movement_score")})
        return terms["per_item_loss"]

    def pop_metrics(self):                                   # new
        masked = torch.cat([d["masked"] for d in self.diagnostics]).float()
        score = torch.cat([d["movement_score"] for d in self.diagnostics])
        self.diagnostics.clear()
        return {"train/art/masked_fraction": masked.mean().item(),
                "train/art/movement_score_mean": score.mean().item()}

Then in the runner, after each accumulated_step for ART, call metrics.update(objective.pop_metrics()) before writing the line. Under DDP or FSDP, all-reduce the sums and counts before dividing, otherwise each rank logs its local fraction and only rank 0's is exported. Exercise 5.5 — Show that DiffusionART-Pairwise with $K=2$ produces exactly the scalar guidance (when the pair is not tied), and explain the toy result where both gave $-0.9960$.

For one prompt with endpoints $x^{(1)}, x^{(2)}$ and rewards $R_1 > R_2$, the scalar covariance with the code's unbiased reduction is $\tfrac{1}{1}\bigl[(R_1-\bar R)x^{(1)} + (R_2-\bar R)x^{(2)}\bigr] = \tfrac{R_1-R_2}{2}(x^{(1)}-x^{(2)})$. Pairwise labels are $y_1=1, y_2=0$, giving $\tfrac12(x^{(1)}-x^{(2)})$. Both are positive multiples of $x^{(1)}-x^{(2)}$, so after RMS normalization they are identical: the winner-minus-loser direction, as the paper's appendix states. With $K=2$ there is exactly one pair, so preference is true for both rows whenever $R_1\ne R_2$, and row_guidance_magnitudes equals the scalar magnitude 1. The only differences are a tie (scalar: exact zero signal; pairwise: anchors if some other prompt has a preference) and the pair-seeding RNG, which consumes no global RNG state. Hence the two toy runs performed the same arithmetic on the same random stream and printed the same $-0.9960 \pm 0.0724$. For $K\ge4$ the two differ: pairwise uses only within-pair order, so a sample that wins its pair narrowly counts as much as one that wins by a lot, and cross-pair differences are ignored.

Takeaways

  • The algorithm lives in three pure kernels: art_scalar_covariance/art_pairwise_covariance ($G(c)$), art_loss (target, $t$-weighting, mask) and disjoint_windows + accumulated_step (two optimizer steps with a fixed denominator). Backends choose the model; algorithm.name chooses the branch.
  • The code's velocity is $\tilde v = \epsilon - x_0 = -v$ and dt is negative. With that substitution, target = old - G is the paper's $v^{(0)}+G$, and the mask's energy and alignment are exactly $m(s_t)$ and $\langle d, G-d\rangle$.
  • The code adds an unbiased $K-1$ denominator, a MAD pre-division with a $10^{-5}$ floor, a second RMS normalization, exact zero-signal gating, restore anchors for video and tied pairs, grad clip 1.0, and fp32 statistics. None of these change the paper's math in the normal regime.
  • Update 1's loss equals the mean trained $t$ times the active fraction; this is a free health check. The mask can only fire on update 2.
  • The rollout cache (states and old velocities, fp32, on CPU) is the price of skipping NFT's extra old-policy forward: about 1.4 GiB per SD3 rank, 0.9 GiB per Hunyuan rank.
  • Microbatch sizes are memory knobs; the window count is an algorithm knob. Drift and mask fraction are not logged by default.

Putting it together

The whole method fits on one screen once the pieces are in place. Use this section as a review sheet and a self-test.

The argument in eight sentences

  1. A rectified-flow model generates by integrating a learned velocity field from noise ($t=1$) to data ($t=0$), and it is trained by regressing the per-sample target $u_t=x_0-\epsilon$.
  2. RL fine-tuning treats each sampler step as an action and scores only the final sample, so one scalar must be spread over many steps.
  3. Reverse-process RL (Flow-GRPO) turns the ODE into an SDE to get step likelihoods, then pushes each step along its own sampled noise, scaled by a trajectory-level advantage. That is noisy credit assignment.
  4. Forward-process RL (DiffusionNFT) re-noises endpoints and regresses velocities toward reward-weighted targets. It needs no likelihoods and learns fast, but each branch alone collapses and the joint version needs a tuned EMA.
  5. The reason: NFT's targets assume the model's sampling velocity equals the ideal forward velocity of its own endpoints. The gap between the two adds a force unrelated to reward that the two branches only partly cancel.
  6. EMA helps because the lagged model pulls the trainable one back (restoration), not because the data is off-policy.
  7. DiffusionART instead aggregates each prompt's $K$ endpoints and rewards into one direction, $G(c)\propto\operatorname{Cov}(R,x_0)$, which is the first-order effect of tilting $p_{\text{old}}$ by $e^{\tau R}$, and adds it to the cached velocity at every visited state. No forward velocity appears, so the consistency force is gone by construction.
  8. Two disjoint MSE updates toward the same frozen target make the second update contain a restoration term $-d(s_t)$ proportional to each state's drift, and a KL-proxy mask stops outward drift beyond a threshold.

Self-test

You are ready to work on this area when you can answer these without notes.

Why can DiffusionART sample with a deterministic ODE when Flow-GRPO cannot?

Flow-GRPO's objective is a likelihood ratio of sampler steps, and a deterministic step has no density, so it must inject noise (SDE). DiffusionART's loss is a velocity regression toward a frozen target, which needs no step likelihood. It borrows the SDE only as a reference to measure drift in the trust-region mask, and the rollout itself stays an ODE.

What does $G(c)$ become when all $K$ rewards for a prompt are equal, and what does the update do then?

The covariance is zero, so there is no reward signal for that prompt. Step 5 traces how the code guards the RMS normalization in this case. With a zero guidance the target equals the cached velocity, so the first update has zero loss on those states and the second update only restores drift toward $v_\theta^{(0)}$.

Why does the second update restore, while the first does not?

Before the first update $v_\theta=v_\theta^{(0)}$, so the residual to the target is exactly $G(c)$. After it, the model has moved by $d(s_t)$ and the residual is $G(c)-d(s_t)$; the $-d(s_t)$ part pulls each state back in proportion to how far it drifted, which is the restoration force EMA provided in NFT.

In one sentence, what is the forward–reverse consistency force?

It is the part of NFT's update that moves the model's sampling velocity toward (positive branch) or away from (negative branch) the ideal forward velocity induced by its own endpoints. That gap has nothing to do with reward, and centered rewards cancel it across samples but not at each state.

Name two settings where you would expect DiffusionART's advantage to shrink, and why.

Examples: (1) rewards whose improvement direction differs strongly between noise levels or between regions of a prompt's sample space, because one shared $G(c)$ per prompt is a first-order, mean-shift signal; (2) very small $K$ with scalar rewards, where the covariance estimate is noisy (the paper's pairwise results with $K=2$ are the counterpoint to test). Step 4's critical-reading section lists more.
