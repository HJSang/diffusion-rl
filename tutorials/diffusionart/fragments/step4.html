The DiffusionART paper, section by section

After this step you can derive every equation in the paper, explain why NFT-style training needs both of its stabilizers and why DiffusionART needs neither, write the algorithm from memory, and judge which experimental claims are strongly supported and which are not.

The paper in one paragraph

Online diffusion RL has two families. Reverse-process methods such as Flow-GRPO treat each denoising step as an action, convert the ODE sampler into an SDE so that the step has a log-probability, and push every step of a trajectory by that trajectory's scalar advantage. They train on the states the sampler actually visits, but credit assignment is poor: each step is nudged along its own sampled noise, weighted by an advantage it only partly caused. Forward-process methods such as DiffusionNFT re-noise the finished samples and regress velocities toward reward-weighted targets. They need no likelihoods and learn fast, but they are unstable unless you train positive and negative branches jointly and roll out from a carefully tuned EMA copy of the policy. DiffusionART (Diffusion Reinforcement with Aggregation-First Reward Tilting) first explains both stabilizers. Joint training partially cancels a hidden forward–reverse consistency force; the EMA works through restoration toward a lagged reference, not through off-policy data. It then removes the consistency force by construction. It collapses each prompt's $K$ samples and rewards into one normalized direction $G(c)$, the empirical reward–endpoint covariance, and regresses every visited reverse-process state toward its cached velocity plus $G(c)$. It replaces the EMA with a second minibatch update whose restoration grows with drift, gated by a trust-region mask built from a KL proxy.

The abstract's headline claim, stated exactly: on HunyuanVideo-13B with equally weighted visual and motion quality rewards, DiffusionART reaches the late-stage mean reward levels of DiffusionNFT and Flow-GRPO-Fast with 12.02× and 11.72× fewer GPU hours, respectively, while achieving 28.8% and 53.0% higher mean training rewards. The contributions box adds that DiffusionART matches the strongest baseline's efficiency on SD3.5-M without EMA or per-task tuning, and that on image-to-video it reaches MQ 6 with 4.8× fewer GPU hours than Flow-GRPO-Fast. The paper also claims that DiffusionART needs no differentiable reward model, works with fewer samples per prompt, and supports pairwise reward models (DiffusionART-Pairwise).

The paper has four contributions, and this chapter follows them in order:

  1. Diagnosis 1 (§3.1): the NFT objective contains a consistency force that joint positive–negative training cancels only across samples, not at each state.
  2. Diagnosis 2 (§3.2): EMA stabilizes NFT through restoration, and its strength must be tuned per task.
  3. Method (§4): aggregation-first guidance (§4.1), two-update restoration (§4.2), a trust-region mask (§4.3), and a pairwise variant (§4.4).
  4. Evidence (§5): SD3.5-M, FLUX.1-dev, HunyuanVideo-13B and Wan2.1-I2V-14B.

From LLM RL

In GRPO, a sequence's scalar advantage $A_i$ multiplies the gradient of the log-probability of the tokens that sequence actually sampled. Bad samples have their own tokens pushed down. DiffusionART has no per-sample sign. Every state on every trajectory of prompt $c$, including the worst sample's trajectory, is pulled in the same reward-improving direction $G(c)$. The group is still the unit of comparison, as in GRPO, but the group's output is a vector in sample space rather than $K$ scalars. Nothing in LLM RL quite matches this, because you cannot add a vector to a discrete token. The closest analogy is distillation toward a reward-tilted target policy rather than policy-gradient ascent.

§3.1 The puzzle: each NFT branch alone collapses

Setup and symbols

Recall from Step 3 that DiffusionNFT samples $K$ endpoints per prompt from the frozen rollout policy $p_{\text{old}}(x_0\mid c)$ and maps each raw reward $R_i$ to a goodness

\[ r_i=\tfrac12\Bigl[1+\operatorname{clip}\Bigl(\tfrac{R_i-\bar R_c}{Z_c},-1,1\Bigr)\Bigr]\in[0,1], \]

where $\bar R_c$ is the prompt's mean reward and $Z_c>0$ a scale. Without clipping, $\sum_i(2r_i-1)=0$. Weighting $p_{\text{old}}$ by $r$ and by $1-r$ defines a positive and a negative endpoint distribution, $p_+\propto r\,p_{\text{old}}$ and $p_-\propto(1-r)\,p_{\text{old}}$. NFT builds $v_{\theta,\pm}=v_{\text{old}}\pm\beta(v_\theta-v_{\text{old}})$ and minimizes

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{NFT}}=\mathbb E_{\mathcal D_{\text{old}}}\bigl[r\lVert v_{\theta,+}-u_t\rVert^2+(1-r)\lVert v_{\theta,-}-u_t\rVert^2\bigr]=\beta^2\,\mathbb E\bigl[\lVert v_\theta-v_{\text{tgt}}\rVert^2\bigr]+\text{const},\qquad v_{\text{tgt}}=a\,u_t+(1-a)\,v_{\text{old}},\ a=\tfrac{2r-1}{\beta}. \]

$\mathcal D_{\text{old}}$ is the training distribution of $(c,x_0,r,t,\epsilon)$ with $x_0\sim p_{\text{old}}$ and $\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$. The states NFT trains on are forward-noised endpoints, $x_t=(1-t)x_0+t\epsilon$, not states the sampler visited.

The paper starts from an experiment. It removes EMA, so $v_\theta=v_{\text{old}}$ at every update, and trains SD3.5-M on GenEval with only the positive branch, only the negative branch, or both. Figure 2(a) of the paper shows the outcome. Both single-branch runs fall from about 0.2 to roughly zero GenEval within the first handful of steps and stay there. The joint run climbs to about 0.9 by step 100. This is puzzling, because DiffusionNFT is motivated by the fact that the positive, original and negative ideal velocity fields are collinear. If they all point the same way, either branch alone should suffice.

To make "ideal" precise, the paper introduces, for $b\in\{+,\text{old},-\}$:

  • $P_b$: the endpoint distribution $p_b(x_0\mid c)$.
  • $Q_b$: the joint forward law of $(x_0,\epsilon,x_t,u_t)$, obtained by drawing $x_0\sim P_b$ and $\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$ independently, then setting $x_t=(1-t)x_0+t\epsilon$ and $u_t=x_0-\epsilon$.
  • $s_t=(x_t,t,c)$: a state.
  • $v^b_{\text{fwd}}(s_t):=\mathbb E_{Q_b}[u_t\mid s_t]$: the ideal forward velocity induced by $P_b$. This is the field that flow matching would learn if trained to convergence on samples of $P_b$ (Step 1).
  • $v^{\text{old}}_{\text{rvs}}:=v_{\text{old}}$: the field the reverse sampler actually integrates to produce $P_{\text{old}}$.

If the model were a perfect flow-matching fit of its own samples, $v^{\text{old}}_{\text{rvs}}=v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}}$. The paper calls this forward–reverse compatibility. After even one RL update, nothing enforces it.

Lemma 1: ideal NFT guidance is a conditional covariance

Fix a state $s_t$. Define the statewise expected goodness and the statewise reward–velocity covariance

\[ q:=\mathbb E_{Q_{\text{old}}}[r(x_0,c)\mid s_t],\qquad g:=\operatorname{Cov}_{Q_{\text{old}}}[r(x_0,c),u_t\mid s_t]. \]

$q$ answers the question "given what I can see in this noisy state, how good do I expect the endpoint to be?" $g$ is the direction in velocity space along which good endpoints differ from bad ones, among endpoints consistent with this state. Lemma 1 says that, if $0<q<1$,

\[ q\bigl(v^+_{\text{fwd}}-v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}}\bigr)=(1-q)\bigl(v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}}-v^-_{\text{fwd}}\bigr)=g. \]

So the step from the old field to the positive field and the step from the negative field to the old field both point along $g$; they differ only in length ($g/q$ versus $g/(1-q)$).

Proof, step by step (paper, Appendix B, "Conditional Covariance Identity for Ideal NFT").

  1. Reweighting the endpoint reweights the whole forward tuple. $P_+$ is $P_{\text{old}}$ reweighted by $r(x_0,c)/Z_+(c)$ with $Z_+=\mathbb E_{P_{\text{old}}}[r\mid c]$. Because $\epsilon$ is drawn independently of $x_0$ and $(x_t,u_t)$ are deterministic functions of $(x_0,\epsilon)$, the same factor reweights the joint law: $Q_+(\cdot\mid t,c)=\tfrac{r(x_0,c)}{Z_+(c)}\,Q_{\text{old}}(\cdot\mid t,c)$. Likewise $Q_-=\tfrac{1-r}{Z_-}Q_{\text{old}}$ with $Z_-=\mathbb E_{P_{\text{old}}}[1-r\mid c]$.
  2. Marginalize onto $x_t$. Integrating the reweighting factor over everything except $x_t$ replaces $r(x_0,c)$ by its conditional mean given $x_t$, which is $q$: $Q_+(x_t\mid t,c)=\tfrac{q}{Z_+}Q_{\text{old}}(x_t\mid t,c)$ and $Q_-(x_t\mid t,c)=\tfrac{1-q}{Z_-}Q_{\text{old}}(x_t\mid t,c)$.
  3. Bayes' rule. Divide step 1 by step 2. The normalizers $Z_\pm$ cancel, and the posterior over endpoints given the state becomes $Q_+(\cdot\mid s_t)=\tfrac{r}{q}Q_{\text{old}}(\cdot\mid s_t)$ and $Q_-(\cdot\mid s_t)=\tfrac{1-r}{1-q}Q_{\text{old}}(\cdot\mid s_t)$. In words: among the endpoints compatible with $s_t$, the positive distribution up-weights each one by its goodness relative to the local average goodness.
  4. Take conditional expectations of $u_t$. $q\,v^+_{\text{fwd}}=\mathbb E_{Q_{\text{old}}}[r\,u_t\mid s_t]$ and $(1-q)\,v^-_{\text{fwd}}=\mathbb E_{Q_{\text{old}}}[(1-r)\,u_t\mid s_t]$.
  5. Subtract $v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}}=\mathbb E_{Q_{\text{old}}}[u_t\mid s_t]$. $q(v^+_{\text{fwd}}-v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}})=\mathbb E[r u_t\mid s_t]-\mathbb E[r\mid s_t]\,\mathbb E[u_t\mid s_t]=g$. The negative side is the same algebra with $1-r$, and $\operatorname{Cov}(1-r,u_t)=-g$, which gives $(1-q)(v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}}-v^-_{\text{fwd}})=g$. ∎

The forward–reverse gap and Proposition 1

Define the gap at a state as

\[ e:=v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}}(s_t)-v^{\text{old}}_{\text{rvs}}(s_t). \]

$e$ measures how far the sampler's velocity is from the velocity that flow matching on the sampler's own outputs would produce. Proposition 1 computes the NFT force at the pure on-policy point $v_\theta=v^{\text{old}}_{\text{rvs}}$. Here "force" means the negative output-space gradient, normalized by $2\beta$:

\[ \mathcal F_{\text{NFT}}(s_t):=-\frac{1}{2\beta}\nabla_{v_\theta(s_t)}\mathcal L_{\text{NFT}}(s_t)\Big|_{v_\theta=v^{\text{old}}_{\text{rvs}}}=\underbrace{2g}_{\text{reward-improving}}+\underbrace{(2q-1)\,e}_{\text{consistency force}}. \]

Proof, step by step (Appendix B, "NFT Force and Restoration Decompositions"). Two short computations are needed.

(i) The MSE form and the per-sample force. Write $d=v_\theta-v_{\text{old}}$ (drift), $h=u_t-v_{\text{old}}$ (training residual) and $A=2r-1$. The positive residual is $v_{\theta,+}-u_t=\beta d-h$ and the negative residual is $-\beta d-h$. Expanding:

\[ \ell_{\text{NFT}}=r\lVert\beta d-h\rVert^2+(1-r)\lVert-\beta d-h\rVert^2=\beta^2\lVert d\rVert^2-2\beta A\langle d,h\rangle+\lVert h\rVert^2=\beta^2\Bigl\lVert d-\tfrac{A}{\beta}h\Bigr\rVert^2+(1-A^2)\lVert h\rVert^2 . \]

The cross terms $\pm2\beta\langle d,h\rangle$ carry weights $r$ and $1-r$, so they combine into $-2\beta(2r-1)\langle d,h\rangle$. Completing the square gives the last form. Since $v_{\text{old}}+(A/\beta)h=v_{\text{tgt}}$, the first term is $\beta^2\lVert v_\theta-v_{\text{tgt}}\rVert^2$, and $(1-A^2)\lVert h\rVert^2=4r(1-r)\lVert h\rVert^2$ does not depend on $\theta$. Differentiating in $v_\theta$ gives $-\nabla_{v_\theta}\ell=2\beta A h-2\beta^2 d$. At $d=0$ the per-sample descent direction is $2\beta(2r-1)(u_t-v_{\text{old}})$. High- and low-reward samples drive the same residual in opposite directions.

(ii) Condition on the state. Now fix $s_t$ and average over all $(x_0,\epsilon)$ compatible with it under $Q_{\text{old}}$. Since $h=u_t-v^{\text{old}}_{\text{rvs}}(s_t)$, and $v^{\text{old}}_{\text{rvs}}(s_t)$ is a constant once $s_t$ is fixed:

\[ \mathcal F_{\text{NFT}}(s_t)=\mathbb E[(2r-1)h\mid s_t]=2\operatorname{Cov}(r,h\mid s_t)+\bigl(2\,\mathbb E[r\mid s_t]-1\bigr)\,\mathbb E[h\mid s_t]. \]

This is the identity $\mathbb E[XY]=\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)+\mathbb E[X]\mathbb E[Y]$ with $X=2r-1$ and $Y=h$. Subtracting a constant does not change a covariance, so $\operatorname{Cov}(r,h\mid s_t)=\operatorname{Cov}(r,u_t\mid s_t)=g$. The mean residual is $\mathbb E[h\mid s_t]=v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}}-v^{\text{old}}_{\text{rvs}}=e$, and $\mathbb E[r\mid s_t]=q$. Substituting gives $2g+(2q-1)e$. ∎

Each branch alone, and why both are needed

Run the same computation with only the positive term $r\lVert\beta d-h\rVert^2$ and only the negative term $(1-r)\lVert-\beta d-h\rVert^2$. At $d=0$, the normalized forces are $\mathcal F_+=\mathbb E[r\,h\mid s_t]$ and $\mathcal F_-=-\mathbb E[(1-r)\,h\mid s_t]$ (Exercise 4.4 does the algebra), which work out to

\[ \mathcal F_+=g+q\,e=q\,\bigl(v^+_{\text{fwd}}-v^{\text{old}}_{\text{rvs}}\bigr),\qquad \mathcal F_-=g-(1-q)\,e=(1-q)\,\bigl(v^{\text{old}}_{\text{rvs}}-v^-_{\text{fwd}}\bigr), \]

and $\mathcal F_++\mathcal F_-=\mathcal F_{\text{NFT}}$. The second form of each follows from Lemma 1: $q\,v^+_{\text{fwd}}=q\,v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}}+g$. The geometry is now clear. The positive branch pulls the sampler's velocity toward $v^+_{\text{fwd}}$, and so toward $v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}}$ with weight $q$. The negative branch pushes it away from $v^-_{\text{fwd}}$, and so away from $v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}}$ with weight $1-q$. Along $g$ the two reinforce each other. Along $e$ they oppose each other. A single branch therefore carries an uncancelled $qe$ or $-(1-q)e$, a force that has nothing to do with reward.

Why joint training is not enough

Joint training leaves the residual $(2q-1)e$. It vanishes at a state only if $q=1/2$ at that state. Prompt-level centering guarantees something weaker: without clipping, $\sum_i(2r_i-1)=0$ across the $K$ samples. That balances rewards across samples. It does not balance them per state, and it does not imply $\sum_i(2r_i-1)e_i\approx0$, where $e_i$ is the one-sample Monte Carlo estimate of $e$. The time dependence is the key:

  • At $t=1$ the state is pure noise and reveals nothing about $x_0$, so $q=\mathbb E[r]=1/2$ (without clipping) and the residual vanishes.
  • As $t\to0$ the state reveals more and more of the endpoint. $q$ moves toward 0 or 1, and the weight $\lvert 2q-1\rvert$ on the gap grows.

States with $q>1/2$ are systematically pulled toward $v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}}$; states with $q<1/2$ are pushed away. The residual dilutes the reward-improving guidance in the aggregate gradient, and something else must hold training together. That something is the EMA.

Worked example. The toy later in this chapter computes $q$, $g$ and $e$ exactly (with a kernel posterior) for a 2D flow model. At initialization it measures a mean $\lvert2q-1\rvert$ of 0.18 for $t\ge0.7$ and 0.75 for $t\le0.4$, which matches the time dependence above. The RMS of $2g$ is 0.934 and the RMS of $(2q-1)e$ is 0.172, so at the start the reward term dominates. During on-policy NFT training with $K=2$, the residual grows to 1.250 while $2g$ is 0.602 (iteration 100), then to 29.3 against 0.825 (iteration 150). The gradient norm reaches $1.17\times10^3$ at that point, and the run later diverges.

Key idea

NFT's target is built from forward-noised endpoints, so it implicitly assumes $v_{\text{rvs}}=v_{\text{fwd}}$. The gap $e$ enters the force weighted by the statewise reward imbalance $2q-1$. Joint training cancels it only on average across samples. The paper's design principle is to remove the force statewise rather than rely on cross-sample cancellation (Insight 1).

§3.2 Off-policy EMA: stability through restoration

Even with joint training, pure on-policy NFT collapses after a sharp rise in its pre-clipping gradient norm. In the paper's Figure 3(a), on OCR, the one-update run reaches OCR 0.9 at step 21, then collapses, and its gradient norm (dashed) spikes as the reward falls. DiffusionNFT uses an EMA rollout policy, $\theta_{\text{old}}\leftarrow\eta\,\theta_{\text{old}}+(1-\eta)\theta$. In this section, and only here, $\eta$ is the EMA decay, not the tilt strength. The EMA could stabilize training through two different mechanisms:

  1. a slower-moving rollout distribution (off-policy data), or
  2. the lagged reference $v_{\text{old}}$ that stays inside $v_{\text{tgt}}$ in the loss.

Proposition 2: the loss splits into guidance plus restoration

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{NFT}}=\mathbb E_{\mathcal D_{\text{old}}}\Bigl[-2\beta(2r-1)\langle d,h\rangle+\beta^2\lVert d\rVert^2\Bigr]+\text{const},\qquad d=v_\theta-v_{\text{old}},\ h=u_t-v_{\text{old}}. \]

Proof. It is line two of the expansion in (i) above, $\beta^2\lVert d\rVert^2-2\beta(2r-1)\langle d,h\rangle+\lVert h\rVert^2$. Take the expectation and absorb $\lVert h\rVert^2$, which does not depend on $\theta$, into the constant. ∎

The linear term carries the reward guidance and the consistency force (its on-policy gradient is exactly $\mathcal F_{\text{NFT}}$). The quadratic term $\beta^2\lVert d\rVert^2$ pulls $v_\theta$ back toward $v_{\text{old}}$. Its gradient, $2\beta^2 d$, is zero at $d=0$. Pure on-policy NFT therefore has no restoration at all, and the residual consistency force acts unopposed. An EMA keeps $\theta_{\text{old}}$ lagging behind $\theta$, so $d\ne0$ and restoration is active.

From LLM RL

This is the role PPO's ratio clipping and KL penalty play, seen from a different side. A quadratic penalty toward the behavior policy (here $\beta^2\lVert v_\theta-v_{\text{old}}\rVert^2$) is the Gaussian analogue of a KL to the rollout policy. The difference is that NFT gets the penalty only when the rollout policy lags. With fully on-policy updates and one gradient step, the penalty is exactly zero, much as the PPO clip never activates on the first minibatch of an epoch.

Two ablations that separate the mechanisms

  • EMA rollouts, no anchor. Keep EMA-policy rollouts but replace $v_{\text{old}}$ by $v_\theta$ (stop-gradient) inside $v_{\text{tgt}}$. Off-policy data remains, but restoration is gone. In Figure 3(b), on GenEval, this run tracks NFT (EMA) for about 70 steps and then collapses to zero. Off-policy data alone does not stabilize NFT.
  • No EMA, two updates. Split each rollout batch into two disjoint minibatches and update once on each with $\beta=1$. The first update starts at $v^{(0)}_\theta=v_{\text{old}}$, where restoration is zero. The second starts from $v^{(1)}_\theta$ and therefore gets restoration toward $v^{(0)}_\theta$. In Figure 3(a), this run avoids collapse over the observed window but reaches OCR 0.9 only at step 320 instead of 21: 15.2× slower.

Restoration is the stabilizer. In NFT, though, it is expensive.

Why a small restoration can dominate the parameter gradient

Figure 3(c) measures both terms at the second update, averaged over rollouts 0–17. Restoration has a 9.4× smaller output-force RMS than guidance, yet a 4.9× larger aggregated parameter-gradient norm. The paper's explanation is that "heterogeneous per-sample guidance largely cancels across states, while restoration survives aggregation", and it defers a detailed analysis. Here is one way to see why this happens. It is my gloss, not the paper's analysis. The parameter gradient is a sum over states of Jacobian-transposed output forces, $\nabla_\theta\mathcal L=-\sum_s J_s^\top f_s$ with $J_s=\partial v_\theta(s)/\partial\theta$.

  • Guidance per state is $f_s=(2r-1)h$. The sign of $2r-1$ varies across samples, and $h=u_t-v_{\text{old}}$ contains the sample noise $\epsilon$. The terms $J_s^\top f_s$ point in many directions, and their sum grows roughly like the square root of the number of states.
  • Restoration per state is $f_s=-\beta^2 d_s$. The drift was itself produced by a parameter change $\Delta\theta$, so $d_s\approx J_s\Delta\theta$ and $J_s^\top d_s\approx J_s^\top J_s\,\Delta\theta$. Every term is a positive semidefinite matrix applied to the same $\Delta\theta$, so the terms add coherently and their sum grows linearly in the number of states.

A small but coherent output force can therefore beat a large but incoherent one after aggregation, which is consistent with Figure 3(c). The design consequence is the one the paper draws. Guidance must also be coherent across states, or restoration will swamp it.

Why EMA is not enough

The EMA controls restoration indirectly, through the lag. Too little lag gives too little restoration and risks collapse; too much slows reward improvement. The paper cites DiffusionNFT's own results. Fully on-policy updates collapse, nearly offline updates converge impractically slowly, and the default schedule $\eta=\min(0.001i,0.5)$ is unstable on OCR, which needs a maximum retention of 0.999. In the paper's FLUX experiments, transferring NFT's GenEval-tuned EMA and $\beta$ makes training unstable (Figure 6(a)). Per-task tuning limits scalability.

Key idea

Restoration stabilizes NFT-style training. EMA requires task-specific tuning, and two updates per rollout avoid the tuning but slow reward improvement about 15×, because NFT's incoherent guidance cancels while restoration does not. A better design adapts restoration to each state's drift and keeps guidance coherent enough to stay dominant (Insight 2).

§4.1 Aggregation-first guidance

Motivation

The consistency force enters because NFT's target is built from $u_t$, whose conditional mean is the forward velocity $v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}}$, and that differs from the reverse field by $e$. The fix is to build a target that never mentions $u_t$. DiffusionART aggregates each prompt's endpoints and rewards into one direction first, then assigns that direction to the reverse-process states the sampler actually visited.

The reward-tilted endpoint distribution

At each iteration, tilt the current rollout distribution (not a fixed pretrained one) exponentially:

\[ p_\tau(x_0\mid c)\ \propto\ p_{\text{old}}(x_0\mid c)\,\exp\bigl(\tau R(x_0,c)\bigr),\qquad \tau\ge0 . \]

The paper writes the tilt strength as $\tau$, because $\eta$ already names the EMA decay. The repo README writes the same formula with $\eta$. Setting $\tau=0$ recovers $p_{\text{old}}$. Larger $\tau$ prefers higher-reward endpoints. This is the familiar KL-regularized RL optimum, $\arg\max_p\ \mathbb E_p[R]-\tfrac1\tau\mathrm{KL}(p\Vert p_{\text{old}})$, applied one iteration at a time.

Lemma 2: the first-order tilt is a covariance

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}\mathbb E_{p_\tau}[x_0\mid c]\Big|_{\tau=0}=\operatorname{Cov}_{p_{\text{old}}}(R,x_0\mid c). \]

Proof (Appendix B, "Covariance as the First-Order Reward Tilt"). Suppress $c$. Let $M(\tau)=\mathbb E_{p_{\text{old}}}[x_0e^{\tau R}]$ and $Z(\tau)=\mathbb E_{p_{\text{old}}}[e^{\tau R}]$. The tilted density is $p_{\text{old}}e^{\tau R}/Z(\tau)$, so the tilted mean is $M(\tau)/Z(\tau)$. Differentiating under the expectation gives $M'(\tau)=\mathbb E[x_0Re^{\tau R}]$ and $Z'(\tau)=\mathbb E[Re^{\tau R}]$. At $\tau=0$, $Z(0)=1$, $M(0)=\mathbb E[x_0]$, $M'(0)=\mathbb E[x_0R]$ and $Z'(0)=\mathbb E[R]$. The quotient rule gives

\[ \Bigl(\tfrac{M}{Z}\Bigr)'(0)=\frac{M'(0)Z(0)-M(0)Z'(0)}{Z(0)^2}=\mathbb E[x_0R]-\mathbb E[x_0]\,\mathbb E[R]=\operatorname{Cov}(R,x_0). \]

Differentiation under the expectation is valid if, for example, $\mathbb E[(1+\lVert x_0\rVert)(1+\lvert R\rvert)e^{a\lvert R\rvert}]<\infty$ for some $a>0$. ∎

For $p_{\text{old}}=\mathcal N(\mu,s^2)$ and $R(x)=x$, the tilted law is $\mathcal N(\mu+\tau s^2,s^2)$, so the derivative is $s^2=\operatorname{Var}(x)=\operatorname{Cov}(R,x)$.

From endpoints to every state, under rectified flow

Under rectified flow, $u_t=x_0-\epsilon$ with $\epsilon$ independent of $x_0$, so $\operatorname{Cov}_{Q_{\text{old}}}(R,u_t\mid c)=\operatorname{Cov}(R,x_0\mid c)-\operatorname{Cov}(R,\epsilon\mid c)=\operatorname{Cov}_{p_{\text{old}}}(R,x_0\mid c)$ at every noise level. The same vector is therefore the first-order change in the average velocity target at every $t$. It is also the prompt-level counterpart of the ideal NFT guidance $g$ from Lemma 1. At $t=1$ the state carries no endpoint information, so conditioning on $s_t$ does nothing. Without clipping, $r=\tfrac12+\tfrac{R-\bar R_c}{2Z_c}$ is affine in $R$, so $g=\operatorname{Cov}(r,u_t)=\tfrac{1}{2Z_c}\operatorname{Cov}(R,x_0)$, a positive multiple of the same direction.

The estimator and its normalization

Each prompt has $K$ endpoints and rewards. The paper estimates and normalizes:

\[ \boxed{\ \overline{\operatorname{Cov}}(R,x_0\mid c)=\frac1K\sum_{i=1}^K(R_i-\bar R_c)\bigl(x_0^{(i)}-\bar x_{0,c}\bigr),\qquad G(c)=\frac{\overline{\operatorname{Cov}}}{\operatorname{RMS}(\overline{\operatorname{Cov}})}\ } \]

$\bar x_{0,c}$ is the group mean endpoint, and $\operatorname{RMS}(z)=\sqrt{D^{-1}\sum_j z_j^2}$ over the $D$ coordinates of the latent. Normalization fixes the guidance magnitude at unit RMS across prompts and reward models. This removes differences in reward scale and spread without a guidance-strength coefficient. A reward multiplied by 100, or a prompt with a tiny reward spread, produces the same $\lVert G\rVert$. Only the direction carries information.

Worked example: $G(c)$ for $K=4$ in 2D

Take endpoints $x^{(1..4)}=(0,0),(2,0),(0,2),(2,2)$ with rewards $R=1,3,2,6$.

  • Means: $\bar R=3$, $\bar x=(1,1)$. Centered rewards: $-2,0,-1,3$. Centered endpoints: $(-1,-1),(1,-1),(-1,1),(1,1)$.
  • Products: $(2,2)$, $(0,0)$, $(1,-1)$ and $(3,3)$, which sum to $(6,4)$. Then $\overline{\operatorname{Cov}}=(6,4)/4=(1.5,1.0)$.
  • $\operatorname{RMS}=\sqrt{(1.5^2+1.0^2)/2}=\sqrt{1.625}=1.2748$, so $G=(1.1767,0.7845)$. Its RMS is 1 and its Euclidean norm is $\sqrt2$.
  • First-order tilted mean at $\tau=0.05$: $\bar x+\tau\,\overline{\operatorname{Cov}}=(1.075,1.050)$. Exact self-normalized tilted mean: $(1.0761,1.0518)$. At $\tau=0.2$, first order gives $(1.30,1.20)$ and exact gives $(1.3092,1.2251)$. The agreement degrades as $\tau$ grows, as a first-order approximation should.

Now apply it. A visited state of this prompt with cached velocity $v^{(0)}=(0.3,-0.5)$ gets the frozen target $v^{(0)}+G=(1.4767,0.2845)$. At the start of the update the loss is $\tfrac wD\lVert G\rVert^2=w$ (unit RMS means $\lVert G\rVert^2=D$), and the output-space descent direction is $\tfrac{2w}{D}G=w\,G$.

The guided target and why the consistency force disappears

For every state $s_t$ visited by the ODE rollout of prompt $c$, freeze

\[ v^{\text{ART}}_{\text{tgt}}(s_t)=v^{(0)}_\theta(s_t)+G(c), \]

where $v^{(0)}_\theta=v_{\text{old}}$ is the prediction cached during the rollout. No extra forward pass is needed: the sampler had to compute it anyway. The loss is $w(s_t)\operatorname{MSE}(v_\theta(s_t),v^{\text{ART}}_{\text{tgt}}(s_t))$. At $v_\theta=v^{(0)}_\theta$ its negative gradient is $\tfrac{2w}{D}G(c)$. Compare this with Proposition 1. NFT's force has the form $\mathbb E[(2r-1)(u_t-v_{\text{rvs}})\mid s_t]$, and the $u_t$ inside it is what produces $e$. ART's target contains $v^{(0)}_\theta$ (the reverse field itself) and $G$ (a constant), and nothing else. There is no $u_t$, so there is no $\mathbb E[u_t\mid s_t]$, so there is no $e$. The force is removed by construction, not by cancellation.

Two more things change relative to NFT:

  • States. ART trains on the states the sampler visited, as reverse-process RL does, not on re-noised endpoints. It inherits Flow-GRPO's on-trajectory supervision without needing a likelihood.
  • Credit assignment. Each state receives an explicit vector, not a scalar advantage passed through a likelihood ratio along that step's own sampled noise.

The CFG analogy

Classifier-free guidance (Step 1) samples with $v=v(x_t,t,\varnothing)+w\,[v(x_t,t,c)-v(x_t,t,\varnothing)]$: it adds a direction to the velocity at sampling time. Distilling CFG means training a single model to output the guided velocity directly. The README describes DiffusionART the same way. The reward-improving direction plays the role of the guidance term, and the model learns to internalize it. The analogy is useful but loose. CFG's direction is statewise, a function of $x_t$ and $t$. $G(c)$ is a single vector shared by all states and all times of a prompt.

What a shared direction gives up

The exact statewise tilt direction at $s_t$ would be $\operatorname{Cov}(R,u_t\mid s_t)$, which varies with the state. Estimating it needs posterior samples of $x_0$ given $s_t$, which is expensive. $G(c)$ uses the $t=1$ (unconditional) value everywhere. The paper says plainly that "a prompt-level direction need not match the posterior guidance at every state" (Appendix B). That mismatch is why the second update and its statewise restoration exist (next section).

Pitfall

Unit-RMS normalization means $G(c)$ never shrinks. A prompt whose $K$ rewards differ only by reward-model noise still receives a full-size push, in a random direction. The repo handles the exactly-constant case explicitly (zero_signal_mode in src/visual_rl/algorithms/covariance.py), but near-constant rewards still yield unit guidance. Nothing in the objective says "stop": convergence relies on the push directions averaging out and on restoration.

§4.2 Two-update restoration

Instead of tuning an EMA, DiffusionART splits each rollout batch into two disjoint minibatches and takes one optimizer step on each. Let $v^{(0)}_\theta$ and $v^{(1)}_\theta$ be the fields before and after the first update. Both updates regress toward the same frozen target $v^{\text{ART}}_{\text{tgt}}=v^{(0)}_\theta+G(c)$. Per state, $\mathcal L_\theta(s_t)=w(s_t)\operatorname{MSE}(v_\theta(s_t),v^{\text{ART}}_{\text{tgt}}(s_t))$ with $\operatorname{MSE}(a,b)=D^{-1}\lVert a-b\rVert^2$, where $w$ combines timestep weighting and the mask. Let $d(s_t)=v_\theta(s_t)-v^{(0)}_\theta(s_t)$. Before each update:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{update 1:}\quad &\mathcal L^{(0)}=\tfrac wD\lVert G(c)\rVert^2, && -\nabla_{v_\theta}\mathcal L\big|_{v^{(0)}_\theta}=\tfrac{2w}{D}\,G(c),\\ \text{update 2:}\quad &\mathcal L^{(1)}=\tfrac wD\lVert G(c)-d(s_t)\rVert^2, && -\nabla_{v_\theta}\mathcal L\big|_{v^{(1)}_\theta}=\tfrac{2w}{D}\bigl(\underbrace{G(c)}_{\text{guidance}}\ \underbrace{-\,d(s_t)}_{\text{restoration}}\bigr). \end{aligned} \]

The derivation is one line: $v^{(1)}_\theta-v^{\text{ART}}_{\text{tgt}}=(v^{(0)}_\theta+d)-(v^{(0)}_\theta+G)=d-G$. The second update keeps the original guidance and adds a pull back toward the rollout prediction whose strength is exactly the drift. Note that the drift $d(s_t)$ on the second minibatch was caused by an update computed on the first minibatch. These states were not in that batch, so their displacement is collateral movement from parameter sharing. This is the generalization behaviour that restoration controls.

Why this is better than NFT's two updates

NFT with two updates also gets restoration, and §3.2 showed it costs 15.2× in speed. The difference lies in the guidance. NFT's per-state guidance $(2r-1)h$ varies in sign and is noisy, so it cancels in parameter space and restoration takes over. ART's guidance is the same vector for every state of a prompt, so it aggregates coherently, just as restoration does. Guidance therefore remains dominant while restoration acts as a statewise brake that is weak where drift is small and strong where drift is large. There is no lag schedule and no $\beta$.

The paper's ablation (Figure 6(b), OCR on SD3.5-M) compares three runs. ART: 1 update rises to about 0.95 within a few dozen steps, then deteriorates steadily, ending near 0.27 by step 800. ART: 2 updates reaches the same level and stays near 0.97 throughout. NFT: 2 updates with a carefully tuned $\beta=0.1$ learns, drops to near zero around step 270 and recovers. In the plotted curve it then falls to zero again around step 440 and stays there through step 800. The text describes only the first, brief collapse. Appendix B explains the single-update deterioration: a prompt-level direction need not match the posterior guidance at each state, and without statewise correction the deviations accumulate.

Pitfall

The two updates must be on disjoint minibatches of the same rollout, with guidance computed from the complete group of $K$ trajectories before splitting. The README recommends splitting within each prompt, so that both halves keep prompt coverage: for example, two batches of 32 prompts with $K/2$ trajectories each, not 16 prompts with $K$ each. It also warns that shrinking the update window to save memory creates more optimizer steps per rollout, which changes the restoration dynamics. Reduce the micro-batch and accumulate instead.

§4.3 Trust-region masking for ODE rollouts

The problem: no KL for a deterministic sampler

Guidance acts in both updates, so one rollout batch can still produce an excessive policy change. PPO would limit this through the likelihood ratio. An ODE step, however, is deterministic, so its transition is a point mass and there is no finite KL between the old and new step. DiffusionART borrows Flow-GRPO's ODE-to-SDE construction only as a measuring device. Rollouts stay ODE trajectories; the SDE defines reference Gaussian transitions for the current and old policies:

\[ q_\theta(\cdot\mid s_t)=\mathcal N\bigl(\mu(s_t,v_\theta(s_t)),\sigma^2_{\text{ref}}(t)I\bigr),\qquad q_{\text{old}}(\cdot\mid s_t)=\mathcal N\bigl(\mu(s_t,v^{(0)}_\theta(s_t)),\sigma^2_{\text{ref}}(t)I\bigr). \]

With reverse step $h=t-t_{\text{next}}>0$ and $0<t<1$ (Appendix C, "Reference Transitions"):

\[ \mu(s_t,v)=x_t+h\Bigl[\Bigl(1+\tfrac{\nu^2}{2}\Bigr)v-\tfrac{\nu^2}{2(1-t)}x_t\Bigr],\qquad \sigma^2_{\text{ref}}(t)=\nu^2\,\tfrac{t}{1-t}\,h, \]

with $\nu=0.8$. At $t=1$ the paper uses $\mu=x_t+hv$ and $\sigma^2_{\text{ref}}(1)=\nu^2$. The means differ only through $v$, so $\mu(s_t,v_\theta)-\mu(s_t,v^{(0)}_\theta)=h(1+\nu^2/2)\,d(s_t)$. The KL between two isotropic Gaussians with equal variance is $\lVert\Delta\mu\rVert^2/(2\sigma^2)$, and the per-coordinate KL proxy is

\[ m(s_t)=\frac1D D_{\text{KL}}\bigl(q_\theta\,\Vert\,q_{\text{old}}\bigr)=\frac{\lVert\mu(s_t,v_\theta)-\mu(s_t,v^{(0)}_\theta)\rVert^2}{2D\,\sigma^2_{\text{ref}}(t)}=\frac{h^2(1+\nu^2/2)^2\,\lVert d(s_t)\rVert^2}{2D\,\sigma^2_{\text{ref}}(t)}. \]

The mask: block large outward moves, keep inward corrections

\[ w(s_t)=t\Bigl[1-\mathbf 1\{m(s_t)\ge\delta\}\;\mathbf 1\{\langle d(s_t),\,G(c)-d(s_t)\rangle>0\}\Bigr],\qquad\delta=10^{-5}. \]
  • The first indicator detects excessive drift.
  • The second detects an outward update: the second update's descent direction $G-d$ has a positive component along the existing drift $d$, so taking the step would move the state further from the rollout policy.
  • A state is dropped only if both hold. A large drift whose update points back inward, $\langle d,G-d\rangle\le0$, is kept, because that update is restoration. The rule is inspired by Flow-DPPO.
  • The factor $t$ up-weights high-noise states. It is the velocity_linear_sigma loss weighting in src/visual_rl/algorithms/art.py.

The mask is recomputed before each update and treated as a constant in backprop. At the first update $d=0$, so $m=0<\delta$ and nothing is masked: in effect the mask constrains only the second update.

From LLM RL

This is PPO's clip in different clothes. PPO zeroes the gradient of a token when the ratio is already outside $[1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon]$ in the direction the advantage pushes, and keeps it when the gradient would move the ratio back inside. Here the "ratio" is the KL proxy $m$, and "the direction the advantage pushes" is the sign of $\langle d,G-d\rangle$. The main difference is that PPO's trust region is per token with a likelihood the sampler really has, whereas this one is per state with a likelihood borrowed from a reference SDE.

Worked example

Use the $G=(1.1767,0.7845)$ from the earlier example, a 10-step schedule ($h=0.1$), $t=0.5$, $D=2$, $\nu=0.8$. Then $\sigma^2_{\text{ref}}=0.64\cdot1\cdot0.1=0.064$ and $h(1+\nu^2/2)=0.132$.

  • $d=(0.4,0.1)$: $m=0.132^2\cdot0.17/(2\cdot2\cdot0.064)=0.0116\ge\delta$. $G-d=(0.777,0.685)$ and $\langle d,G-d\rangle=0.379>0$, so the update is outward. Masked ($w=0$).
  • $d=(1.5,1.0)$ (overshoot): $m=0.221\ge\delta$, but $\langle d,G-d\rangle=-0.700<0$, so the update is inward. Kept, with $w=t=0.5$: the second update pulls this state back.
  • $d=(0.01,0)$: $m=6.8\times10^{-6}<\delta$. Kept.

Solving $m=\delta$ for this $t$ and $h$ gives a threshold of $\operatorname{RMS}(d)\approx0.0086$ in velocity units, against velocities of order 1. In practice, any state with noticeable drift and an outward-pointing update is masked. $\delta$ acts nearly as a switch on the sign condition, and the threshold scales with $h$ and $t$.

Evidence for the mask

  • Video (HunyuanVideo-13B, VQ:MQ 1:1). The no-mask run drops around step 200 and then recovers; the masked run improves steadily. In the plotted curves the masked run reaches VQ near 15 by about step 300, while the unmasked run sits near 10–12.5 (Figure 8).
  • Diagnostics. Over the shared first 300 steps, the maximum first-update gradient norm is 1.96 with masking and 3.33 without. The velocity drift measured before second updates shows much larger spikes without the mask. Masking the second update therefore also keeps later first updates well behaved.
  • Images. On SD3.5-M (three tasks) and FLUX.1-dev, training stays effective without the mask and can improve slightly faster. The FLUX no-mask run reaches a training GenEval of 0.971 by step 180. The mask is a stability safeguard that matters more for video.

§4.4 Pairwise reward models

Visual quality has no verifier, so preferences between pairs are a natural reward signal. DiffusionART-Pairwise splits each prompt's $K$ endpoints into $M=K/2$ disjoint pairs. It queries a pairwise judge and assigns labels $y_i=1$ to winners and $y_i=0$ to losers, or $y_i=\tfrac12$ to both for a tie. The labels replace $R_i$ in the otherwise unchanged covariance. Everything downstream (normalization, targets, two updates, mask) is identical.

The pairwise covariance identity (Appendix C)

Every pair's labels average to $\tfrac12$ (including ties), so the global label mean is $\tfrac12$. Because $\sum_i(y_i-\tfrac12)=0$, subtracting $\bar x_{0,c}$ changes nothing:

\[ \overline{\operatorname{Cov}}=\frac1K\sum_{i=1}^K\bigl(y_i-\tfrac12\bigr)\bigl(x_0^{(i)}-\bar x_{0,c}\bigr)=\frac1K\sum_{i=1}^K\bigl(y_i-\tfrac12\bigr)x_0^{(i)}=\frac{1}{2K}\sum_{j\ \text{non-tied}}\bigl(x^{\text{win}}_{0,j}-x^{\text{lose}}_{0,j}\bigr). \]

A winner contributes $+\tfrac12x^{\text{win}}$, a loser $-\tfrac12x^{\text{lose}}$, and a tie contributes zero. A single pair's own covariance, with denominator 2, is $\tfrac14(x^{\text{win}}-x^{\text{lose}})$, and averaging $M$ of these gives the same expression with coefficient $\tfrac{1}{4M}=\tfrac{1}{2K}$. The coefficient disappears after RMS normalization. For $K=2$:

\[ G(c)=\frac{x^{\text{win}}_0-x^{\text{lose}}_0}{\operatorname{RMS}(x^{\text{win}}_0-x^{\text{lose}}_0)}, \]

a unit-RMS vector from the worse sample to the better one, and zero for a tie.

One consequence the paper does not state: for $K=2$ with scalar rewards, $\overline{\operatorname{Cov}}=\tfrac14(R_1-R_2)(x_1-x_2)$, which after normalization is the same vector. At $K=2$, scalar DiffusionART and DiffusionART-Pairwise produce identical guidance whenever the judge agrees with the reward's ordering. The widget shows a 0.0° angle between them at $K=2$.

The full algorithm

The paper's Algorithm 1, reproduced exactly. Equation 5 is the boxed covariance estimator in §4.1 above.

Algorithm 1: DiffusionART, one training iteration.
Rollout stage
1Sample with an ODE solver using $v_\theta^{(0)}=v_\theta$.
For each prompt $c$, collect $K$ endpoints with rewards $R$, states $s_t$, and predictions $v_\theta^{(0)}(s_t)$.
2Compute $G(c)$ by Equation 5 for each prompt $c$.
3Freeze $v_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathrm{ART}}(s_t)=v_\theta^{(0)}(s_t)+G(c)$ for each state $s_t$ of prompt $c$.
Training stage
4Split the samples into two equal-sized disjoint minibatches $B_1,B_2$.
5For each $B\in\{B_1,B_2\}$, recompute $w(s_t)$ and update $\theta$ with one step on
$\mathcal L(B;\theta)=\dfrac{1}{|B|}\sum_{s_t\in B}w(s_t)\operatorname{MSE}\bigl(v_\theta(s_t),v_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathrm{ART}}(s_t)\bigr)$.

Line by line:

  1. Line 1. Roll out with the deterministic ODE sampler of the current policy, so $v^{(0)}_\theta=v_\theta$: there is no separate old or EMA copy. Store, for each visited state, the latent $x_t$, the time $t$, the step size and the velocity the sampler used. That cached velocity is $v^{(0)}_\theta(s_t)$, and reusing it saves the extra old-policy forward pass on re-noised states that NFT needs. Rewards are black-box calls on the endpoints. Unlike Flow-GRPO, the sampler is not converted to an SDE, so samples come from the model's actual inference sampler.
  2. Line 2. One reduction per prompt over its complete group of $K$: center rewards and endpoints, average their products, divide by RMS. With several reward models, combine their weighted contributions before normalizing (README); normalizing each separately changes their relative influence.
  3. Line 3. Every state on every one of the prompt's $K$ trajectories gets the same offset $G(c)$ added to its cached velocity. Targets are frozen tensors.
  4. Line 4. Two equal, disjoint halves, preferably split within each prompt.
  5. Line 5. Before each update, run the current model on the half's states to get $v_\theta$ and compute $d=v_\theta-v^{(0)}_\theta$, the KL proxy $m$ and the outward test. Then set $w=t$ or 0 and take one optimizer step on the weighted MSE. For $B_1$, $d=0$ and $w=t$ everywhere. For $B_2$, the first step has moved the model, so the mask and the restoration term are live.

Algorithm 2 (Pairwise) is identical except that line 2 becomes three lines: form $M=K/2$ disjoint pairs and query the pairwise reward model; assign $y_i\in\{1,0,\tfrac12\}$; compute the covariance with $y_i$ in place of $R_i$.

The same iteration as algorithm-level pseudocode, in the paper's sign convention. The repo's loss (src/visual_rl/algorithms/art.py) uses the SD3/diffusers convention, in which the network predicts $\epsilon-x_0=-v$. There the target is written old_velocity - displacement, which is $v^{(0)}+G$ after flipping signs. Step 5 walks through that code.

  • ART does not care about $e$. ART's $\lvert e\rvert$ grows to about 5, far larger than NFT's. ART's sampler becomes very inconsistent with flow matching on its own outputs, yet it optimizes the reward cleanly, because its update never references $v_{\text{fwd}}$. The corollary is a caveat: ART does not train off-trajectory, forward-noised states at all, so the field away from the sampler's paths is left unconstrained.

Here $p_{\text{old}}$ for each prompt is an 8-mode ring, and improving the reward means moving mass between modes. A single translation $G(c)$ applied to every trajectory moves all eight modes together, which is the wrong shape of update. With $K=2$, each pair's $G$ is the direction from one sample to the other, local to those two trajectories, so the update behaves more like a per-pair correction. This is my reading of the toy, not a claim from the paper. The paper's FLUX experiment found the opposite preference (prompt-global pooling beat pair-local, 0.91 against 0.78), presumably because variance reduction dominates there. Which effect wins depends on how multimodal $p_{\text{old}}(x_0\mid c)$ is and how noisy the rewards are.

Critical reading

Assumptions

  • The theory is at the on-policy point and in expectation. Proposition 1 holds at $v_\theta=v^{\text{old}}_{\text{rvs}}$ with exact conditional expectations. Practical NFT uses one-sample estimates per state, and after the first step of an epoch it is no longer at $d=0$. The decomposition is not measured in the paper's experiments; Figure 2(b) is a schematic. (The toy above measures it in 2D.)
  • Clipping is ignored. Statements such as "$q=1/2$ at $t=1$" and "$g$ is a positive multiple of $\operatorname{Cov}(R,x_0)$" need the unclipped goodness. NFT clips.
  • Rectified flow. The step $\operatorname{Cov}(R,u_t)=\operatorname{Cov}(R,x_0)$ uses $u_t=x_0-\epsilon$ with $\epsilon\perp x_0$. Other schedules give $u_t=-\dot\alpha_tx_0-\dot\sigma_t\epsilon$, whose covariance with $R$ is $-\dot\alpha_t\operatorname{Cov}(R,x_0)$, the same direction with a $t$-dependent scale.
  • First order. $G(c)$ is the derivative of the tilted mean at $\tau=0$. It cannot express a reward that wants more or less spread (try "prefer far from origin" in the widget), and for a multimodal $p_{\text{old}}(x_0\mid c)$ a shared translation is a poor proxy for reweighting modes (the $K=8$ toy).

Limitations the paper acknowledges or implies

  • The prompt-level direction need not match the statewise posterior guidance (Appendix B). Restoration controls the accumulated mismatch but does not remove it.
  • Results depend on reward quality, as for ReFL and other reward-based fine-tuning (README). No KL to a reference model is used, and no diversity or out-of-distribution metric is reported. Reward hacking is plausible, but untested here.
  • Evaluation largely reuses the training reward models (GenEval, VideoReward), so improvements are measured by the same instrument being optimized.

Things to watch for

  • Text versus figure. In Figure 6(b), NFT: 2 updates collapses to zero a second time near step 440 and stays there through step 800. The text describes only a brief collapse and recovery.
  • Seeds and variance. No error bars or seed counts are given. The non-monotone pairwise results ($K=4$ below both $K=2$ and $K=8$) are a hint that single-run differences of this size may be noise.
  • Baseline tuning. On FLUX, NFT's hyperparameters were transferred deliberately to show tuning sensitivity. That is the point being made, but it also means the FLUX comparison is against an untuned NFT.
  • Fixed $\delta$. $\delta=10^{-5}$ is "fixed throughout", but its meaning in drift units scales with $h$ (the number of steps) and $t$. A 4-step I2V schedule and a 16-step T2V schedule enforce different effective radii.

What I would test first

  1. Log $\lvert2q-1\rvert$ and $(2q-1)e$ during a real NFT run, using a cheap estimator (for example, re-noise a batch of endpoints and compare $v_\theta$ with the batch-posterior average of $u_t$ at large $t$). Check whether its growth precedes the gradient-norm spike, as it does in the toy.
  2. Statewise guidance: compute $G$ separately per time bucket, or per noise level with a small posterior estimate, and compare with the shared $G(c)$. If the single-update deterioration disappears, the Appendix B explanation is confirmed.
  3. Diversity under ART: per-prompt endpoint variance, LPIPS diversity or mode coverage, against NFT and Flow-GRPO, since a shared translation per prompt might collapse diversity faster.
  4. Three seeds for the pairwise $K$ sweep, and a true pairwise judge rather than binarized scalar scores.
  5. A $\delta$ sweep across step counts, to see whether the mask is really a sign test in practice.

Pitfall

Do not read "removes the consistency force by construction" as "ART's model stays forward–reverse consistent". It does not: the toy's $\lvert e\rvert$ grows to about 5 under ART. The claim is that ART's update contains no term proportional to $e$. Whether the inconsistency matters downstream (for example, if you later switch samplers or step counts) is untested.

Exercises

Exercise 4.1: Derive the first-order tilt, then the second-order term

Let $p_\tau\propto p_{\text{old}}e^{\tau R}$ and $m(\tau)=\mathbb E_{p_\tau}[x_0]$. Show that $m'(0)=\operatorname{Cov}(R,x_0)$, and find $m'(\tau)$ for general $\tau$.

Solution. Write $m=M/Z$ with $M=\mathbb E_{\text{old}}[x_0e^{\tau R}]$ and $Z=\mathbb E_{\text{old}}[e^{\tau R}]$. Then $m'=M'/Z-MZ'/Z^2$. Note $M'/Z=\mathbb E_{p_\tau}[x_0R]$, $M/Z=\mathbb E_{p_\tau}[x_0]$ and $Z'/Z=\mathbb E_{p_\tau}[R]$. Hence $m'(\tau)=\operatorname{Cov}_{p_\tau}(R,x_0)$ for every $\tau$, not only at 0, and at $\tau=0$ it is $\operatorname{Cov}_{p_{\text{old}}}(R,x_0)$. Differentiating once more gives $m''(\tau)=\mathbb E_{p_\tau}[(R-\bar R_\tau)^2(x_0-m(\tau))]$, a third mixed central moment. If the reward is roughly symmetric around its mean within $p_{\text{old}}$, this term is small, and the first-order direction stays accurate for larger $\tau$. The worked example confirms this: at $\tau=0.05$ first order is within 0.001 of exact; at $\tau=0.2$ the error is about 0.025.

Exercise 4.2: Why the consistency term vanishes for ART, and how to bring it back

(a) Compute the on-policy output-space force of the ART loss at a state, conditioned on $s_t$. (b) Suppose someone "simplifies" ART by training on forward-noised states with target $v_{\text{tgt}}=u_t+G(c)$. Compute the conditional on-policy force and identify the consistency term.

Solution. (a) The ART target $v^{(0)}_\theta(s_t)+G(c)$ is a deterministic function of $s_t$ and $c$. With $\mathcal L=\tfrac wD\lVert v_\theta-v^{(0)}_\theta-G\rVert^2$, $-\nabla_{v_\theta}\mathcal L|_{v_\theta=v^{(0)}_\theta}=\tfrac{2w}{D}G(c)$. Conditioning on $s_t$ changes nothing, because nothing random remains. No $u_t$ appears, so there is no $\mathbb E[u_t\mid s_t]=v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}}$, and no $e$.
(b) Now $-\tfrac{D}{2w}\nabla=\mathbb E[u_t+G-v_{\text{rvs}}^{\text{old}}\mid s_t]=G+\bigl(v^{\text{old}}_{\text{fwd}}-v^{\text{old}}_{\text{rvs}}\bigr)=G+e$. The consistency force returns with weight 1, larger than NFT's $\lvert2q-1\rvert\le1$. The variant also regresses toward $v_{\text{fwd}}$, which is plain flow matching on the model's own samples. The lesson: the force appears exactly when the target's conditional mean involves $\mathbb E[u_t\mid s_t]$.

Exercise 4.3: Compute G(c) by hand for K = 4 in 1D, and for K = 2

(a) Endpoints $x=(-1,0,1,2)$, rewards $R=(0,1,3,2)$. Compute $\overline{\operatorname{Cov}}$, $G$, the first-order tilted mean at $\tau=0.1$ and the exact tilted mean. (b) Show that for $K=2$ the scalar-reward $G$ equals the pairwise $G$.

Solution. (a) $\bar R=1.5$, $\bar x=0.5$. Centered rewards are $(-1.5,-0.5,1.5,0.5)$ and centered endpoints $(-1.5,-0.5,0.5,1.5)$. The products are $2.25,0.25,0.75,0.75$, which sum to 4, so $\overline{\operatorname{Cov}}=1.0$. In 1D, $\operatorname{RMS}(z)=\lvert z\rvert$, so $G=\operatorname{sign}(\overline{\operatorname{Cov}})=+1$: in one dimension, normalization keeps only the sign. First order: $0.5+0.1\cdot1.0=0.6$. Exact: weights $e^{0.1R}=(1,1.10517,1.34986,1.22140)$, sum 4.67643, $\sum w_ix_i=2.79267$, mean $0.59718$.
(b) With $K=2$, $\bar R=(R_1+R_2)/2$, so $R_1-\bar R=(R_1-R_2)/2=-(R_2-\bar R)$, and likewise for $x$. Both terms of $\tfrac12\sum_i(R_i-\bar R)(x_i-\bar x)$ equal $\tfrac{R_1-R_2}{2}\cdot\tfrac{x_1-x_2}{2}$, so $\overline{\operatorname{Cov}}=\tfrac12\cdot2\cdot\tfrac{(R_1-R_2)(x_1-x_2)}{4}=\tfrac14(R_1-R_2)(x_1-x_2)$. After RMS normalization this is $\operatorname{sign}(R_1-R_2)\,(x_1-x_2)/\operatorname{RMS}(x_1-x_2)$, the unit-RMS vector from loser to winner, which is the pairwise $G$. Scalar magnitudes are discarded at $K=2$.

Exercise 4.4: Branch forces and when the residual dominates

(a) Derive $\mathcal F_+=g+qe$ and $\mathcal F_-=g-(1-q)e$ at the on-policy point. (b) At a low-noise state, $q=0.8$, $g$ has norm 0.1 and $e$ has norm 0.5 and is parallel to $g$. Compare the reward-improving and consistency parts of the joint force, and of each branch alone.

Solution. (a) Positive branch: $\ell_+=r\lVert\beta d-h\rVert^2$, so $-\tfrac1{2\beta}\nabla_d\ell_+|_{d=0}=r\,h$. Conditioning gives $\mathbb E[rh\mid s_t]=\operatorname{Cov}(r,h)+\mathbb E[r]\mathbb E[h]=g+qe$. Negative branch: $\ell_-=(1-r)\lVert\beta d+h\rVert^2$, so $-\tfrac1{2\beta}\nabla_d\ell_-|_{d=0}=-(1-r)h$, and $\mathbb E[-(1-r)h\mid s_t]=-\operatorname{Cov}(1-r,h)-(1-q)e=g-(1-q)e$.
(b) Joint: $2\lVert g\rVert=0.2$ against $(2q-1)\lVert e\rVert=0.6\cdot0.5=0.3$, so the consistency term is 1.5× the guidance at this state. Positive alone: $0.1$ against $0.8\cdot0.5=0.4$ (4×). Negative alone: $0.1$ against $0.2\cdot0.5=0.1$ (equal magnitude, opposite sign along $e$). Joint training helps but does not save this state; only states with $q$ near $1/2$, which are high-noise states, are clean.

Exercise 4.5: The trust-region mask by hand

Take $D=2$, $\nu=0.8$, a 10-step schedule ($h=0.1$), $t=0.5$, $G=(1.1767,0.7845)$. For $d=(0.4,0.1)$, $(1.5,1.0)$ and $(0.01,0)$, compute $m(s_t)$, decide whether the state is masked, and find the drift RMS at which $m=\delta=10^{-5}$.

Solution. $\sigma^2_{\text{ref}}=\nu^2\tfrac{t}{1-t}h=0.064$, and $\Delta\mu=h(1+\nu^2/2)d=0.132\,d$, so $m=0.132^2\lVert d\rVert^2/(2\cdot2\cdot0.064)=0.06806\lVert d\rVert^2$.

  • $d=(0.4,0.1)$: $\lVert d\rVert^2=0.17$, so $m=0.01157\ge\delta$. $\langle d,G-d\rangle=0.4\cdot0.7767+0.1\cdot0.6845=0.379>0$. Masked.
  • $d=(1.5,1.0)$: $m=0.2212\ge\delta$, but $\langle d,G-d\rangle=1.5\cdot(-0.3233)+1.0\cdot(-0.2155)=-0.700<0$. Kept, $w=0.5$.
  • $d=(0.01,0)$: $m=6.8\times10^{-6}<\delta$. Kept.

Threshold: $m=0.06806\cdot D\cdot\operatorname{RMS}(d)^2=0.1361\operatorname{RMS}(d)^2=10^{-5}$ gives $\operatorname{RMS}(d)\approx0.0086$. In general, $m=\tfrac{h(1+\nu^2/2)^2(1-t)}{2\nu^2t}\operatorname{RMS}(d)^2$. With more steps (smaller $h$) or larger $t$, the same $\delta$ tolerates more drift.

Takeaways

  • NFT's on-policy force is $2g+(2q-1)e$: reward-improving guidance plus a consistency force from the gap between the sampler's velocity and the ideal forward velocity of its own samples. Joint training cancels it across samples, not per state, and $\lvert2q-1\rvert$ grows as noise falls.
  • NFT's loss is guidance plus $\beta^2\lVert v_\theta-v_{\text{old}}\rVert^2$. On-policy, the restoration term is zero; EMA keeps it alive, and that, not off-policy data, is what stabilizes NFT.
  • DiffusionART aggregates first: $G(c)=\overline{\operatorname{Cov}}(R,x_0\mid c)/\operatorname{RMS}$, the normalized first-order reward tilt of the endpoint mean. It adds $G(c)$ to the cached velocity of every visited state. The target never uses $u_t$, so the consistency force is absent by construction.
  • A second disjoint-minibatch update adds restoration $-d$ proportional to each state's drift, with no EMA schedule. A KL-proxy trust-region mask drops only large outward moves.
  • Pairwise labels plug into the same covariance. At $K=2$, $G$ points from loser to winner and equals the scalar-reward $G$.
  • The large-scale evidence is strong on efficiency: 12.02× and 11.72× fewer GPU hours on T2V (1:1), and 4.8× on I2V. The mechanistic claims are supported by targeted ablations but not by direct measurement of the consistency force. In a multimodal 2D toy, a shared prompt-level direction is a weak instrument at larger $K$.
